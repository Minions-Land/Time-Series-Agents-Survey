\abstract{Time series analysis supports forecasting, diagnosis, and decisions in changing environments, and Time Series Agents carry out such analyses as workflows in which a language model reads the state, calls tools, and interprets their results. Building such an Agent requires deciding which mechanisms can be reused from a general Agent runtime, which need temporal semantics such as information availability, and which must be redesigned around a task. Existing surveys organize the field by model adaptation, reasoning, or task family and leave these decisions implicit at the level of modules. This survey addresses that gap with one decomposition: a Time Series Agent is an LLM, a General Harness, and a Time Series Harness, which consists of a General Time Series Harness shared across tasks and a Task-Specific Harness bound to one task; every module of a reviewed work is assigned to one term by its scope of reuse. The LLM is the caller of the Harness, and each Harness term is described by a vocabulary grounded in prior work: mechanism dimensions, temporal functions of the availability record, and a task grid of output modes on the two sides of the current time, with one evaluation criterion per term. Applied to the literature, the decomposition shows that most works automate an existing analysis, that solutions to time series requirements are rarely tested and mostly stay inside task modules, and that projection and hindsight decision have no evaluated output. It offers a common basis for building Time Series Agents from reusable modules and for evaluating each part against the requirement it serves.}
\keyword{Time Series; Large Language Models; Agent Systems; Harness Engineering}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Time series analysis supports decisions in environments that change as new observations arrive. Demand planning uses forecasts to set inventory, equipment monitoring uses anomalies to schedule maintenance, and traffic control uses current measurements to select actions \citep{lim2021deepts,collmllight2026}. In each setting, a numerical prediction or diagnostic result is one part of a longer workflow: the system must acquire data, inspect temporal context, call analytical tools, validate the result, and pass an admissible output to the next decision. Recent Agent systems make these steps executable through workspaces, evidence checks, and tool feedback \citep{tsagent2025,aion2026}. Research on such Agents is growing quickly, and its emphasis is moving from adapting the model toward engineering the runtime around it: contributions to every part of an Agent grow approximately exponentially, and Harness modules now outnumber changes to the LLM (Figure~\ref{fig:contribution-trend}).

The construction problem is therefore broader than adapting an LLM to a time series. A developer must decide which interaction, memory, planning, and execution mechanisms can be carried over from a general Agent; which operations need temporal semantics such as order, windows, units, and availability time; and which procedures must be redesigned around a task objective. General Harness research supplies reusable runtime mechanisms \citep{ning2026codeharness,lin2026agenticharness,guo2026harnesssurvey}, while temporal workspaces and contextual forecasting protocols constrain the information and numerical state that those mechanisms may use \citep{timeclaw2026explore,drcik2026}. The output contract then changes the required feedback: a forecast is judged at a future horizon, an anomaly workflow must localize and support a suspicious interval, and a decision must connect evidence to an action and its consequences \citep{chen2026tsagentsurvey}.

This survey answers that construction question with one formula: a Time Series Agent is an LLM, a General Harness, and a Time Series Harness, and the Time Series Harness is a General Time Series Harness plus a Task-Specific Harness (Eq.~\eqref{eq:tsa-formula}, Section~\ref{sec:what-is}).
The LLM is the caller: it reads the state, makes function calls, and interprets their results. The General Harness supplies the domain independent runtime that any Agent needs. The Time Series Harness supplies what that runtime does not know about time series. Its modules either hold temporal semantics shared across tasks (General Time Series Harness, GTS) or are bound to the output and objective of one task (Task-Specific Harness, TSK); we abbreviate the General Harness as GH. Statistical models and time series foundation models are tools within the Harness rather than part of the LLM term. The three parts of the title follow the formula: LLMs are its first term, Harness architectures the other two, and evaluation checks each term.

\subsection{Position relative to existing surveys}

Time series surveys supply complementary accounts of predictive architectures, foundation models, LLM adaptation, and multimodal temporal modeling \citep{lim2021deepts,liang2024foundation,zhang2024llmts,jiang2024empowering,jiang2025multimodal}. Our construction question links these bodies of work: which general mechanisms remain reusable, which require temporal semantics, and which become tied to a particular task?

Five recent surveys review Time Series Agents directly. Zhang et al.\ code each paper on four axes, namely representation, model adaptation, workflow autonomy, and analytical task \citep{promptstoagents2026}. Chang et al.\ place each paper at one reasoning topology and one primary objective: traditional analysis, explanation and understanding, causal inference and decision making, or generation \citep{changsurvey2026}. Chen et al.\ group systems by problem family, namely forecasting, synthesis, anomaly diagnosis, and decision support, and compare their architecture, tools, and memory \citep{chen2026tsagentsurvey}. Xu et al.\ review Agents for forecasting and prediction by architecture (standalone, tool augmented, or hybrid) and treat their training, evaluation, and applications \citep{xu2026forecastagentsurvey}. Hu et al.\ trace a progression from foundation models through translators and reasoners to Agents, and organize capabilities into five layers, namely perception, reasoning, planning and action, memory and knowledge, and temporal world models, with reliability across all of them \citep{hu2026agenticts}.

Several choices of this survey have precedents in these works. The objectives of Chang et al.\ already separate decision making and generation from analysis, as our output modes do. Zhang et al.\ code time series foundation models as tools, and Xu et al.\ and Hu et al.\ describe Agents that call forecasting models as tools; we make this caller view the organizing basis. Hu et al.\ separate the closed loop that Time Series Agents share with language Agents from the temporal pressures placed on that loop, which parallels our division between the General Harness and the General Time Series Harness. Xu et al.\ define the information set available before the decision time and treat leakage and contamination in more depth than we do; our availability record extends that set with the event time and the availability time of each value. Chen et al.\ link documented failure modes to design choices and implementation checks.

The difference lies in the question the classification answers. The prior surveys assign a category to a whole paper or system according to how it works or which problem it solves. Our question is constructive: for a new scenario, which parts can be reused from a general Agent, which need temporal semantics shared across tasks, and which must be rebuilt for the task. The survey therefore differs in six respects (Table~\ref{tab:direct-prior-comparison}). First, the unit is a module, assigned by the scope over which it can be reused, so one paper can contribute to several terms. Second, the LLM is the caller of the Harness, and forecasting models are tools within the Harness. Third, temporal validity is the function of a shared Harness term, applied in data management, processing, context, and validation, rather than only a concern of evaluation. Fourth, the task grid crosses output modes with the current time and shows two well defined cells without a task output. Fifth, evaluation assigns one criterion to each term. Sixth, the formula yields a construction order, reference modules for each cell, and an assessment of a new work, and every classification is linked to a source passage. Decomposing language agents into modules has precedent in cognitive architectures \citep{sumers2024coala} and in surveys that separate profile, memory, planning, and action modules \citep{wang2024agentsurvey}; ordering modules by the scope over which they can be reused follows the commonality and variability analysis of software product line engineering \citep{kang1990foda,clements2001spl}. Zhang et al., Chang et al., and Hu et al.\ also maintain public repositories that list each reviewed work with its category \citep{promptstoagents2026,changsurvey2026,hu2026agenticts}.

\subsection{One formula, one assignment rule}

Our principal contribution is to apply Eq.~\eqref{eq:tsa-formula} at the level of modules. The unit of classification is a concrete module of a work, and each module is assigned to one term. A Harness module goes to the most specific of the three Harness terms whose condition it meets, judged from its inputs, state, operations, feedback, and outputs (Table~\ref{tab:layer-rules}). A paper can therefore contribute modules to several terms: a forecasting Agent may reuse a General Harness planner, add a General Time Series Harness availability check, and define a Task-Specific Harness model selection procedure. Existing approaches develop both action selection through tool feedback \citep{tsagent2025} and temporal interpretation through model adaptation \citep{jin2024timellm,chatts2025}; the formula places these contributions in the LLM term and the Harness terms they use.

The formula is the only concept we introduce. Every vocabulary is adopted from prior work or derived from the definition of its term (Table~\ref{tab:term-basis}), and the LLM term needs none, because every LLM-side contribution changes the same caller. The General Harness uses five mechanism dimensions synthesized from Agent and Harness research \citep{sumers2024coala,ning2026codeharness,lin2026agenticharness}. The General Time Series Harness uses four temporal functions derived from the availability record of a temporal state, whose event and availability times are the valid and transaction times of temporal databases \citep{snodgrass1985taxonomy,jensen1999temporal}; the functions are the structures, operators, and integrity rules of a data model \citep{codd1980datamodels}, together with the scope of the record. The Task-Specific Harness uses a task grid with two standard axes: the output mode, given by how an output is checked \citep{hastie2009esl,gneiting2007scoring}, and whether the output refers to the record observed by the current time or to what comes after it \citep{wiener1949extrapolation}; each cell holds standard task names \citep{wu2023timesnet,kong2025timemqa}. The four evaluation criteria check one term each. Each Harness vocabulary is divided once more by a single criterion of the same origin (Tables~\ref{tab:component-vocab}, \ref{tab:gts-vocab}, and \ref{tab:tsk-vocab}), and Table~\ref{tab:cell-lookup} lists reference modules in every layer for each cell of the grid.

The formula also gives a way to assess a new work. Its modules are placed in the terms; each placement is checked against the scope the work claims, since a general mechanism can be presented as task-specific and a task procedure as general; and the work's contribution is read from the map it lands on: automation of an existing analysis, another implementation where many exist, a module where none exists, or the consolidation of a task solution into a shared time series module (Section~\ref{sec:task-impact}).

Applied to the reviewed works, the formula yields four findings. First, most works automate an existing analysis, letting the LLM and the General Harness run it with existing time series tools. Second, solutions to time series requirements stay inside tasks: fewer than half of the works that propose methods test the requirement their modules address, such as information availability or regime change, and most tested solutions are task modules. Third, generality is claimed more often than shown: only about half of the shared modules are applied by their own sources to tasks in at least two cells of the task grid. Fourth, the grid exposes outputs that Agents produce only internally: projections and hindsight decisions appear inside decision procedures and reflection memories but are never evaluated, and few forecasting works report a probabilistic forecast. Section~\ref{sec:task-impact} gives the counts.

Figure~\ref{fig:survey-organization} shows how a Time Series Agent is built, and the survey follows the same construction order. Sections~\ref{sec:intro} and~\ref{sec:what-is} define the problem and the system. Sections~\ref{sec:llm-side}--\ref{sec:task-specific-harness} build it: the LLM side, then Harness modules from general to task-specific. Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} evaluates the result, and Sections~\ref{sec:open-problems} and~\ref{sec:conclusion} derive open problems and conclusions. Each section therefore answers the next design or evaluation question.

\subsection{Contributions}

The paper follows the terms of Eq.~\eqref{eq:tsa-formula}: Section~\ref{sec:what-is} defines the Agent, the assignment rule, and the vocabulary of each term; Section~\ref{sec:llm-side} reviews the LLM term; Sections~\ref{sec:general-harness}--\ref{sec:task-specific-harness} review the General Harness, General Time Series Harness, and Task-Specific Harness terms and what each changes for the tasks; Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} evaluates the terms with infrastructure and benchmarks; and Section~\ref{sec:open-problems} derives open problems. Module assignments, source passages, and official resources are kept in source-linked records (Paper Cards) in a public repository.\footnote{Survey and companion resources: \url{https://github.com/Minions-Land/Time-Series-Agents-Survey}.} Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{One formula applied at module level.} Every module of every reviewed work is assigned to one term of Eq.~\eqref{eq:tsa-formula} by its scope of reuse. The LLM is treated as the caller of the Harness, and each Harness term is described with a vocabulary adopted from prior work or derived from its definition, including a task grid that places every task, classification among them, by output mode and by time.
  \item \textbf{A comparative synthesis by term.} We analyze representative methods through their mechanisms, assumptions, feedback, and evaluation, and compare eleven systems (Table~\ref{tab:representative-systems}), showing which modules carry over to a new scenario and which must be rebuilt, and list reference modules in every layer for each cell of the task grid (Table~\ref{tab:cell-lookup}).
  \item \textbf{An assessment of contribution with source-linked records.} A new work is placed in the terms, its claimed scope is checked against the assignment rule, and its contribution is read from the map, from automation to consolidation into a shared module (Section~\ref{sec:task-impact}). Paper Cards link each module assignment to the passage that supports it and to official implementations (Appendix~\ref{app:full-ledger}).
  \item \textbf{Findings on the state of the field.} Most reviewed works automate existing analyses; solutions to time series requirements are rarely tested and mostly stay in task modules; generality is demonstrated for about half of the shared modules; and projection and hindsight decision have no evaluated output. The open problems of Section~\ref{sec:open-problems} follow as the requirements that remain untested or unconsolidated.
\end{itemize}




\section{What Is a Time Series Agent?}
\label{sec:what-is}

A Time Series Agent is a system in which an LLM operates through a reusable runtime and modules specific to time series. This is the one concept the survey introduces; the box states it as Eq.~\eqref{eq:tsa-formula}, together with the split of its last term and the general LLM-based Agent it specializes.

\begin{tcolorbox}[
  colback=surveyWarn,
  colframe=surveyWarn,
  boxrule=0pt,
  arc=7pt,
  left=12pt,
  right=12pt,
  top=10pt,
  bottom=10pt
]
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\textbf{LLM-based Agent} &\approx \text{LLM}+\text{Harness},\\
\textbf{Time Series Agent} &\approx \text{LLM}+\text{General Harness}+\text{Time Series Harness},\\
\textbf{Time Series Harness} &= \text{General Time Series Harness}+\text{Task-Specific Harness}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:tsa-formula}
\end{equation}
\end{tcolorbox}

The LLM interprets the request and current observations, chooses the next tool or environment action, and uses feedback to revise its plan. The General Harness supplies interface and interaction, memory and context, tools and execution, planning and orchestration, and optimization and evolution. The Time Series Harness preserves temporal state, operations, context, and validation; statistical models, time series foundation models, databases, simulators, retrieval systems, and evaluators operate as callable components within it. Such components can also sit outside a single Agent: a workbench exposes shared knowledge, data, and experiment tools to any compatible Agent, so improvements persist across Agents and teams \citep{tsflab2026}.

At an abstract level, the runtime can be written as
\begin{equation}
  a_k = \pi_\theta(s_k), \qquad
  s_{k+1} = \mathcal{H}(s_k,a_k,o_{k+1}),
  \label{eq:agent-loop}
\end{equation}
where $s_k$ is the visible state at execution step $k$, $a_k$ is a tool or environment action, $o_{k+1}$ is the returned observation, and $\mathcal{H}$ is the Harness transition. Time Series Harness modules add temporal type information to each state, action, and observation.

Each term is described with one vocabulary, listed in Table~\ref{tab:term-basis} with its basis. None of these vocabularies is a new classification: the mechanism dimensions are taken from prior surveys, the task names inside each cell of the task grid are standard in time series analysis, the two axes of the grid are standard output types and the classical division at the current time, and the temporal functions and evaluation criteria follow from the definition of their term. The LLM term needs no vocabulary, because every LLM-side contribution changes the same caller. The labels are many-to-many annotations of a module, so a module may carry several mechanism, function, or task labels while counting once at its assigned term. A module keeps its term when another task calls it; a new task objective instead motivates a distinct task module.

\noindent\textbf{Scope of inclusion.} The formula also fixes which works are in scope and in what role. A work that changes only the LLM term is an LLM-side technique, as in Time-LLM \citep{jin2024timellm} and ChatTS \citep{chatts2025}. Benchmarks, environments, and datasets evaluate the terms and are recorded as resources. Of the 162 works in scope, 106 are feedback-driven Agents, 21 fixed workflows, 18 LLM-side techniques, and 17 resources.

\section{LLM Side: The Caller of the Harness}
\label{sec:llm-side}

In Eq.~\eqref{eq:agent-loop}, the LLM is the policy $\pi_\theta$: it reads the visible state $s_k$, selects the next action $a_k$, usually a function call with its arguments, and interprets the returned observation $o_{k+1}$. Every LLM-side contribution we review serves this role, so the LLM side is not divided into categories. Two questions organize it instead: what training adds to the model that makes the calls, and which time series capabilities remain with the tools it calls. Training has been used to let the model read time series directly (Section~\ref{sec:llm-read}) and to make it a better caller of temporal workflows (Section~\ref{sec:llm-caller}). Time series foundation models, by contrast, enter the Agent as forecasting tools (Section~\ref{sec:llm-tsfm}). A model that perceives time series natively, as multimodal LLMs perceive images, is the prospect that connects these lines (Section~\ref{sec:llm-native}).

Figure~\ref{fig:llm-contributions} places these contributions in the interaction loop: training changes the model, while execution, state, and feedback remain in the Harness.

\subsection{Reading time series inside the model}
\label{sec:llm-read}

Time-LLM \citep{jin2024timellm} makes a frozen language backbone operate on temporal patches. It constructs a compact set of text prototypes $E'$ from the pretrained word embedding space and uses the temporal patch embeddings as attention queries. For channel $i$ and attention head $k$, its reprogramming operation is
\begin{equation}
  Q_k^{(i)}=\widehat{X}^{(i)}_P W_k^Q,\quad
  K_k^{(i)}=E'W_k^K,\quad V_k^{(i)}=E'W_k^V,\qquad
  Z_k^{(i)}=\operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\frac{Q_k^{(i)}K_k^{(i)\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V_k^{(i)}.
  \label{eq:timellm-reprogramming}
\end{equation}
Here $d_k$ is the key dimension of attention head $k$. The text prototypes provide combinations of language space cues for local temporal patterns. After aggregation and projection, the reprogrammed patches enter the frozen backbone alongside Prompt-as-Prefix context describing the task, domain, and input statistics. An output projection produces the forecast and is trained with the input transformations under prediction loss. LLM4TS \citep{chang2024llm4ts} likewise studies adapting a pretrained language model to temporal inputs, illustrating how the learned numerical interface can be developed before the Agent workflow is assembled.

ChatTS \citep{chatts2025} develops a conversational temporal interface. Fixed-size patches pass through a five-layer MLP into the text embedding space and are inserted among the text tokens at their corresponding series positions. Its value-preserving normalization couples normalized patches with scale and offset information in the prompt. For a nonconstant series, the transformation and its reconstruction are
\begin{equation}
  x_t^{\mathrm{norm}}=\frac{x_t-x_{\min}}{x_{\max}-x_{\min}},\qquad
  x_t=(x_{\max}-x_{\min})x_t^{\mathrm{norm}}+x_{\min}.
  \label{eq:chatts-value-preserved}
\end{equation}
This mathematical restatement of the paper's normalization makes the role of the prompt metadata explicit: a statement about magnitude can refer to the original scale while the encoder processes normalized values. In an Agent, the input layer must preserve this correspondence between a numerical series and its language-facing description.

Agentic Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Time Series Analysis \citep{agenticrag2024} conditions specialized language models through differentiable prompt pools. Retrieved continuous prompt embeddings are concatenated with numerical inputs and passed through a learnable projection. The pattern store thus participates in model adaptation: its learned representation determines how historical temporal patterns condition the predictor. This choice suits temporal conditioning inside the model, whereas external episode retrieval preserves inspectable records for the running Agent.

For numerical forecasting, Time-LLM \citep{jin2024timellm} offers reprogramming of a frozen backbone, whereas ChatTS \citep{chatts2025} learns a direct patch-to-language interface for conversational analysis. Eq.~\eqref{eq:timellm-reprogramming} identifies where numerical patches interact with language prototypes; Eq.~\eqref{eq:chatts-value-preserved} identifies the scale information that must accompany normalized inputs. The choice therefore depends on the required output: a numerical forecast head or answers to temporal questions. The Harness supplies window construction, channel and unit metadata, consistent preprocessing, and routing between the learned interface and downstream tools.

A temporal encoder becomes useful for analysis through supervision that links signal structure to questions and explanations. ChatTS \citep{chatts2025} generates synthetic series with selected temporal attributes, constructs matching text, and evolves instructions for understanding and reasoning. Controlling the generated attributes supplies known temporal properties around which analytical instructions can be built.

\textit{Towards Time Series Reasoning with LLMs} \citep{chow2024tsreasoning} follows a related learning route through temporal encoding and reasoning supervision. Its encoder warm-up and subsequent language model adaptation connect temporal perception to explicit reasoning traces. Time-R1 \citep{timer12025} specializes this idea for forecasting: supervised warm-up teaches structured reasoning and complete forecast outputs, followed by reinforcement fine-tuning with format, length, and numerical feedback. Its reward maps normalized forecast error to a bounded signal,
\begin{equation}
  \gamma_{\mathrm{MSE}}=2\left(1-\frac{1}{1+e^{-2\operatorname{MSE}}}\right),
  \label{eq:timer1-reward}
\end{equation}
which equals one at zero error and approaches zero as error grows. This bounded transformation compresses differences among forecasts with large errors; the normalization of predictions and targets consequently determines the effective sensitivity of the reward. Eq.~\eqref{eq:timer1-reward} illustrates a training choice driven by outcomes: forecast error supplies a continuous signal alongside rewards for format, output length, and temporal structure. The accompanying GRIP optimizer uses nonuniform sampling and adaptive weighting over reasoning paths. Time-R1 \citep{timer12025} evaluates forecasting with MSE and MAE and ablates its reward components (Tables~2, 4, and 6); these experiments support comparisons of numerical performance and training choices. Explanation faithfulness requires an additional test of whether the stated reasoning reflects the evidence and computation that produced the forecast.

ChatAD \citep{chatad2026}, Section~4.2, develops an anomaly reasoning model through supervised training with LoRA and TKTO preference optimization. Feedback combines format compliance, task correctness, fluency, and human preference into score $S$. A smooth preference weight mixes desirable and undesirable response losses:
\begin{equation}
 \alpha=\sigma\!\left(\kappa(S-\eta)\right),\qquad
 \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{TKTO}}=\mathbb{E}_{(x,y,d)\sim\mathcal{D}}
 [\alpha\mathcal{L}_{D}(\pi_\theta,x,y)+(1-\alpha)\mathcal{L}_{U}(\pi_\theta,x,y)].
 \label{eq:chatad-tkto}
\end{equation}
At $S=\eta$, the two losses receive equal weight; increasing $\kappa$ makes the transition between preferred and rejected responses sharper. ChatAD \citep{chatad2026} uses this soft assignment to address noise near a hard preference threshold (Section~4.2.2, Eqs.~3--5). The composite score combines LLM assessments of format, correctness, and fluency with human preference. Thus Eq.~\eqref{eq:chatad-tkto} compares graded preference supervision with a binary accept/reject decision: its practical requirements include a consistent scoring rubric and calibration of the score relative to $\eta$. ChatAD's evaluation separates detection accuracy, F1, and false positive rate from open-ended reasoning scores and format parsing rates (Section~4.3.2, Tables~3--4). The latter measure judged answer quality and parseability; checking explanation faithfulness additionally requires evidence about how the answer was produced. The synthetic dialogue agents create supervision, while the resulting ChatAD model supplies the learned anomaly reasoning capability. AnomaMind \citep{anomamind2026} also combines a trained detector with an external evidence acquisition workflow; its task-specific feedback and detection equations are developed in the anomaly section.

The practical choice is between supervising temporal descriptions, rewarding completed forecasts, and grading analytical responses. ChatTS \citep{chatts2025} supplies supervision built from series attributes for temporal questions; Time-R1 \citep{timer12025} ties learning to forecast outcomes; ChatAD \citep{chatad2026} weights preferred and rejected answers by a graded score. Their use requires different Harness support: preservation of series--text correspondence, parsing and chronological scoring of forecasts, or a consistent scoring rubric.

These models still act as the caller once they are placed in an Agent. ChatTS \citep{chatts2025} answers questions about the shape and scale of a series, and Time-R1 \citep{timer12025} emits a forecast together with its reasoning, but neither replaces the operations on which an Agent's output depends: exact statistics over declared windows, forecasts with calibrated uncertainty, and checks of availability time remain tool calls (Sections~\ref{sec:general-ts-harness} and~\ref{sec:task-specific-harness}). Temporal training improves what the caller perceives and how it reasons about a result; the Harness keeps what must be computed exactly and audited.

Evaluation follows the capability being selected. Forecast accuracy concerns predicted values; explanation faithfulness concerns whether the explanation reflects the model's actual computation; evidence grounding concerns whether its claims are supported by the supplied series or documents; and action selection quality concerns feasible, useful tool or environment actions. These distinctions motivate complementary checks: held-out numerical errors, controlled interventions on claimed reasoning factors, verification of cited observations and their availability times, and evaluation of actions by executing them. Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} connects these requirements to evaluation resources.

\subsection{Training the caller}
\label{sec:llm-caller}

Workflow training changes the model that selects or assesses actions. TSOrchestra, introduced in \textit{Conversational Time Series Foundation Models} \citep{conversationaltsfm2025}, trains a language-model judge for iterative ensemble optimization. A numerical optimizer proposes nonnegative weights over forecasting models that sum to one. The judge inspects metrics, weights, and optimization history to decide whether to continue or accept a configuration. Supervised fine-tuning uses real trajectories and augmented boundary cases; subsequent GRPO training incorporates held-out forecasting performance and explanation feedback. The trained judge controls numerical optimization by deciding when an ensemble configuration is ready to accept.

CastFSR \citep{castfsr2026} trains a broader policy over the Fast--Slow--Reflect workflow. Supervised distillation teaches executable stage sequences, followed by multi-turn reinforcement learning over complete trajectories. Training checks tool calls, stage order, timestamps, and completed forecasts before applying forecasting feedback. At inference, the policy still interacts with the numerical forecasting and contextual retrieval tools described in Section~\ref{sec:task-specific-harness}. TimeART \citep{timeart2026} similarly combines a trained reasoner with an analytical toolkit, connecting policy learning to explicit temporal operations.

These methods train the function calling role directly. TSOrchestra \citep{conversationaltsfm2025} is useful when a judge must accept or continue numerical ensemble optimization; CastFSR \citep{castfsr2026} learns decisions across a prescribed tool workflow; TimeART \citep{timeart2026} learns tool use from experience and reflection trajectories. A developer therefore selects both a model and its required feedback interface. The Harness must expose optimizer histories or tool observations, retain intermediate state, enforce valid calls and temporal boundaries, and record outcomes for evaluation. Action quality is tested through executed trajectories and their task outcomes, while any accompanying explanation is assessed separately.

\subsection{Time series foundation models as tools}
\label{sec:llm-tsfm}

Time series foundation models such as TimesFM \citep{das2024timesfm}, Chronos \citep{ansari2024chronos}, and Moirai \citep{woo2024moirai} are pretrained on large collections of series and map a context window to a forecast. In a Time Series Agent they do not belong to the LLM term: they take a series and return values, whereas the LLM decides which model to call, on which window, and whether to accept the result. Last-Mile \citep{lastmile2026} lets an Agent revise a TimesFM baseline forecast through constrained, evidence-backed actions; MoiraiAgent \citep{moiraiagent2026} lets an LLM select a forecasting expert; TimeCopilot \citep{timecopilot2025} exposes statistical, machine learning, and foundation models behind one forecasting interface and combines them in ensembles; and the evolved forecasting code of \citet{promptevolutionforecast2026} receives a default TabPFN forecast before a final LLM pass.

Because a foundation model is a tool, it is also replaceable. MetaCaster \citep{metacaster2026} notes that foundation models remain uneconomical in resource-constrained settings; its Harness instead generates few-shot training series and trains lightweight forecasters as scheduled jobs. TSFLab \citep{tsflab2026} moves forecasting models out of any single Agent: its agent-agnostic workbench integrates 323 forecasting models and 93 datasets that any compatible Agent can call. The choice among foundation, statistical, and lightweight forecasters is therefore a Harness decision about cost and accuracy rather than a property of the LLM.

STRIDE \citep{stride2026} connects language reasoning to a separate numerical foundation model. A teacher constructs reference reasoning from training windows and their pseudo-future targets; a smaller language model learns this reasoning. Its last hidden states $H$ are mean-pooled and projected into a reasoning vector $e_R$ compatible with the numerical model, which is fused with native time series embeddings $E_{\mathrm{TS}}$:
\begin{equation}
  h_R=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{L}H_i,\qquad
  e_R=h_R W_{\mathrm{proj}},\qquad
  E_{\mathrm{fused}}=e_R\oplus E_{\mathrm{TS}},\qquad
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{total}}=\alpha\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}+\beta\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Quantile}}.
  \label{eq:stride-fusion}
\end{equation}
The fusion operation uses sequence prefixing or initial-state substitution according to the forecaster architecture. The joint objective updates the numerical model and projection together with LoRA parameters in the language model. This design lets a language-derived reasoning representation influence continuous forecasts while retaining a dedicated numerical decoder. The language model here contributes a representation rather than a call, so the trained model belongs to the LLM side while its numerical decoder remains a foundation model; STRIDE marks the boundary between the two.

\subsection{Toward native temporal perception}
\label{sec:llm-native}

Vision followed a comparable path: image understanding first reached LLM agents through captioning and recognition tools and later became a native modality of multimodal LLMs. Time series show the first steps of the same path. ChatTS \citep{chatts2025} inserts patch embeddings among text tokens, as vision encoders do for image patches; TimeMaster \citep{timemaster2025} and Zephyrus-R1 \citep{zephyrusr12026} train vision-language models that read plotted series; and TimeOmni-1 \citep{timeomni2026} trains one model for temporal reasoning across four tasks. If time series become a native modality of omni-modal LLMs, perception will move from the series views of Section~\ref{sec:temporal-processing} into the model. Operations whose results must be exact and auditable, namely statistics over declared windows, calibrated forecast distributions, and checks of availability time, should remain Harness tools. Which temporal capabilities belong in the model and which in the Harness is an open problem (Section~\ref{sec:open-problems}).

\section{General Harness}
\label{sec:general-harness}

The General Harness connects the LLM's action choices to executable operations, persistent state, and feedback. We synthesize five recurring mechanism dimensions from Agent architecture and Harness research \citep{sumers2024coala,wang2024agentsurvey,ning2026codeharness,lin2026agenticharness}, the basis given in Table~\ref{tab:term-basis}. A recent survey of Agent engineering likewise treats an Agent as a foundation model coupled with an execution Harness and divides the Harness into six runtime responsibilities: observation, context, control, action, state, and verification \citep{guo2026harnesssurvey}. Our dimensions cover these responsibilities: observation and action fall under interface and interaction, context and state under memory and context, control under planning and orchestration, and verification under verification tools. Table~\ref{tab:component-vocab} gives each dimension a design question and divides it into second-level classes by one criterion: the part of the Agent loop an interface defines, the scope and lifetime of stored information, the object that is executed, the object that orchestration organizes, and the reusable asset an update changes. The classes follow the Harness mechanisms of \citet{ning2026codeharness}, the memory types of \citet{sumers2024coala}, and the editable Harness components of \citet{lin2026agenticharness}. The dimensions can coexist: an analytical episode may retrieve context, plan a sequence, execute tools, and revise an artifact using validation feedback. The examples below examine how time series systems implement these dimensions and which parts of their implementation can support another task. Temporal requirements and task objectives subsequently determine how these runtime choices are specialized in Sections~\ref{sec:general-ts-harness} and \ref{sec:task-specific-harness}.

\subsection{Interface and Interaction}
\label{sec:gh-interface}

The interface defines what the Agent observes, which actions it can request, and how a result returns to the next reasoning step. Its three classes correspond to the three parts of the loop in Eq.~\eqref{eq:agent-loop}: the reasoning step, the action, and the environment that returns the observation.

\noindent\textbf{Reasoning loop.} TS-Agent \citep{tsagent2025} uses an iterative thought--action--observation loop: the reasoner chooses its next step after receiving the structured result of the preceding analytical operation. TimeART \citep{timeart2026} applies the same loop to atomic temporal tools. This supports questions whose required computation becomes clear during analysis.

\noindent\textbf{Action interface.} An action interface lets the LLM identify an operation, supply its arguments, and interpret success or failure. Open-TI \citep{openti2024} matches a user intent to a tool and retries when the match fails, and NeuroAct \citep{neuroact2026} discloses tools and skills progressively, so that the model sees the operations relevant to the current stage. The numerical operations and their temporal arguments are developed in Section~\ref{sec:general-ts-harness}; the action interface can remain the same as the analytical objective changes.

\noindent\textbf{Environment and users.} AION \citep{aion2026} starts from an explicit environment: a task file, an accessible workspace, and a validator. The task file declares the required artifact and constraints before work begins. Observations need not be text: the plotting tool of Zephyrus-R1 \citep{zephyrusr12026} returns an image of the series. Users are part of this environment. An exploratory request benefits from successive observations and follow-up questions; a submission-oriented task benefits from an explicit output specification and acceptance check. BatteryLake \citep{batterylake2026} routes low-confidence extractions to a person for verification, and TimeSeriesScientist \citep{timeseriesscientist2025} returns a report of its workflow decisions, metrics, and provenance.

\subsection{Memory and Context}
\label{sec:gh-memory}

Memory determines which earlier observations remain available to later steps. Its classes differ in scope and lifetime: the next step, the current episode, a knowledge store, or several roles \citep{sumers2024coala,ning2026codeharness}.

\noindent\textbf{Working context.} Working context holds what the next step needs. TS-Reasoner \citep{tsreasoner2026} keeps a buffer of explored solution paths so that its planner does not evaluate them again. The design choice is which information remains as raw tool output and which becomes a compact summary.

\noindent\textbf{Episodic records.} TS-Agent \citep{tsagent2025} retains an evidence log so that subsequent reasoning can use completed analytical results. AION \citep{aion2026} records progress, decisions, and structural state around execution and review, and CLIMATEAGENT \citep{climateagent2025} persists plans, scripts, results, and logs with checkpoints. The evidence log supports an answer; the progress record supports continuation after interruption or handoff.

\noindent\textbf{Knowledge retrieval.} FinAgent \citep{finagent2024} stores summaries with a dedicated query field and retrieves them by vector similarity, and From Plots to Words \citep{plotstowords2026} supplies system configuration and operational knowledge to downstream reasoning. Retrieval keyed by signal structure or bounded by availability time adds a temporal requirement and is discussed in Section~\ref{sec:temporal-context}.

\noindent\textbf{Shared state.} TradingAgents \citep{tradingagents2024} shares state through structured reports. Each role extracts the information it needs and returns a completed report with an identifiable author and purpose, so the input to each decision is easier to inspect than an unrestricted conversation history. MAS4TS \citep{mas4ts2026} instead exchanges agent states through a confidence-gated shared memory.

\subsection{Tools and Execution}
\label{sec:gh-tools}

Execution converts a selected action into a computation and returns its result, artifacts, and status. The classes differ in what is executed: a registered function, code or a computational job, or a check \citep{ning2026codeharness}.

\noindent\textbf{Function calls.} A function call invokes a registered capability. Agentic RAG for TSA \citep{agenticrag2024} routes a request through a master agent to sub-agents and their tools, so that a new task family adds a sub-agent rather than a new runtime. FinArena \citep{finarena2025} calls external retrieval only when the model judges that its pretrained knowledge cannot answer the query.

\noindent\textbf{Code and job execution.} Generated code and long computations need a controlled environment. SIREN \citep{siren2026} runs Python through a guarded server that validates paths and tools before execution, and TS-Reasoner \citep{tsreasoner2026} lets the LLM write a custom operator when its library lacks one. MetaCaster's Forecaster Trainer \citep{metacaster2026} executes at the level of computational jobs: it assembles jobs indexed by model, hyperparameters, and dataset, queues them, assigns available GPUs, monitors processes, and recovers errors or interrupted runs. The reusable mechanism is scheduling and recovery; the job's forecasting objective is supplied by the task procedure.

\noindent\textbf{Verification tools.} A verification tool returns a deterministic check rather than an analytical result. TimeSage's reference runtime in TimeSage-MT \citep{timesagemt2026} separates the LLM Planner and Reporter from deterministic validation, profiling, execution, and evaluation, and HydroAgent \citep{hydroagent2026} binds each skill to an output contract that a deterministic executor enforces. These divisions show whether an error arose in the chosen workflow or in the implementation of an operation, and they separate the ability to complete a computation from the quality of its result.

\subsection{Planning and Orchestration}
\label{sec:gh-planning}

Orchestration organizes four objects: the steps of a plan, the execution of those steps, the checkpoints a result must pass, and the roles that share the work \citep{ning2026codeharness}.

\noindent\textbf{Decomposition and planning.} Planning specifies dependencies among the operations needed to deliver an artifact. TS-Agent \citep{tsagent2025} chooses actions incrementally from accumulated observations. TS-Reasoner \citep{tsreasoner2026} constructs an ordered executable plan and revises it after failure. The first approach makes the next action sensitive to newly observed evidence; the second provides an explicit plan that can be inspected and rerun as a whole. From Plots to Words \citep{plotstowords2026} turns a query into a hierarchical plan whose steps are delegated, and SAGE-Sleep \citep{sagesleep2026} routes each request by intent before planning.

\noindent\textbf{Execution control.} Control determines whether the Agent continues, revises its work, requests review, or terminates. In TS-Reasoner \citep{tsreasoner2026}, the task decomposer produces an ordered operator plan, the executor runs it, and an execution error triggers a new end-to-end plan; the error message is actionable feedback about the current procedure. CLIMATEAGENT \citep{climateagent2025} likewise retries a failed step with its error. The source and timing of feedback determine what can change during an episode. An execution error is immediately available; a resolved forecast error is available only after the target window has been observed. FLAIRR-TS \citep{flairr2025} uses recent ground truth to refine forecasting prompts and is therefore developed with the forecasting methods. A system that combines these controls should expose both the retry policy for failed operations and the schedule under which task outcomes become available for learning.

\noindent\textbf{Checkpoints and governance.} AION \citep{aion2026} extends planning to the lifecycle of work through protocols for execution, review, rechecking, memory updates, and stopping. It adds artifact-level validity checks and a separate scored evaluation, so a candidate can be returned for revision before acceptance. The design separates exploratory choices from required checkpoints: an Agent may explore several analytical routes while every route must still pass an artifact validator. Governance applies the same idea to changes of the Harness itself. Steward \citep{steward2026} admits a prompt edit only after schema, allow-list, append-only, and similarity checks, and keeps a one-commit revert.

\noindent\textbf{Multi-agent coordination.} Coordination defines responsibilities and the information passed between roles. TradingAgents \citep{tradingagents2024} uses structured reports so that each role consumes a defined state and returns a completed result. AION \citep{aion2026} makes work, review, rechecking, and stop signals explicit in the communication protocol. AgentFM \citep{agentfm2025} reassigns system roles at runtime, and TS-Debate \citep{multimodaldebate2026} assigns modalities to separate agents that exchange reports over debate rounds. The task determines the content of the handoff. In FinCon \citep{fincon2024}, analysts and a manager aggregate financial evidence before portfolio actions; in TimeSeriesScientist \citep{timeseriesscientist2025}, curation and planning outputs guide forecasting and reporting. Their objectives are analyzed in Section~\ref{sec:task-specific-harness}. These examples suggest defining a role around the artifact it must deliver and the check that permits the next role to proceed, which makes handoffs inspectable and gives failures a recovery point.

\subsection{Optimization and Evolution}
\label{sec:gh-optimization}

A Harness improves by changing one of its reusable assets across episodes: the prompt, the stored experience, the tools and skills, or the Harness configuration \citep{lin2026agenticharness}. Each update needs a record of the source episodes and the criterion used to accept it.

\noindent\textbf{Prompt optimization.} ATLAS \citep{atlas2025} edits only the static instruction block of a trading prompt, keeps its run-time placeholders fixed, and scores each candidate with the task metric. TradeGrad \citep{textualgradienttrading2026} samples scored programs and asks an LLM to estimate a textual gradient from them. Both take the task metric only as an input, so they are General Harness mechanisms.

\noindent\textbf{Experience distillation.} TimeClaw with Exploratory Execution Learning (TimeClaw-EEL) \citep{timeclaw2026explore} compares alternative executed trajectories and distills useful experience into a state available to later episodes; the focused TimeClaw-EEL analysis in Section~\ref{sec:open-problems} gives the source equations for this update. NeuroAct \citep{neuroact2026} stores conditional recommendations keyed by task and model evidence and revises later decisions from their outcomes.

\noindent\textbf{Tool and skill evolution.} TimeClaw-Harness \citep{timeclaw2026harness}, a separate TimeClaw runtime for contextualized time series, develops a tool-promotion mechanism: a code-specialized LLM receives clustered trajectories and task descriptions, extracts a recurring procedure, and produces code with an invocation description. The candidate is tested on held-out similar tasks and enters the registry after meeting the success threshold. TimeSage-EV \citep{timesageev2026} induces reusable Python skills and admits them after a quality check.

\noindent\textbf{Harness configuration search.} EvoTS-Agent \citep{evotsagent2026} evolves whole trajectories through revision, alternative strategies, and recombination, and accepts a new incumbent only when an external validation score improves. AQuA \citep{aqua2026} compiles and scores one configuration change at a time. Meta-Harness \citep{lee2026metaharness} optimizes a Harness end to end outside time series.

These mechanisms change different assets: a prompt edit or distilled experience changes the guidance available to the reasoner, while tool promotion changes what the executor can call. The procedure's analytical content determines its temporal or task scope; the mechanism for extracting, testing, and installing the update remains reusable.

\section{General Time Series Harness}
\label{sec:general-ts-harness}

The General Time Series Harness specializes the General Harness mechanism dimensions through temporal order, index, frequency, window, horizon, regime, availability time, and leakage control. These temporal mechanisms build on the interaction, execution, and feedback interfaces of the General Harness.

The contract boundary makes the distinction explicit. General Harness specifies how an Agent stores state, invokes tools, coordinates roles, and verifies outputs. General Time Series Harness specifies what a valid temporal state or operation means: which observations were available, how a window is aligned, which frequency and units apply, and how a revision is represented. TimeClaw-Harness \citep{timeclaw2026harness} and AION \citep{aion2026} make this boundary explicit through time series-native execution, temporal grounding, and reliability mechanisms.

At the current time $\tau$, when the Agent issues its output, the admissible information is
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{I}_{\tau} = \{(x_i,t_i,v_i): v_i \leq \tau\},
  \label{eq:temporal-availability}
\end{equation}
where $t_i$ is the event time and $v_i$ is the time at which the value became available. In a backtest, $\tau$ is the simulated origin rather than the time the experiment is run. The record keeps the two times that temporal databases distinguish, the valid time at which a fact holds and the transaction time at which it is recorded \citep{snodgrass1985taxonomy,jensen1999temporal}. Real-time macroeconomic data sets keep every released vintage of a series for the same reason \citep{croushore2001realtime}, and LLM forecasts that use information published after the current time show look-ahead bias \citep{glasserman2023lookahead,gao2025lookahead}. The information boundary admits information published by $\tau$, including a known future calendar event; the task specifies which observation and target windows may be used.

Figure~\ref{fig:temporal-harness-functions} connects the four shared functions through the objects they manage. Read the upper timeline first: availability time determines whether a source can be used at the current time. Each card of the middle row lists the second-level classes of its function (Table~\ref{tab:gts-vocab}) above one illustration: a record of time, value, and unit; a transformed, analyzed, and LLM-viewed window; external events and a past case; and an availability check that admits or excludes a source. The lower paths connect the LLM to requests and returned observations. AION \citep{aion2026} and TimeClaw-Harness \citep{timeclaw2026harness} implement these functions in their temporal runtimes.

The four functions below follow from the availability record in Eq.~\eqref{eq:temporal-availability}. A shared module can store and version the records $(x_i,t_i,v_i)$ (data management), operate on the values $x_i$ over windows of $t_i$ (processing), admit information other than the series into $\mathcal{I}_\tau$ (context and memory), or check that inputs and outputs respect $v_i\leq\tau$, windows, and units (validation). These are the components of a data model in the sense of \citet{codd1980datamodels}, namely its structures, the operators on them, and its integrity rules, together with the scope of what the record admits. The same division appears in the basic steps of a forecasting task, which gather data and accumulated expertise, analyze and model them, and evaluate the result \citep{hyndman2021fpp}. Each function applies the General Harness mechanisms of Table~\ref{tab:component-vocab} to temporal objects: data management draws on interfaces and state; processing on tools and workflow; context on memory and retrieval; and validation on control and feedback. A module can serve several functions, but none of the four can be removed: all 97 General Time Series Harness modules carry at least one function, and each function contains modules that carry no other (26 for processing, 8 for validation, 8 for context and memory, and 5 for data management). Coordination, scheduling, and optimization act on any function and belong to the General Harness, while the output a module produces belongs to the Task-Specific Harness. Section~\ref{sec:task-specific-harness} examines how target outputs specialize these shared capabilities.

Table~\ref{tab:gts-vocab} divides each function with the same record. Data management and validation split by the component they handle: the value $x_i$ with its measurement, the event time $t_i$, and the availability time $v_i$; data management adds the workspace that holds the records, and validation adds the claims made from them. Processing splits by what an operation returns: a transformed series, statistics about the series, a view of the series for the LLM, or a reusable library of operations. Context and memory splits by what is admitted into $\mathcal{I}_\tau$: external events and text, historical analogues of the current series, and procedural experience from earlier analyses, the three memory contents also distinguished by \citet{chen2026tsagentsurvey}. The first and last correspond to the semantic and procedural memory of \citet{sumers2024coala}; historical analogues follow the use of naturally occurring analogues, past states of the observed system that resemble the present one, in weather prediction \citep{lorenz1969analogues}, rather than the Agent's own episodes. The availability and past-only checks of validation guard against the leakage defined by \citet{kaufman2012leakage}, and chronological evaluation follows the rolling-origin design of \citet{tashman2000outofsample}. The subsections follow the rows of the table, and their paragraph heads follow its classes.

\subsection{Temporal Data Management}
\label{sec:temporal-data}

Before an Agent can compare signals or call a model, its inputs must identify what was measured, when it was measured, when it became available, and how the stored values should be interpreted. The input schema therefore includes timestamps, sampling intervals, units, missingness, and revision information. These properties form the interface between the raw source and every later operation. AION \citep{aion2026}, Section~3.3.1, places this interface before model selection: the task must resolve the observation unit, visible history, cutoff, requested horizon, granularity, and output frequency. The resulting requirements determine which data are admissible and which operations can satisfy the request.

\noindent\textbf{Measurement schema.} BatteryLake \citep{batterylake2026} makes measurement semantics explicit through a canonical sample schema. Time, voltage, current, temperature, capacity, and step type have fixed units and domains; the current-sign convention distinguishes charging from discharging. This supports consistent interpretation of heterogeneous battery files before downstream health or lifetime analysis. SIREN \citep{siren2026} normalizes timestamps, units, and spatial matches across its weather sources, and World2TS \citep{world2ts2026} consolidates reports into timestamped event records with a declared scope, so that events are stored with the same care as values.

\noindent\textbf{Time index and alignment.} LEMAD \citep{ji2025lemad} addresses a related ingestion problem for operational services: its preprocessing module aligns timestamps, normalizes formats, and handles missing values across heterogeneous streams. AgriWorld \citep{agriworld2026} resamples agricultural data to a canonical frequency. Streaming inputs add an ordering requirement. CALM \citep{calm2025} buffers incoming observations per key before its stateful sliding-window function emits the ordered window; without this step, out-of-order arrival changes which observations enter a computation. The schema defines the meaning of each sample, while the index and the buffer preserve its position and order as samples arrive.

\noindent\textbf{Point-in-time access.} A record can be used only from its availability time $v_i$. SwingGuard \citep{swingguard2026} aligns time-indexed evidence under an availability cutoff before its trading procedure runs, and Quant-Arena \citep{quantarena2026} serves market observations through an interface scoped by the decision date. Storing $v_i$ with the data lets the availability check of Section~\ref{sec:temporal-validation} be enforced at access rather than reconstructed afterwards.

\noindent\textbf{Temporal workspace.} Stored data also need a representation that preserves numerical precision. TimeClaw-Harness \citep{timeclaw2026harness}, Section~3.2, keeps the full time series object in a task-local workspace. Tools run on that object and return compact structured observations to the LLM. SAGE-AD \citep{sagead2026}, Section~3.2, likewise separates the original series used by numerical tools from a compressed summary used in prompting, and SAGE-Sleep \citep{sagesleep2026} exposes sensor data through a queryable store with normalized timestamps. These mechanisms keep the language interface compact while the analytical operations retain access to the measurements. The use of SAGE-AD's evidence for anomalies is discussed in Section~\ref{sec:task-anomaly}.

For a new application, the ingestion design follows the source of ambiguity: measurement schemas address heterogeneous files, time indices and ordered buffers handle alignment and streaming arrivals, point-in-time interfaces record availability, and numerical workspaces preserve tool inputs. These choices can be combined as the data sources require. Testing should vary arrival order, units, missing entries, or timestamp formats and inspect the numerical object delivered to the next operation.

\subsection{Temporal Processing}
\label{sec:temporal-processing}

Processing converts a stored series into the evidence needed for analysis. Its classes differ in what an operation returns: a transformed series, statistics about the series, a view of the series for the LLM, or a reusable library of operations. Inputs and outputs must preserve the relation to the source sequence: the same numerical array may describe levels, differences, normalized values, or an aggregate over a longer interval. A useful tool interface therefore exposes both the operation and the temporal meaning of its result.

\noindent\textbf{Preprocessing and transformation.} TimeSeriesScientist's Curator \citep{timeseriesscientist2025}, Section~3.2, illustrates how inspection determines the transformation. Given a series $D$, it produces data statistics $S$, missing-value information $M$, outlier findings $O$, and a treatment strategy $\pi$:
\begin{equation}
  Q=A_f(D)=(S,M,O,\pi),\qquad
  \widetilde{D}=\phi_{\pi}(D).
  \label{eq:tsci-curator}
\end{equation}
The processed series then supports visualization, decomposition, and autocorrelation analysis. These diagnostics precede the forecasting-specific model selection in Section~\ref{sec:task-forecasting}. For example, identifying missing intervals changes the preprocessing plan, whereas ranking candidate forecasters requires a target horizon and validation loss. Separating the two decisions makes the preparation stage reusable while keeping model selection tied to its output objective. GenAutoML \citep{genautoml2026} wraps a model with reversible instance normalization and resamples a series causally before imputing missing values; such a transformation must be recorded so that results can be mapped back to the original scale.

\noindent\textbf{Statistical and spectral analysis.} TS-Agent \citep{tsagent2025} supplies analytical APIs that operate directly on native numerical series and return structured findings. TESSA \citep{tessa2024} computes decomposition, lag, rolling-window, and correlation features, CastFlow \citep{castflow2026} profiles distribution statistics and spectral entropy of the observed series, and ElliottAgents \citep{elliottagents2025} enumerates candidate wave structures on historical charts. The output is evidence about the series rather than a new series.

\noindent\textbf{Series views for the LLM.} Processing can also change how a series is shown to the LLM. MAS4TS \citep{mas4ts2026} represents a series through plot anchors and a latent trajectory, TS-Debate \citep{multimodaldebate2026} renders time--frequency charts with numerical lookup, and the Metric Agent of AgentFM \citep{agentfm2025} turns multivariate measurements into intervals, trends, and anomaly descriptions. A view must keep its mapping to the values, so that a claim made from it can be checked against the series.

\noindent\textbf{Operator and skill libraries.} TS-Reasoner \citep{tsreasoner2026} wraps statistical tools, learned models, and numerical utilities in a consistent calling structure, and TimeART \citep{timeart2026} uses atomic tools for computation-intensive temporal operations. TimeSage-MT \citep{timesagemt2026} provides a skill library that a planner uses across analytical turns. MAS4TS combines decomposition, smoothing, statistical prediction, constraint projection, and residual correction into chains; its router selects from a task-indexed chain registry using a downstream objective, so we record the routed chain as a task-specific procedure and its plot-anchor representation as the shared module. Atomic operators expose intermediate findings for inspection and revision; composed chains capture recurring sequences of analysis. The appropriate granularity depends on where new observations can change the plan. In either case, the next tool needs the processed window, applied transformation, and output scale, which makes explicit intermediate metadata a practical condition for reuse. Evaluation should check individual numerical results and the composed sequence, as in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}.

\subsection{Temporal Context and Memory}
\label{sec:temporal-context}

Temporal analysis often requires information beyond the numerical sequence. Documents describe events, earlier windows provide analogies, and earlier analyses supply reusable procedures; these are the three classes below. The retrieval problem has two parts: representing relevance to the current signal and determining whether the evidence is available at the current time. An Agent needs both to use retrieved material consistently.

\noindent\textbf{External events and text.} NEXUS \citep{nexus2026}, Section~3.1, aligns historical values and contemporaneous textual factors into a chronological context timeline. The result preserves when a contextual explanation applies to the observations, and organizes the history around the relationship between a numerical change and its accompanying evidence. TFRBench \citep{tfrbench2026} retrieves external events only from a bounded window and returns them with their dates. \citet{williams2025cik} distinguish five kinds of such information for forecasting: intemporal, historical, covariate, future, and causal.

\noindent\textbf{Historical analogues.} Similar past windows or cases can inform the current analysis. FLAIRR-TS \citep{flairr2025} ranks past windows by their similarity to the current one, SIREN \citep{siren2026} keeps a database of earlier operational cases with their event conditions, and MOSAIC \citep{mosaic2026} retrieves prior cases conditioned on a profile of the current data. Relevance is defined by the signal, so retrieval must compare series structure as well as text.

\noindent\textbf{Procedural experience.} TimeClaw-Harness \citep{timeclaw2026harness}, Section~3.4, retrieves prior analytical episodes through separate signal and text keys. A memory record is
\begin{equation}
  m=(\mathcal{T},\phi,\psi,\zeta,\rho),
  \label{eq:timeclaw-memory}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{T}$ denotes the task, $\phi$ and $\psi$ are signal and text keys, $\zeta$ is the successful trajectory, and $\rho$ records its transferable rationale. Similar wording may describe different signal structures, while similar shapes may occur under different contextual conditions. Separate keys preserve these two sources of relevance, and the retained trajectory explains how a preceding episode was solved. TSHarness \citep{tsharness2026} stores analytical procedures with their applicability conditions, recommended tools, and evidence requirements, and FinCon \citep{fincon2024} decays procedural and episodic memories according to the timeliness of the underlying information.

The task-specific boundary enters when the retrieval or retention policy depends on the target outcome. FinMem \citep{finmem2023} ranks and promotes financial memories for trading, while FLAIRR-TS \citep{flairr2025} combines its analogue retrieval with observed forecasting errors. Their policies are analyzed under decision making and forecasting in Section~\ref{sec:task-specific-harness}. The memory design should follow the object to be reused: external events require temporal alignment, historical analogues require signal-sensitive retrieval, and procedural experience requires the associated trajectory and rationale. Task feedback then determines how those records are selected or retained. Comparing retrieved content, availability time, and downstream effects reveals whether the memory contributes relevant evidence to the current decision.

\subsection{Temporal Validation}
\label{sec:temporal-validation}

An analysis can execute successfully while using information from the wrong period, computing with future values, reading malformed data, or making a claim unsupported by its observations. Temporal validation checks these four relationships before the task score is interpreted. AION \citep{aion2026} separates validity findings, including timestamp alignment and leakage boundaries, from scored outcomes such as forecasting error or report quality. The distinction allows the same temporal checks to precede several different output metrics.

\noindent\textbf{Availability checks.} Some implementations enforce availability at retrieval time. WorldReasoner \citep{worldreasoner2026} filters article and event records against the simulated forecast date. TIEM \citep{tiem2026} additionally checks the provenance of retained skills: every ancestor must have resolved before the current cutoff. TimeSage-EV \citep{timesageev2026}, Appendix~A.6, imposes a related capability boundary by activating a newly accepted skill only in the next period. These mechanisms address different paths through which later information can enter an earlier analysis: retrieved records, inherited experience, and newly created tools. Equation~\eqref{eq:temporal-availability} provides the common information boundary, while each implementation checks a particular access path. TraceMAS \citep{tracemas2026} also reviews retrieved evidence for availability and for consistency between sources.

\noindent\textbf{Past-only computation.} A computation can use the future even when every input was available. FactorMAD \citep{duan2025factormad} instructs factor construction to exclude future timestamps and use rolling statistics instead of statistics computed over the full series. This check is separable from the financial value of the factor. AQuA \citep{aqua2026} builds the same condition into its operator grammar, which admits only trailing-window and current-timestamp operators.

\noindent\textbf{Data and schema checks.} Validation also checks that values mean what the schema says. BatteryLake \citep{batterylake2026} admits converted files only through a schema validation gate, and NeuroAct \citep{neuroact2026} checks input shapes, channels, and units before windowed training runs. TSQAgent \citep{tsqbench2026} judges data quality with a shared library of temporal measurements; its selection and adjudication procedure is task-specific, and its judgment can guide data selection before forecasting or classification.

\noindent\textbf{Temporal claim verification.} The last check concerns what is claimed from the data. TS-Debate \citep{multimodaldebate2026} verifies each claim against the temporal scope it refers to, and EpiCoach \citep{epicoach2026} records hypotheses and predictions together with the data cutoff at which they were made. TS-Agent \citep{tsagent2025} uses temporal question intents to declare required evidence predicates: an anomaly location answer requires evidence of an anomaly and its supported segment, while other intents require trend, seasonality, or lag findings. That gate, specific to the question intent, is a Task-Specific Harness module, because its required predicates and answer schema are defined by the temporal question; shared numerical tools supply the findings it checks.

Across these examples, a validation interface should return the relevant finding and its supporting data, so that a developer can distinguish an invalid information boundary, a computation that uses the future, a deficient input series, and a poorly supported answer. These checks provide distinct feedback for revision. Task-specific scores then measure the usefulness of the accepted result. Together, the four temporal functions establish the data and evidence conditions under which the task procedures in the next section operate.

\section{Task-Specific Harness}
\label{sec:task-specific-harness}

Task-Specific Harness modules organize shared capabilities around a required output and objective. We classify tasks along two axes that need no new concepts. The first is the output mode, given by how the output is checked. Regression produces numerical values that are compared with observed values, and classification produces classes or class probabilities that are compared with observed classes \citep{hastie2009esl,bishop2006prml}. Generation produces new content, text or series, that has no single reference and is judged by its quality, fidelity, or use; decision making produces an action that is judged by its consequences. A probability does not change the mode: a predictive distribution of a continuous quantity is a probabilistic regression output, the probability of a discrete event is a classification output, and each is scored by its own proper scoring rules \citep{gneiting2007scoring,gneiting2011point}. The second axis is time. The output refers either to the record observed by the current time, $\mathcal{I}_\tau$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:temporal-availability}, or to quantities that become observable only after it. This is the classical division between interpolation or smoothing and extrapolation or prediction \citep{wiener1949extrapolation,kalman1960filtering}.

The two axes give eight cells (Table~\ref{tab:tsk-vocab}), and each cell contains the standard task names of time series analysis \citep{wu2023timesnet,wang2024tslib,kong2025timemqa}. Three rules place a module. A module enters every cell that its outputs occupy. Question answering is a format rather than a task: a question about the observed series with a single correct answer is classification, a free-text answer or explanation is generation, and a question about future values or events is forecasting. A method without an output of its own, such as representation or transfer learning, enters the cell of the downstream task on which it is evaluated.

Among the 162 works in scope, regression covers 15 works on the observed record and 74 after the current time, classification 65 and 14, generation 40 and none, and decision making none and 46; a work may occupy several cells. Classification is thus the second most common mode, and most of it concerns the observed record: anomaly detection, classification of series, root cause identification, and closed questions. Of the 84 works that forecast a value or an event, 76 report point forecasts and 18 probabilistic forecasts; some report both. Two cells are well defined but have no evaluated task output: generating futures under assumed conditions, and choosing after the fact the action that would have changed an observed outcome. Both appear in the reviewed systems only as internal mechanisms (Sections~\ref{sec:task-generation} and~\ref{sec:task-decision}). The temporal data, processing, context, and validation functions of Section~\ref{sec:general-ts-harness} supply reusable building blocks for every cell.

\subsection{Regression}
\label{sec:task-regression}
\label{sec:task-forecasting}

A regression output is a number, or a distribution over numbers, that is compared with values observed either already or after the horizon.

\noindent\textbf{Imputation and estimation.} Imputation appears mostly as one output of multi-task workflows: MAS4TS \citep{mas4ts2026} routes forecasting, classification, imputation, and anomaly detection requests to tool chains over a shared memory, and TESSA \citep{tessa2024} evaluates its series annotations through downstream forecasting and imputation. Estimation recovers a quantity that is not measured directly: the physiological Agent of \citet{physiologicalagent2025} orchestrates the retrieval of wearable PPG signals and a heart-rate estimation pipeline, and HydroAgent \citep{hydroagentcalibration2026} calibrates hydrologic model parameters through tool-mediated simulator episodes.

\noindent\textbf{Value forecasting.} A value forecast describes numerical values after the current time. Forecasting fixes an origin, target, horizon, and evaluation rule, and the Agent must choose how to build a forecast, which contextual evidence to use, and when to revise it. A point forecast reports one value per target time; a probabilistic forecast reports an interval, quantiles, a predictive distribution, or sample paths. The task contract determines the final object being optimized. A typical forecasting contract is
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{y}_{\tau+1:\tau+H}=f_\theta\!\left(\mathcal{I}_{\tau};H\right),
  \label{eq:forecast-contract}
\end{equation}
where $H$ is the declared horizon and $\mathcal{I}_{\tau}$ contains observed target history and covariates known at the origin, including future calendar information available under Eq.~\eqref{eq:temporal-availability}.

The reviewed procedures for value forecasting either select and combine numerical models or revise a forecast with context. TimeSeriesScientist \citep{timeseriesscientist2025} develops a numerical forecasting workflow in which the Curator's diagnostics guide the Planner's model search. For each candidate forecaster, the Planner tunes its configuration using validation MAPE:
\begin{equation}
  \theta_i^{\star}=\arg\min_{\theta_i\in\mathcal{C}_i}
  \operatorname{MAPE}_{\mathrm{val}}\!\left(m_i(\theta_i)\right),\qquad
  \operatorname{MAPE}_{\mathrm{val}}(m_i(\theta_i))=
  \frac{100\%}{|\widetilde{D}_{\mathrm{val}}|}
  \sum_{t\in\widetilde{D}_{\mathrm{val}}}
  \left|\frac{x_t-\widehat{x}^{(i,\theta_i)}_t}{x_t}\right|.
  \label{eq:tsci-planner}
\end{equation}
The selected configurations are ranked, and the Forecaster uses their validation results to choose a single model or an ensemble, fixing its strategy and weights before evaluating the test set. This links the temporal diagnostics in Section~\ref{sec:general-ts-harness} to a task-specific selection criterion. TimeCopilot \citep{timecopilot2025} likewise makes forecasting models callable components; the model library supplies numerical capabilities while the Agent organizes their use.

The second procedure revises a value forecast with context. FLAIRR-TS \citep{flairr2025}, Algorithm~1, refines the forecasting prompt through retrieved historical segments and observed forecast errors:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  S_{\mathrm{retr}}^{(k)}&=\operatorname{RetrieveSegments}(X_{\mathrm{HistDB}},X_{\mathrm{Ctx}},M),\\
  C_{\mathrm{aug}}^{(k)}&=\operatorname{AugmentContext}(X_{\mathrm{Ctx}},S_{\mathrm{retr}}^{(k)}),\\
  \widehat{X}^{(k)}&=F_{\mathrm{LLM}}(P_{\mathrm{curr}}^{(k)},C_{\mathrm{aug}}^{(k)},H),\\
  \operatorname{mae}^{(k)}&=\operatorname{MAE}(\widehat{X}^{(k)},X_V),\\
  (P_{\mathrm{next}}^{(k)},d^{(k)})&=R_{\mathrm{LLM}}\!\left(\{(P^{(i)},\operatorname{mae}^{(i)})\}_{i<k},P_{\mathrm{curr}}^{(k)},\tau_{\mathrm{stop}}\right).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:flairr-loop}
\end{equation}
Here $X_V$ is the observed target sequence for an $H$-step refinement window $V=(t_1,\ldots,t_H)$. The refiner proposes prompt updates from earlier prompts and errors; early stopping returns the current prompt, while reaching the iteration limit returns the lowest-MAE prompt. In deployment, this feedback becomes available when the refinement window has resolved.

CastFSR \citep{castfsr2026} separates fast numerical forecasting, slower contextual deliberation, and reflective evaluation. The initial prediction supplies a numerical prior; retrieved context then supports a candidate revision. Its state and action families make that division explicit:
\begin{equation}
  s_k=\Phi(I,M_k,\widehat{Y}^{\mathrm{prior}}_k,C_k),\qquad
  a_k\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid s_k),\quad a_k\in\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{fast}}\cup\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{slow}}\cup\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{reflect}}\cup\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{final}}.
  \label{eq:castfsr-state-action}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  C_k&=T_{\mathrm{ctx}}(D_{\mathrm{task}},D_{\mathrm{domain}},X_{1:L},H_{\mathrm{ctx}},\widehat{Y}^{\mathrm{prior}}_k,M_k;W_k),\\
  \widehat{Y}^{\mathrm{cand}}_k&=G_{\theta}(\widehat{Y}^{\mathrm{prior}}_k,C_k,M_k),\\
  E_k&=E_{\theta}(\widehat{Y}^{\mathrm{cand}}_k,\widehat{Y}^{\mathrm{prior}}_k,C_k,M_k),\qquad
  \widehat{Y}_{1:H}=F_{\theta}(\widehat{Y}^{\mathrm{cand}}_k,E_k,M_k).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:castfsr-revision}
\end{equation}
Here $I=(D_{\mathrm{task}},D_{\mathrm{domain}},X_{1:L},H_{\mathrm{ctx}})$ contains the target and horizon, domain description, observed series, and available contextual information; $M_k$ stores the interaction history and $W_k$ sets the context lookback. Reflection checks the candidate against temporal alignment, contextual support, and domain constraints such as units and capacity. Weak or redundant context can leave the numerical prior unchanged, whereas relevant evidence supports a targeted revision. Last-Mile Forecasting \citep{lastmile2026} and CastFlow \citep{castflow2026} provide related settings for contextual revision, while HydroAgent \citep{hydroagent2026} connects predictions to hydrological simulation and warnings.

SCENARIODIFF \citep{scenariodiff2026} uses a Historical Context Agent, a Scenario Agent, and an Anchor Guidance Agent to translate documents into historical summaries, a future scenario, and sparse anchor constraints. A diffusion forecaster generates the future trajectory conditioned on this information:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x^{(0)},t,\epsilon}
  \left\|x^{(0)}-x_{\theta}\!\left(x^{(t)},t,c\right)\right\|_2^2,
  \qquad
  x^{(t)}=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}x^{(0)}+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon,
  \label{eq:scenariodiff-denoising}
\end{equation}
Here $x^{(0)}$ is the future target horizon, $t$ indexes diffusion steps, $\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)$, and $c$ contains the numerical lookback, historical-text summary, and a scenario inferred from historical information. Anchor Blended Sampling edits anchor-relevant regions of the generated trajectory while retaining the remaining temporal structure. The generation step therefore implements probabilistic forecasting. It differs from the training series generation below in both its output and its evaluation target. Its trajectories are sampled from a generative model but scored against the realized future, so SCENARIODIFF is a probabilistic forecaster rather than a generation procedure.

\subsection{Classification}
\label{sec:task-classification}
\label{sec:task-analysis}
\label{sec:task-anomaly}

A classification output is a class or a class probability. On the observed record the reference lies in the data and its labels, so feedback can be computed during the analysis; for a future event it is available only after the outcome resolves.

\noindent\textbf{Classification and detection.} Classification Agents attach a label to a series or a window. FETA \citep{feta2025} decomposes a multivariate series by channel, retrieves exemplars by dynamic time warping, reasons over each channel, and aggregates the channel decisions by confidence. MarsTSC \citep{marstsc2026} instead builds a class knowledge bank from a few labeled examples and revises it through self-checks at test time.

\noindent\textbf{Anomaly detection and root cause identification.} Anomaly detection asks where a series departs from expected behavior; diagnosis adds an explanation of that departure and may lead to a response. These outputs require different evidence. An interval detector needs numerical and contextual support for the flagged region, while a service-diagnosis workflow also needs logs, incident context, and a mapping from symptoms to possible causes.

AnomaMind \citep{anomamind2026} develops the interval-detection workflow through coarse evidence acquisition, adaptive evidence construction, anomaly detection, and iterative refinement. At step $k$, the Agent maintains state $s_k$, selects action $a_k$, and receives numerical or knowledge evidence $o_k$. Its toolkit $B=\{K,N\}$ separates knowledge memory $K$ from numerical diagnostics $N$. The workflow updates the evidence state and applies a learned detector to the accumulated numerical and contextual evidence:
\begin{equation}
  s_{k+1}=E\!\left(s_k,a_k,o_k,\widehat{Y}^{(k)}\right),\qquad
  \widehat{y}_j=D_{\theta}\!\left(u_j,Z_{1:k},C_{1:k}\right),\quad \widehat{y}_j\in\{0,1\},
  \label{eq:anomamind-decision}
\end{equation}
where $u_j=[\ell_j,r_j]$ is a candidate interval and $s_k=(S^{(k)},Z_{1:k},C_{1:k})$ contains the candidate set, numerical diagnostics, and contextual knowledge. During detector training, rule-based feedback combines parse validity, agreement with labeled anomalous intervals, and a false positive penalty:
\begin{equation}
  r_k=\lambda_p r_k^{\mathrm{parse}}+\lambda_f r_k^{F1}-\lambda_{fp}r_k^{FP},\qquad
  \max_{\theta}\;\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{Y}^{(k)}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid s_k)}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{K}r_k\right].
  \label{eq:anomamind-reward}
\end{equation}
The reward supplies learning signals for a parseable and accurate interval decision. Figure~\ref{fig:anomamind-workflow} follows the four stages and shows how evidence acquisition and iterative revision support that output. The trained detector belongs to the LLM side; the surrounding evidence acquisition and refinement procedure supplies the anomaly specific Harness.

SAGE-AD \citep{sagead2026} uses a complementary form of evidence. It preserves the original series for precise tools and a compressed representation for the LLM. Family-specific analyzers and diagnostic visualizations feed a detector that returns anomaly intervals, confidence, and anomaly types. Its in-context reference database is constructed from normal training segments: representative subsequences are selected by clustering, then rule-based perturbations create normal--anomalous examples for nine anomaly types. This makes example retrieval part of the detection procedure, with evidence specific to the anomaly family guiding the interpretation of a suspicious interval.

\textit{Detection rules and executable pipelines.} ARGOS \citep{argos2025}, Sections~3.1--3.3, turns labeled temporal chunks into executable detection rules. A Detection Agent proposes rules, a Repair Agent executes them on dummy inputs to resolve implementation errors, and a Review Agent compares their accuracy on validation data. During deployment, the rules are combined with an existing detector. The learned artifact is a reusable detector program, whereas AnomaMind selects evidence and refines an interval decision within an analysis. Their evaluation must therefore account for when rule construction occurs and which labels are available during that phase.

AD-AGENT \citep{adagent2025} constructs a pipeline from a natural-language request and a dataset. Its Processor extracts modality and supervision requirements, the Selector chooses a supported library and model, and the subsequent agents retrieve implementation information, generate code, and review execution. TSLib supplies its time series branch. This approach suits requests that require assembling an executable detector from existing libraries. Its Reviewer tests execution on synthetic samples; optional Evaluator and Optimizer agents assess labeled data and tune hyperparameters. For a deployed time series pipeline, executable-code success and detection performance on held-out series provide complementary checks. Across these methods, the central choice is whether to learn evidence acquisition, retrieve diagnostic examples, or construct a detection pipeline; the available supervision, evidence sources, and required output determine which procedure fits the scenario.

\textit{From detection to diagnosis.} LEMAD \citep{ji2025lemad} extends the output to service diagnosis in power-grid operations. Metric and log collectors send aligned streams through Kafka to a real-time analysis agent, which correlates the signals and produces alert summaries and preliminary hypotheses. An anomaly-detection agent further evaluates suspicious events using hybrid models and contextual reasoning, returning confidence, root cause localization, and recommendations. Its reported evaluation includes precision, recall, F1, and latency on service data. The construction lesson is that adding diagnosis changes the evidence inputs and final artifact: an interval label becomes one input to a broader incident-analysis workflow. Root cause identification selects the responsible component or fault type from candidates; TraceBench \citep{tracebench2026} evaluates such attribution in controlled simulated tasks.


\noindent\textbf{Closed question answering.} A question about the observed series with a single correct answer is a classification output. TS-Reasoner \citep{tsreasoner2026} decomposes a query into a workflow of temporal operators, executes it, and revises the plan from execution feedback, while TimeART \citep{timeart2026} trains a tool-using reasoner over atomic temporal tools. The evidence gate of TS-Agent, specific to the question intent, \citep{tsagent2025}, described in Section~\ref{sec:temporal-validation}, is the corresponding Task-Specific Harness check: the question type fixes which findings an answer must cite. Section~\ref{sec:temporal-qa} discusses how question answering is evaluated.

\noindent\textbf{Event forecasting.} Event forecasting specifies a future event label or probability as its output. TimeCAP \citep{timecap2025}, Section~3.3, first asks a frozen contextualizer to describe the input series, $s_x=A_C(x)$. A trained multimodal encoder then combines the series and its description to predict the event and construct a retrieval embedding:
\begin{equation}
  (\widehat{y}_{\mathrm{MM}},z)=E_{\phi}(x,s_x),\qquad
  \mathcal{S}=\{(s_{x_j},y_j):j\in\operatorname{NN}_k(z)\}.
  \label{eq:timecap-retrieval}
\end{equation}
The nearest neighbors are retrieved from the training set. Their descriptions and labels serve as in-context examples for the predictor LLM, which returns a discrete event class $\widehat c_{\mathrm{LLM}}$. This class is converted to a one-hot vector and combined with the encoder logits $\widehat y_{\mathrm{MM}}$, with $\lambda\in[0,1]$:
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{c}_{\mathrm{LLM}}=A_P(s_x,\mathcal{S}),\qquad
  \widehat{y}=\lambda\operatorname{onehot}(\widehat{c}_{\mathrm{LLM}})+(1-\lambda)\widehat{y}_{\mathrm{MM}}.
  \label{eq:timecap-dual-agent}
\end{equation}
The distinctive mechanism is the learned retrieval of relevant event examples and their use in complementary predictors. Here augmentation enriches the prediction context; the output contract is event prediction. AIA Forecaster \citep{aiaforecaster2025} combines search, supervisor aggregation, and calibration for judgmental event forecasts; AUDITFORECAST \citep{auditforecast2026} anchors predictions in a quantitative baseline and updates them in odds space. These methods require probability and outcome evaluation alongside evidence access and trace inspection. Their comparison is most informative when the target event, resolution time, and available evidence are fixed. Across forecasting workflows, a numerical trajectory calls for horizon-specific validation, while an event probability calls for an explicit resolution rule and calibration assessment. Retrieval, model selection, and forecast revision should be chosen according to the evidence available before that target resolves. This connects the Agent's analytical choices to the feedback that can actually guide them.

\subsection{Generation}
\label{sec:task-generation}

A generated output has no single reference: a description, an explanation, or a new series is judged by its quality, its fidelity, or its use.

\noindent\textbf{Description and explanation.} Open questions, reports, and diagnoses produce text about the observed series. Time-RA \citep{timera2025} defines a structured anomaly-diagnosis task and refines its reasoning data with feedback; LineupRL \citep{lineuprl2026} rewards a captioner when a frozen verifier identifies the described series among statistic-matched distractors; and Zephyrus-R1 \citep{zephyrusr12026} scores forecast-discussion reports with a grounding-gated rubric and claim entailment. Because no single reference exists, these procedures turn the check into a verifiable proxy: identification of the series, entailment of claims, or a rubric.

\noindent\textbf{Synthesis and augmentation.}\label{sec:task-synthesis} Synthesis and augmentation create new series for learning or analysis. Augmentation derives new series from existing ones, such as a transformed positive view; conditioned synthesis produces series from text, domain context, or a small support set, such as additional training series. Temporal instruction dialogues generated to train a language model are LLM-side training data (Section~\ref{sec:llm-side}). Evaluation depends on the use: preserving a useful representation and training an accurate downstream model require different checks.

MERIT \citep{merit2025} targets unsupervised time series representation learning. A retrieval agent selects relevant series from a candidate set; an augmentation agent uses those examples to choose transformations; and a review agent assesses the resulting positive views. Accepted samples enter a memory bank, while rejection sends the workflow back to retrieval for refinement. The retrieved neighbors and augmented views then support contrastive learning. The review acts on the generated examples during construction, while downstream experiments evaluate the learned representation. Figure~\ref{fig:merit-augmentation} follows these decisions through a schematic augmentation example and shows how the resulting positive set enters the representation learner.

BRIDGE \citep{bridge2025}, Section~3, defines text-controlled generation through
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{x}=f_{\theta}(\mathcal{D},l,z),
  \label{eq:bridge-generation}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{D}$ supplies the domain, $l$ describes desired temporal properties such as trends and periodicity, and $z$ is sampled noise. Its multi-agent preparation stage collects textual templates, evaluates the resulting descriptions through a text-conditioned forecasting task, and refines the templates using feedback. The generation stage combines the description with domain prototypes to condition a diffusion model. This separates control of the generated object's properties from construction of the textual conditions used to obtain it. The forecasting evaluator supplies feedback for template refinement; the final generation experiments examine the synthetic series.

MetaCaster \citep{metacaster2026} generates training series from a small support set and domain context, then trains lightweight forecasters on the generated data. Its Harness Proposer changes the generator's prompts and skills to improve the downstream forecasting result. The optimization objective compares forecasters trained on authentic and generated data:
\begin{equation}
  \theta^{\star}=\arg\min_{\theta}\;\mathbb{E}_{m,l}\,\delta\!\left(\omega(f_l^m),\omega(\bar f_l^m)\right),\qquad
  \bar D^m=\operatorname{MGAgent}(D_{\mathrm{sup}}^m,C^m),
  \label{eq:metacaster-objective}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \delta\!\left(\omega(f_l^m),\omega(\bar f_l^m)\right)=
  \max\!\left\{\frac{\omega(\bar f_l^m)-\omega(f_l^m)}{\omega(f_l^m)},0\right\}
  \label{eq:metacaster-hinge}
\end{equation}
Here $\theta$ denotes the generator's prompt and skills, and $\omega$ measures forecasting error on the evaluation split of a dataset in the Harness-optimization corpus. The hinge-style discrepancy penalizes a generated-data forecaster when it performs worse than its authentic-data counterpart. This feedback updates the generator; final generalization is assessed on separate in-domain and out-of-domain datasets. After optimization, the Harness Proposer is removed and the learned prompts and skills guide generation for a new support set. Generation and Harness optimization are coupled to the forecasting objective; the job scheduler and recovery mechanism remain separately reusable General Harness modules. This provides a concrete example of one paper contributing distinct modules at different layers.

For language-model supervision, ChatAD \citep{chatad2026} constructs temporal instruction dialogues through TSEvol; this LLM-side use of synthesis is discussed in Section~\ref{sec:llm-side}. Comparing these methods requires identifying the generated object and the learner or analysis it is intended to support.

Figure~\ref{fig:merit-augmentation} makes the design choice explicit: retrieval supplies examples, augmentation constructs positive views, and review determines which views enter contrastive learning. This workflow suits representation learning, where the generated views must preserve useful signal structure. MetaCaster couples generation to downstream forecast error, making forecaster performance the criterion for revising the generator. Selecting a synthesis workflow therefore starts with the intended learner and the feedback available to improve its training data.


\noindent\textbf{Projection.} The cell after the current time is empty as a task output. A projection generates the future under an assumed condition, such as an intervention or a stress scenario. Climate science distinguishes such scenario-dependent projections from predictions, because the assumed condition may never occur \citep{ipcc2021glossary}; a projection therefore cannot be scored against the realized future, and generated trajectories that are scored in that way, as in SCENARIODIFF, are probabilistic forecasts. In the reviewed systems, projections appear only inside decision procedures. EpiWorld \citep{epiworld2026} rolls out a policy-conditioned epidemic world model, AgriWorld \citep{agriworld2026} checks the causal direction of what-if interventions, and NeuroAgentic \citep{neuroagentic2026} perturbs a TimesFM forecast according to each proposed control command. The rollouts select an action and the action is evaluated; no reviewed work evaluates the projections themselves.

\subsection{Decision Making}
\label{sec:task-decision}

Decision making Agents select an action using predictive or diagnostic evidence. The action space and feedback determine the task-specific procedure: portfolio allocation acts through positions and returns, traffic control acts through signal configurations and queues, and maintenance support produces recommendations for review. The Agent must therefore represent both the temporal state and the consequences of the available actions. After the current time, the two tasks differ in who executes the action: 19 works recommend actions for review and 29 execute them.

\noindent\textbf{Hindsight decision.} The cell before the current time is also empty. A hindsight decision is the action that should have been taken at an earlier time, given the outcome that has since been observed; it can be checked by replaying the earlier state in a simulator or by counterfactual estimation \citep{pearl2009causality}. Online learning measures regret against the best action in hindsight \citep{cesabianchi2006prediction}, and hindsight relabeling turns observed outcomes into training goals \citep{andrychowicz2017her}. The reviewed Agents form hindsight judgments only to improve later decisions: EpiWorld distills counterfactual rollout feedback into regime-specific lessons, ForecastCompass \citep{forecastcompass2026} revises its memories from resolved questions, FinMem \citep{finmem2023} reflects on past trades, and TimeClaw-EEL \citep{timeclaw2026explore} compares alternative executed trajectories. None outputs the hindsight decision as a result to be evaluated.

\noindent\textbf{Advisory decision.} In an advisory decision, the Agent recommends an action that a person reviews and executes. AgentFM \citep{agentfm2025} chains detection, diagnosis, and mitigation for failures in distributed databases; AgenticTwin \citep{agentictwin2026} grounds its mitigation agent in retrieved knowledge; FinArena \citep{finarena2025} writes a financial report conditioned on the user's risk preference; and Smart Energy Agent \citep{smartenergyagent2026} reports appliance diagnoses with their reliability. Because a person takes the action, the procedure must expose the evidence and the risk behind each recommendation.

\noindent\textbf{Autonomous control.} In autonomous control, the Agent sets positions or control signals itself and observes their consequences. The context policy can itself be specialized for an action. FinMem \citep{finmem2023}, Section~3, separates financial memories into layers with different retention times and ranks events using recency, relevance, and importance. Significant investment memories can be promoted to deeper layers, and retrieved events condition a trading decision together with the current market state. The memory policy therefore depends on the useful lifetime of financial information and trading experience. A general retrieval module supplies storage and access; this task-specific policy decides what to retain and how it influences the action.

FinCon \citep{fincon2024} specifies financial analyst roles that deliver investment insights to a trading manager. Their output contracts include market evidence, stock selection, and risk feedback, making the implemented coordination procedure task-specific. Its prompt-controlled policy maximizes cumulative profit and loss,
\begin{equation}
  \max_{\theta}\;\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\alpha^t R_t^{\Pi_{\theta}}\right],
  \qquad R_t^{\Pi_{\theta}}=R(O_t,B_t,A_t),
  \label{eq:fincon-objective}
\end{equation}
where $\theta$ comprises agent prompts, $\alpha$ discounts future rewards, and $O_t$, $B_t$, and $A_t$ denote market observations, managerial reflection, and trading action. FinCon updates prompts between training episodes using comparative performance feedback; risk alerts within an episode also guide testing decisions. Its portfolio manager solves a mean--variance allocation subject to the buy/sell/hold direction selected by the agents:
\begin{equation}
  \max_{w}\;\langle w,\mu\rangle-\langle w,\Sigma w\rangle,\qquad
  w_n\in\begin{cases}[0,1],&\text{buy},\\\left[-1,0\right],&\text{sell},\\\{0\},&\text{hold},\end{cases}
  \quad n=1,\ldots,N.
  \label{eq:fincon-portfolio}
\end{equation}
Here $\mu$ and $\Sigma$ are shrinkage estimates of mean daily returns and their covariance. In FinCon's encoding, $w_n$ is portfolio exposure: buy permits a long position, sell permits a short position, and hold assigns zero exposure. The portfolio is rebalanced daily. The analyst--manager interaction supplies the directional evidence, and the allocation problem converts that evidence into a position under the method's risk objective. TradingAgents \citep{tradingagents2024} emphasizes a different decision procedure: analyst reports feed bullish and bearish research roles, whose debate precedes trading and risk deliberation. Both systems connect evidence aggregation to an action, while their coordination and acceptance procedures differ.

CoLLMLight \citep{collmllight2026} makes the prediction-to-action relation explicit in traffic control. Each intersection observes queue lengths, moving vehicles, waiting time, and occupancy at its own and neighboring lanes. A graph $G$ represents their connectivity, and a history window preserves the observations and signal configurations that preceded the current decision:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{T}_t=\{(O_{t_i},a_{t_i}):t-\Delta t<t_i<t\},\qquad
  X=\operatorname{Prompt}(O_t,G,\mathcal{T}_t,D).
  \label{eq:collmlight-state}
\end{equation}
For complex coordination, the LLM analyzes current traffic, predicts neighboring states under candidate configurations, and selects a signal:
\begin{equation}
  Y_{\mathrm{ana}},\{\widehat{O}^{a}_{t+1}:a\in\mathcal{A}\},\widehat{a}=f_{\mathrm{LLM}}(X).
  \label{eq:collmlight-action}
\end{equation}
Here $D$ supplies the control instructions. Simpler situations omit some reasoning stages according to the number of critical neighboring lanes; simulation-derived data support policy fine-tuning. The resulting action changes future traffic observations, so evaluation measures policy outcomes in the control environment. Portfolio and traffic applications both require reasoning about future outcomes; each instantiates its own state, feasible actions, feedback timing, and utility. A decision making Harness must connect these quantities to an executable policy and measure the consequences of that policy over time.

Decision procedures also differ in how the action is produced. Most reviewed Agents map the current evidence to an action directly. Others first project the consequences of candidate actions and then choose among them, as EpiWorld, AgriWorld, and NeuroAgentic do. This is model-based planning in the sense of reinforcement learning \citep{sutton2018rl}, and it is where the empty projection cell is used.

A new decision making Agent should specify these quantities before choosing its reasoning workflow. The numerical or language prediction supplies evidence; the action contract determines how that evidence becomes a position, a control signal, or a recommendation. Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} connects each resulting artifact to an appropriate evaluation protocol.

The comparison yields five observations. First, fixed pipelines are reported where the candidate operations and acceptance criterion are known before execution, as in model-library forecasting, deployment-time series generation, and trading role hierarchies; feedback-driven control appears where the required evidence emerges during analysis, as in interval refinement, context-dependent forecast revision, and event search. Second, execution errors, parse checks, held-out labels, and simulator rollouts are available while a system is constructed (ARGOS, AnomaMind, TimeClaw-Harness, CoLLMLight), whereas FLAIRR-TS, AIA Forecaster, and FinCon depend on errors, resolutions, or returns that exist only after the target is realized, which limits how often they can revise in deployment. Third, evidence for memory is largely within distribution: TimeClaw-Harness and AnomaMind build memory from training splits of the evaluated benchmarks, FinCon updates beliefs across episodes on the same assets, and among these systems only MetaCaster evaluates reused learned artifacts on out-of-domain datasets. Fourth, system gains are rarely separable from model scale, tool access, and compute. Several comparisons set a tool-equipped Agent against a prompted LLM without tools, and backbone sweeps change the model without fixing tools or token budget. In the matched GPT-5-nano comparison of TimeClaw-Harness, TimeSeriesScientist and ReAct reach similar average accuracy (40.5\% and 40.8\%), so the margin of TimeClaw-Harness co-varies with its native tools and memory rather than isolating dynamic control. Fifth, results are comparable only within shared protocols such as ETT forecasting or the KPI and Yahoo anomaly benchmarks; even there, slicing, windowing, metric variants such as point-adjusted event F1, and the position of the LLM knowledge cutoff relative to the test period differ across papers.

Together with its faster growth, this indicates that time series modules are consolidating around tools and data management, while orchestration, validation, and the improvement of the Harness itself remain task-bound. The same holds more strongly for tested solutions to time series requirements, as the counts below show. Agent-agnostic workbenches carry this consolidation further: TSFLab \citep{tsflab2026} moves forecasting knowledge, temporal data protocols, and experiment execution out of individual Agents into shared infrastructure that any compatible coding Agent can use and that maintains itself. \noindent\textbf{Placement.} Each module of the work is assigned to one term with the rule of Table~\ref{tab:layer-rules} and labeled with that term's vocabulary (Table~\ref{tab:term-basis}): a mechanism dimension for the General Harness, a temporal function for the time series modules, and a cell of the task grid for task modules. The result is a set of cells, such as a General Time Series Harness validation module used for forecasting, rather than a single label for the paper.

\noindent\textbf{Scope check.} A work states the scope of its mechanisms, and the assignment rule tests that statement. Two errors are possible. A general mechanism can be presented as task-specific: a prompt optimizer, an experience-distillation loop, or a feedback loop is described and evaluated on one task, although its logic only takes the task metric as an input, as in ATLAS \citep{atlas2025}, TimeClaw-EEL \citep{timeclaw2026explore}, and TS-Harness \citep{tsharness2026}. Such a module belongs to the General Harness and can be carried to another task. Conversely, a task-specific procedure can be presented as general: AQuA's manager and specialist pipeline \citep{aqua2026} reads as a general multi-agent runtime, but its roles and plan are built for factor research on forward returns, so it is a Task-Specific Harness module. Reuse evidence shows how often generality is claimed rather than shown: a General Harness or General Time Series Harness module has demonstrated reuse only when its source applies it to tasks in at least two cells of the task grid, which holds for 95 of 189 such modules; the generality of the other 94 is inferred from their interfaces.

\noindent\textbf{Size of the contribution.} Once placed and checked, a work adds to the map in one of four ways, listed from the most to the least common. It can automate an existing analysis, combining the LLM and the General Harness with existing time series tools so that the analysis runs without an analyst; 136 of the 162 works do this for at least one task. It can add another implementation to an occupied cell, as most task-specific processing modules do (108 such modules). It can fill an empty or thin cell. Two task cells, projection and hindsight decision, have no task output at all; no General Time Series Harness module optimizes the Harness for decision making; and decision making has seven shared validation modules, of which three have demonstrated reuse. Finally, it can consolidate: move a solution that earlier works rebuilt inside task modules into a General Time Series Harness module and show that it works for a task in a second cell. A contribution of each kind counts only when the evaluation tests the time series requirement the module addresses, for example an availability check evaluated with and without leakage control, a memory evaluated under a regime change, or an action evaluated by its consequences. Of the 143 works whose main contribution is not a benchmark or dataset, 70 report such a test, and of the 181 links between a tested requirement and the module that meets it, 108 point to Task-Specific Harness modules and 26 to General Time Series Harness modules. Consolidation is therefore the rarest and most valuable contribution, and Section~\ref{sec:open-problems} takes the untested and unconsolidated requirements as its agenda.


\section{Infrastructure and Benchmarks for Time Series Agents}
\label{sec:evaluation}

Evaluation completes the construction of a Time Series Agent: the benchmark must expose the information, actions, and output requirements that the deployed workflow will encounter. AION \citep{aion2026}, Section~3.1, expresses this connection through a task file $\phi$, an accessible workspace $\mathcal{W}$, and a validator $\mathcal{V}$:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{T}=(\phi,\mathcal{W},\mathcal{V}),\qquad
  \phi=(\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{access}},\Sigma,\mathcal{O},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{A}),\qquad
  (g,s)=\mathcal{V}(o).
  \label{eq:aion-task-contract}
\end{equation}
The task file specifies access, prior knowledge, output form, constraints, and metrics. The validator returns validity checks $g$ and scores $s$ for candidate output $o$. This separation lets an evaluator identify both an incorrectly executed analysis and a valid analysis with poor predictive performance. The reviewed benchmarks instantiate these requirements in different ways: some grade submitted code, others test evidence discovery, and others evaluate a sequence of analytical turns or recurring data releases.

\subsection{Evaluating each term}


The unit of evaluation is an episode with a declared information boundary. Its record includes the request, visible data, tool calls, intermediate artifacts, validator decisions, revisions, final output, and resource use. Each term of Eq.~\eqref{eq:tsa-formula} raises one question about this record. Did the General Harness execute the intended operations and handle failures? Did the temporal modules preserve the data window, numerical meaning, and information availability? Did the task module produce an accurate forecast, useful synthetic sample, or supported diagnosis, and does the output improve the decision that uses it? The LLM term is evaluated through the same record, since its contribution appears only in the actions it selects.

The four evaluation criteria answer these questions: process validity, temporal validity, task performance, and decision utility. The Paper Cards show how unevenly they are reported. Of the 162 works in scope, 154 report task performance, 104 report some evidence of temporal validity, 66 report process validity, and 32 report decision utility. Even in decision making, decision utility appears in only 30 of the 46 works. Testing a time series requirement means measuring the matching criterion under a controlled change: temporal validity for information availability and regime change, process validity for verifiable conclusions, and decision utility for the consequences of an action. A temporal-validity entry is usually a chronological split, which shows that the evaluation respects time but does not show that the Agent would detect or survive a violation.

These questions require different observations. A tool-call schema check establishes that a requested operation is well formed; running the tool establishes that it executes; comparing its output against a reference establishes numerical correctness. TSAG \citep{tsag2026}, for example, evaluates invocation over hard-coded tool stubs. That protocol isolates tool selection and argument formation. TimeSeriesGym \citep{timeseriesgym2025} instead evaluates artifacts produced in an executable environment, so errors in code, data handling, and submission format affect the outcome. The choice between these protocols follows the capability being tested.

Figure~\ref{fig:evaluation-stack} follows an episode from its task contract and accessible resources through execution to the submitted artifact, and separates the three records needed to interpret its outcome. An inaccurate forecast can arise from a weak predictor, a failed tool call, or incorrectly aligned input; preserving the execution trace distinguishes these causes. Resource measurements add the cost of reaching the result and of recovering from failures.

\subsection{Existing benchmark landscape}

TimeSeriesGym \citep{timeseriesgym2025} evaluates time series ML engineering through 34 challenges from 23 sources, spanning eight temporal problem types. A challenge combines resources such as data, code, papers, and documentation with a task description and a grading function. The Agent must produce a usable artifact within the execution budget. The reported protocol uses a Docker environment and default limits of four hours and 50 steps, and distinguishes valid and reasonable submissions from task-native performance. This makes it suitable for a system expected to inspect a repository, adapt a model, and deliver runnable analysis. Figure~\ref{fig:benchmark-landscape} summarizes the resources-to-artifacts path; the grader closes the loop between the requested engineering task and the submitted result.

TimeSage-MT \citep{timesagemt2026} examines multi-turn analytical continuity. Its 240 tasks span four levels and eight domains; each task includes a visible series slice, dialogue, analysis trajectory, reference code, numerical ground truth, and output requirements. Outcome scoring measures whether the requested result is correct, while capability diagnostics examine the steps used to obtain it. Its reference system combines an LLM Planner and Reporter with deterministic validation, profiling, execution, and evaluation, and exposes 226 analytical skills across 36 categories. It therefore supports comparisons between model backbones and between runtime configurations under the same temporal workflow.

TimeSage-EV \citep{timesageev2026} adds a second timescale: the analysis recurs as new evidence becomes available. The reported snapshot contains 60 scenarios across six domains and 1,485 scenario-period instances spanning February 2023 to May 2026. At each period the Agent receives the permitted evidence and produces structured answers and reports, while the next target release is withheld for evaluation. The benchmark measures keypoint accuracy and coverage together with report faithfulness and quality, and compares independent with sequential contexts. A system intended for recurring economic or operational monitoring can thus be tested for its use of accumulated context as well as its current answer.

Contextual forecasting benchmarks isolate the value of external information. Dr-CiK \citep{drcik2026} provides 240 forecasting tasks and 8,849 documents with supporting evidence, multi-hop dependencies, and distractors, and it shifts dates and anonymizes entities so that the targets are not covered by current model training cutoffs. Its evaluation separates research and evidence recovery from forecast quality, allowing a designer to investigate whether a failure occurred during retrieval or after the relevant context was found. TemporalBench \citep{temporalbench2026} varies historical and future partitions and the availability of contextual or event information across four temporal understanding and forecasting settings. These controlled conditions help test when context changes a numerical or language-facing prediction.

TFRBench \citep{tfrbench2026} evaluates forecasting explanations as well as numerical forecasts on ten datasets across five domains. Its reference construction uses future events and outcomes to select explanatory trajectories; test-time systems receive historical inputs. An LLM judge compares candidate reasoning with those references along domain relevance, forecasting correctness, event relevance, and logic-to-number consistency, alongside numerical metrics such as MAE and MASE. The resulting signal measures agreement with outcome-conditioned reference reasoning. For deployment-oriented evaluation, that signal can be paired with an audit of the information actually available when the forecast was issued.

TraceBench \citep{tracebench2026} studies root cause attribution through controlled dynamical-system interventions. An Agent identifies which simulator parameter changed, with conditions varying noise, domain context, support examples, and direct versus programmatic submission. A submitted program can also be evaluated on held-out samples. The benchmark records accuracy together with tokens, cost, elapsed time, tool use, Python execution, and plotting behavior. It therefore exposes the trade-off between exploratory analysis and diagnostic generalization. TSQBench \citep{tsqbench2026} addresses a different prerequisite: selecting trustworthy temporal data. Controlled pairs are derived from a common template and degraded along specified quality dimensions, enabling comparisons of quality identification, pairwise judgment, and downstream data-selection utility.

\subsection{Temporal question answering}
\label{sec:temporal-qa}


Question answering tests whether a model or Agent can interpret temporal evidence in language. TSAQA \citep{tsaqa2026} covers six analytical tasks, including anomaly detection, characterization, comparison, and temporal relationships, using true/false, multiple-choice, and puzzling formats across thirteen domains. MMTS-Bench \citep{mmtsbench2026} contains 2,424 question--answer pairs in Base, InWild, Match, and Align subsets, testing structural perception, temporal reasoning, sequence matching, and cross-modal alignment. Such evaluations are useful when a user asks about a trend, a change, or an event visible in a series. In an operational Agent, the same question can initiate a tool workflow: inspect the series, calculate a statistic, retrieve relevant context, and return an answer tied to those observations.

Infrastructure determines which parts of an episode can be replayed. AION \citep{aion2026} specifies task files, workspaces, artifacts, and validation interfaces. TimeSeriesGym \citep{timeseriesgym2025} packages challenge resources and graders into executable environments. TimeClaw-Harness \citep{timeclaw2026harness} keeps numerical series in a workspace and exposes executable analytical tools, trajectories, and memory. These choices make different boundaries explicit: the task request, the execution environment, and the persistent analytical state.

TSFLab \citep{tsflab2026} provides such infrastructure as a workbench for forecasting research. Compatible coding Agents share layered knowledge cards for 323 methods, 93 datasets, and 71 reusable model units; a runner that executes declared experiments under one data, training, and evaluation protocol; and an improver that verifies new methods against their papers and code before merging them. In paper reproduction and benchmarking, the workbench raised task completion by 20--163\% and reduced fresh tokens per completed task by 11--66\% relative to Agents without it. In a new Agent system, such a workbench can supply the numerical tool layer, while the Agent adds evidence retrieval, model selection, revision, and reporting. Recording the dataset version, model configuration, tool inputs and outputs, and resulting artifacts makes these choices comparable across runs. \subsection{Selecting a benchmark for a new scenario}

Resource selection follows the same task--function grid as system construction. Begin with the required artifact and the mechanism whose contribution is being tested. A numerical model library supplies callable forecasting capabilities; an execution environment establishes whether the Agent can deliver a working analysis; a controlled context benchmark measures how external evidence changes the result. Each answers a different development or evaluation question.

For the demand planning example in Figure~\ref{fig:task-function-grid}, TSFLab \citep{tsflab2026} supplies model and dataset routines for the numerical tool layer. A direct forecasting run provides a baseline for the Agent's selection or revision procedure. Dr-CiK \citep{drcik2026} provides a controlled test of evidence retrieval and contextual prediction, including supporting and distracting documents. TimeSeriesGym \citep{timeseriesgym2025} tests whether the Agent can construct the required executable artifacts under a challenge's grading protocol. An inventory simulator then evaluates how the forecasts affect stockouts, holding costs, and feasible actions under the chosen operational assumptions. These evaluations isolate numerical prediction, evidence use, execution, and downstream utility within the same proposed workflow.

Other task contracts lead to different combinations. TimeSage-MT \citep{timesagemt2026} tests continuity across analytical turns, and TimeSage-EV \citep{timesageev2026} tests revision and capability reuse across evidence releases. TraceBench \citep{tracebench2026} supplies interventions with known causes for diagnostic exploration. TSQBench \citep{tsqbench2026} tests dimension-specific data quality comparisons before downstream analysis. Selecting a resource by the capability it exposes makes the result interpretable: a quality judgment, a runnable program, a forecast, and a diagnosis are distinct artifacts with distinct evidence requirements.

Generation and decision making require evaluation through the use of the resulting data or actions. MERIT \citep{merit2025} evaluates representations learned from generated positive views on downstream temporal tasks, whereas MetaCaster \citep{metacaster2026} evaluates forecasters trained on synthetic series through forecasting error. The intended learner therefore determines the synthesis evaluation, with separate data for revising the generator and assessing its result. FinCon \citep{fincon2024} evaluates trading and portfolio decisions using cumulative return, Sharpe ratio, and maximum drawdown; CoLLMLight \citep{collmllight2026} evaluates traffic-signal decisions in CityFlow through average travel and waiting times. For a new application, these examples guide the choice of action space, outcome measure, and feedback schedule.

A module comparison should preserve the backbone, accessible tools, information boundary, and resource budget while changing the module under investigation. The record should connect the input, intermediate artifacts, checks, and final output, so that an improvement can be attributed to processing, retrieval, validation, or the task procedure. A repository supplies the means of implementing the comparison; a benchmark supplies a declared protocol for judging it.
\section{Open Problems}\label{sec:open-problems}


The assessment in Section~\ref{sec:task-impact} suggests what counts as an open problem. Agents already automate most analytical workflows, and better scores on existing protocols show that a workflow runs well; they do not show that a time series requirement is met. The open problems below are the requirements that current works name but rarely test, or test only inside one task module: verifiable conclusions (claimed in 49 work--task pairs, tested in 14), information availability (claimed in 20, controlled only by the test split in 12, tested in 9), behavior under changing regimes, decision consequences beyond trading and traffic, and operation in deployment (tested in 4). Each problem sits at one term of Eq.~\eqref{eq:tsa-formula}: the division of labor between the LLM and the Harness, shared context and memory, the move of task solutions into shared modules, the temporal and process validity of evaluation, the decision utility of task outputs, and the cells of the task grid that current systems do not fill. Each is stated with the controlled comparison that would count as a solution. A gain of a few points in accuracy or speed does not count.

\noindent\textbf{Dividing temporal capabilities between the model and the Harness.} ChatTS \citep{chatts2025} learns to interpret temporal attributes and language instructions, while tool-based systems such as TS-Agent \citep{tsagent2025} explicitly compute analytical evidence. Their combination raises a concrete allocation problem: which judgments should be handled by learned temporal perception, and which should trigger a numerical tool? Matched-backbone experiments can vary the tool policy and compare numerical error, evidence quality, and execution cost. This would identify where model adaptation reduces tool dependence and where explicit computation remains valuable. The allocation also determines verifiability: a computed statistic can be checked, whereas a perceived pattern must be validated against evidence. This matters most in anomaly diagnosis, where only 9 of the 42 works with an anomaly task test such a requirement. The question becomes sharper as time series move toward a native modality of omni-modal LLMs (Section~\ref{sec:llm-native}): perception can move into the model, while exact statistics, calibrated distributions, and availability checks should remain auditable tools. A controlled comparison would hold the Harness fixed, replace tool-based series views with native perception, and measure which outputs change and which can still be verified.

\noindent\textbf{Memory under changing information.} TimeSage-MT \citep{timesagemt2026} tests continuity within a dialogue, whereas TimeSage-EV \citep{timesageev2026} tests recurring analysis as releases accumulate. These settings distinguish remembering a useful result from deciding whether it is still applicable. Future memory evaluations can preserve an old explanation while changing its supporting observation, release vintage, or regime, then test whether the Agent revalidates, revises, or retires that explanation. This links memory quality to the temporal validity of later actions. Memory also needs a stable record of what it was built from: AION \citep{aion2026} connects the task file, workspace, and validator, and TimeSage-EV \citep{timesageev2026} makes evidence release time part of the recurring analysis. Replaying an earlier analysis after a later release has arrived tests whether the original information boundary is preserved. Nonstationarity is tested in 24 work--task pairs, mostly as held-out domains or regimes for the whole system. No test isolates whether retained context was revalidated when the regime changed.

\noindent\textbf{Transferring learned procedures.} TimeClaw-Harness \citep{timeclaw2026harness} promotes generated tools using held-out execution, and TimeClaw-EEL \citep{timeclaw2026explore} distills successful trajectories into external experience. The remaining question is how far each acquired capability transfers. Evaluation can hold out a task cell, a domain, or a temporal regime while keeping the base LLM fixed. Recording tool identity, training episodes, and promotion criteria makes it possible to distinguish reusable procedure learning from repeated adaptation to similar instances. TimeSage-EV \citep{timesageev2026} provides a concrete availability rule: a skill is activated only from the next period. Transfer concerns solutions as well as procedures. Of the modules that meet a tested requirement, 108 of 181 are task modules, so a leakage guard or a shift-robust memory that works in one system is usually rebuilt in the next. Moving such a solution into a General Time Series Harness module, and showing that it still works for a task in a second cell under changes in sampling frequency, units, and data availability, would turn a single result into shared infrastructure. TimeClaw-Harness \citep{timeclaw2026harness} and the skill library of TimeSage-MT \citep{timesagemt2026} provide shared tool layers on which such transfer can be tested.

\noindent\textbf{Evidence-sensitive evaluation.} Dr-CiK \citep{drcik2026} controls supporting and distracting documents, TraceBench \citep{tracebench2026} controls diagnostic interventions, and TFRBench \citep{tfrbench2026} scores reasoning against outcome-conditioned references. These protocols address different relationships between evidence and result. Combining controlled evidence changes with an executable trace can test whether a correct answer was supported by information available to the Agent and whether removing a relevant observation changes the conclusion appropriately. Such tests would make reasoning evaluation more directly connected to the operations that produced the answer. The same design applies to information availability. Most works enforce a cutoff by construction or assume one, and only nine compare a system with and without leakage control or measure foreknowledge directly \citep{openforecaster2026,tiem2026,aiaforecaster2025}. Injecting post-cutoff evidence and testing whether the Agent detects or ignores it would make temporal validity a measured property of the Agent rather than of the split.

\noindent\textbf{Closing the prediction-to-decision loop.} Forecast quality matters through its use. FinCon \citep{fincon2024} couples analytical evidence to portfolio positions, and CoLLMLight \citep{collmllight2026} connects local traffic observations to network control. These settings introduce action costs, risk, delayed feedback, and effects on subsequent observations. Evaluations should compare the complete decision procedure under the same information and action constraints, recording both predictive performance and the resulting utility. A method that changes recommendations also needs evaluation across repeated decisions, where its own actions can alter the future data distribution. Decision consequences are tested in 15 works, almost all in trading or traffic control, and decision utility is reported by only 30 of the 47 decision-support works. Operational deployment, the strongest evidence that an Agent solves a practical problem, appears in four works \citep{trafficr12025,steward2026,epicoach2026,finanalyst2026}. Domains such as energy, maintenance, healthcare, and supply chains need the same evidence.

\noindent\textbf{Projection as an evaluated output.} Generating futures under assumed conditions is a well-defined task with no evaluated instance in the corpus (Section~\ref{sec:task-generation}); EpiWorld \citep{epiworld2026}, AgriWorld \citep{agriworld2026}, and NeuroAgentic \citep{neuroagentic2026} use projections only to choose actions. Because a projection cannot be scored against the realized future, its evaluation needs other references: consistency with a simulator or a known mechanism, coverage of the scenarios that matter for the decision, and agreement with interventions that were later carried out. An environment in which the same state can be rolled out under several actions would make projection a testable output.

\noindent\textbf{Hindsight decisions as evaluated outputs.} Choosing the action that should have been taken at an earlier time is the other empty cell. Agents form such judgments in reflection and lesson memories, as in EpiWorld, ForecastCompass \citep{forecastcompass2026}, FinMem \citep{finmem2023}, and TimeClaw-EEL \citep{timeclaw2026explore}, but never output them for evaluation. Replaying the earlier state in a simulator and executing the proposed action gives a direct check, and regret against the best action in hindsight gives a summary measure \citep{cesabianchi2006prediction}. Evaluating the hindsight judgment separately would also show whether the lessons that drive later decisions are correct.

\noindent\textbf{Probabilistic outputs.} Only 18 of the 84 works that forecast a value or an event report a probabilistic forecast; the others report point forecasts. A downstream decision under risk needs a calibrated distribution rather than a single value, and proper scoring rules evaluate it \citep{gneiting2007scoring}. Agents that revise forecasts with context, such as Last-Mile \citep{lastmile2026} and CastFSR \citep{castfsr2026}, could be compared on the calibration of the revised distribution as well as on its point error.

Changing the information release, task setting, evidence, or output criterion exposes a different aspect of reuse and reliability. The three design analyses below isolate complementary choices along that path: the numerical input interface, retained execution experience, and the forecasting substrate.


\subsection{Design analyses}

\noindent\textbf{ChatTS: selecting a temporal interface.} ChatTS \citep{chatts2025} makes the numerical input interface a design variable. An analytical assistant can compare direct text serialization with a learned temporal encoder while keeping its runtime, tools, and task inputs fixed. Preserving scale metadata in both conditions allows the comparison to examine magnitude-sensitive questions as well as temporal shape. Answer quality, evidence grounding, and tool use then reveal how the input representation changes the analysis.

\noindent\textbf{TimeClaw-EEL: learning from executed trajectories.} TimeClaw-EEL \citep{timeclaw2026explore} develops an Explore--Compare--Distill--Reinject loop with a fixed underlying LLM. Alternative tool trajectories are executed, scored, and distilled into external experience. For task instance $x$, the method selects the best valid execution using the task loss:
\begin{equation}
  q(\pi_k;x)=-L_{c}(\widehat{y}_k,y^{\star}),\qquad
  \pi^{\star}(x)=\arg\max_{\pi_k\in\Pi_{\mathrm{valid}}(x)}q(\pi_k;x).
  \label{eq:timeclaw-compare}
\end{equation}
Here $\pi_k$ denotes an executed candidate trajectory, $L_{c}$ the loss for task type $c$, and $y^{\star}$ the exploration target. The scored comparison occurs during experience construction; later inference receives the retained experience. The comparison is summarized and cleaned, and the resulting evidence updates the task-scoped experience state $A_s$:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  n_x&=\operatorname{Summarize}\!\left(x,\Pi(x),\{q(\pi_k;x)\}_{k=1}^{K},\pi^{\star}(x)\right),\\
  e_x&=\operatorname{Clean}(n_x),\qquad
  A_s^{(m+1)}=\operatorname{Distill}\!\left(A_s^{(m)},e_x\right).
\end{aligned}
  \label{eq:timeclaw-distill}
\end{equation}
The experience-update mechanism can use different task losses and is recorded as General Harness. Individual analytical operations retain their temporal or task-specific scopes. Comparing the initial and updated experience states on held-out episodes, with the backbone and tool access fixed, measures the contribution of retained experience. Separate tests across new instances, domains, and task types establish the scope over which that content remains useful.

\noindent\textbf{TSFLab: a shared workbench for forecasting research.} Most Time Series Agents place domain support in their own Harness, so it serves one Agent and must be rebuilt for the next. TSFLab \citep{tsflab2026}, the first workbench dedicated to time series forecasting research among the works we review, moves this support outside the Agent. A forecasting guide supplies workflows, evaluation settings, and known pitfalls; knowledge cards describe methods, reusable units, and datasets at summary, card, and source levels; a runner executes declared experiments under shared protocols; and a recursive improver merges verified methods, data, and fixes after usability, complexity, and consistency checks. Because the workbench is independent of any particular Agent, newer coding Agents benefit without modification, and the output of one task, such as a verified reproduction, becomes a resource for the next. Holding the numerical substrate fixed also makes Agent comparisons informative: a direct forecasting run and an Agent-controlled run can share the same model routines, data windows, and computational budget, so differences reflect the selection procedure and its execution cost.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This survey reviews Time Series Agents through one formula: an LLM, a General Harness, and a Time Series Harness that consists of a General Time Series Harness and a Task-Specific Harness. The LLM is the caller of the Harness, and time series foundation models are tools within it. Every reviewed Harness module is assigned to one term by its scope of reuse and labeled with a vocabulary adopted from prior work or derived from the term's definition: five mechanism dimensions, four temporal functions of the availability record, and a task grid of four output modes on the two sides of the current time. The assignment supports three design judgments for a new scenario: which runtime mechanisms can be retained, which operations require temporal semantics such as availability time and chronology guards, and which procedures must be rebuilt around the task objective, and the task grid turns these judgments into reference modules for each cell. Evaluation follows the same terms and separates process validity, temporal validity, task performance, and decision utility. Applied to the reviewed works, the map shows that most of them automate an analysis, that tested solutions to time series requirements appear in fewer than half of the works that propose methods and are mostly built inside task modules, that projection and hindsight decision have no evaluated task output, and that verifiable diagnosis, measured information availability, robustness to regime change, and evidence from deployment remain largely claimed. Progress should therefore be judged by the requirements a work meets under controlled tests and by whether its solutions move from task modules into shared time series modules. The central design judgment is that the three parts must match: the capabilities of the LLM must be supported by temporal interfaces in the Harness, execution must respect the information boundary at the current time, and evaluation must separate whether a workflow ran correctly from whether its output is accurate and useful once acted on. Aligned in this way, Time Series Agents can be assembled from tested, reusable modules and compared on the requirements that matter for the decisions they support.
