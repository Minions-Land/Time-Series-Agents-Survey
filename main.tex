\abstract{Time series analysis supports forecasting, diagnosis, and decisions in changing environments. Time Series Agents are emerging to connect language-model reasoning, numerical analysis, and tool execution within the workflows that produce these outcomes. Building one for a new scenario raises a construction problem: a general Agent runtime can often be reused, while temporal state, information availability, and task objectives determine which mechanisms require adaptation or redesign. Existing surveys organize the field by model adaptation, reasoning mechanisms, or application tasks; we complement these views with a module-level account of how such systems are composed. We review LLM-side capabilities, harness architectures, and evaluation through a \emph{from-general-to-specific} taxonomy based on the scope of module reuse. The framework distinguishes model-side contributions from General Harness, General Time Series Harness, and Task-Specific Harness modules, and links their composition to temporal requirements, task contracts, and evaluation protocols. We compare representative systems by the operations they perform, the assumptions they require, the feedback they consume, and the evidence used to evaluate them. The resulting map connects scenario requirements to module selection, system composition, and benchmark choice, and identifies open problems in temporal grounding, experience transfer, and downstream decision evaluation. Structured source-linked records support continued inspection and extension of the taxonomy.}

\section{Introduction}

Time series analysis supports decisions in environments that change as new observations arrive. Demand planning uses forecasts to set inventory, equipment monitoring uses anomalies to schedule maintenance, and traffic control uses current measurements to select actions \citep{lim2021deepts,collmllight2026}. In each setting, a numerical prediction or diagnostic result is one part of a longer workflow: the system must acquire data, inspect temporal context, call analytical tools, validate the result, and pass an admissible output to the next decision. Recent Agent systems make these steps executable through workspaces, evidence checks, and tool feedback \citep{tsagent2025,aion2026}.

The construction problem is therefore broader than adapting an LLM to a time series. A developer must decide which interaction, memory, planning, and execution mechanisms can be carried over from a general Agent; which operations need temporal semantics such as order, windows, units, and availability time; and which procedures must be redesigned around a task objective. General Harness research supplies reusable runtime mechanisms \citep{ning2026codeharness,lin2026agenticharness}, while temporal workspaces and contextual forecasting protocols constrain the information and numerical state that those mechanisms may use \citep{timeclaw2026explore,drcik2026}. The output contract then changes the required feedback: a forecast is judged at a future horizon, an anomaly workflow must localize and support a suspicious interval, and decision support must connect evidence to an action and its consequences \citep{chen2026tsagentsurvey}.

This survey organizes the field around that construction question. We use a \emph{from-general-to-specific} taxonomy based on module reuse: General Harness captures domain-independent runtime mechanisms, General Time Series Harness captures temporal capabilities shared across tasks, and Task-Specific Harness captures procedures whose outputs, objectives, or validation rules are bound to a target task. LLM-side contributions remain an independent part of the system because they change how observations are represented, interpreted, and converted into actions. The three parts of the title play complementary roles: LLM-side capabilities determine how temporal evidence is interpreted and actions are selected; harness architectures organize execution, state, and feedback; and evaluation establishes whether the resulting workflow is temporally valid and effective for its intended task. The taxonomy provides one route through the literature: identify the required output, locate the temporal functions it needs, select reusable runtime modules, and then evaluate the complete workflow under its information and action constraints.

\subsection{Position relative to existing surveys}

Time series surveys supply complementary accounts of predictive architectures, foundation models, LLM adaptation, and multimodal temporal modeling \citep{lim2021deepts,liang2024foundation,zhang2024llmts,jiang2024empowering,jiang2025multimodal}. Our construction question links these bodies of work: which general mechanisms remain reusable, which require temporal semantics, and which become tied to a particular task?

Recent surveys of LLM-driven time series analysis organize the literature through complementary perspectives. One distinguishes representation, model adaptation, workflow autonomy, and analytical tasks \citep{promptstoagents2026}; these axes are also specified in its author-maintained taxonomy.\footnote{Author-maintained survey taxonomy: \url{https://github.com/CoderPowerBeyond/Agent-Prompt-TS-Survey}.} Other reviews examine reasoning topologies, objectives, and mechanisms \citep{changsurvey2026}, or organize Agent methods by forecasting, synthesis, anomaly diagnosis, and decision support \citep{chen2026tsagentsurvey}. A prediction-focused synthesis relates standalone, tool-augmented, and hybrid architectures to training, evaluation, and applications \citep{xu2026forecastagentsurvey}. These organizing choices clarify different aspects of the field; our construction question concerns how their capabilities should be selected and combined for a particular scenario.

These surveys establish complementary views of model capabilities, reasoning, and applications. Task-oriented organization brings together methods that address similar outputs \citep{chen2026tsagentsurvey}, while model- and mechanism-oriented organization reveals shared technical choices \citep{promptstoagents2026,changsurvey2026}. System construction additionally requires tracing how those choices depend on temporal semantics and task objectives. Our taxonomy uses the scope of module reuse to connect these perspectives: it distinguishes the runtime mechanisms that can be carried across domains, the temporal capabilities shared across tasks, and the procedures whose objectives and feedback are task-bound. This distinction provides the organizing basis for comparing and selecting components throughout the survey.

Table~\ref{tab:direct-prior-comparison} compares the closest time series Agent surveys. All of them treat Agent runtime mechanisms systematically, and most treat LLM-side adaptation and evaluation in depth; the prediction-focused survey also treats information availability and leakage systematically \citep{xu2026forecastagentsurvey}. The surveys differ mainly in their organizing basis and unit of analysis. Prior surveys classify papers or systems by agent components \citep{promptstoagents2026}, reasoning topology and objective \citep{changsurvey2026}, task family \citep{chen2026tsagentsurvey}, or architecture type \citep{xu2026forecastagentsurvey}. This survey instead assigns each module of a work to a layer by its scope of reuse, so a single paper can contribute to several layers, and it links every classification to source passages in machine-readable records. Two prior surveys also maintain per-paper codes in public repositories \citep{promptstoagents2026,changsurvey2026}.

\subsection{A taxonomy from general to specific}

Our principal contribution is a module-level taxonomy based on the scope of reuse. \GeneralHarness{} organizes domain-independent interaction, memory, planning, tool execution, and feedback. \GeneralTSHarness{} introduces temporal semantics and operations that can serve different time series tasks. \TaskSpecificHarness{} specifies procedures whose outputs, objectives, or validation rules depend on the target task. LLM-side capabilities form an independent component that interacts with these layers. Existing approaches develop both action selection through tool feedback \citep{tsagent2025} and temporal interpretation through model adaptation \citep{jin2024timellm,chatts2025}; our taxonomy locates these capabilities alongside the runtime mechanisms they use. The three Harness layers describe a progression from a general runtime, through shared temporal capabilities, to a specialized task procedure.

The unit of classification is a concrete module. We examine the conditions on its inputs, state, operations, feedback, and outputs to assign it to the most specific applicable layer. A paper can contribute several modules at different layers. Synthesizing the Agent and Harness literature \citep{ning2026codeharness,lin2026agenticharness,changsurvey2026}, we use five mechanism dimensions---interface and interaction; memory and context; tools and execution; planning and orchestration; and optimization and evolution---to describe module functions across these layers. Shared temporal mechanisms are examined through data management, processing, context and memory, and temporal validation. Task-specific implementations are organized around forecasting and prediction, augmentation and synthesis, anomaly detection and diagnosis, and decision support, following the task grouping in prior time series Agent synthesis \citep{chen2026tsagentsurvey}; Section~\ref{sec:task-specific-harness} relates conventional tasks such as classification, imputation, and question answering to these families. These coordinates answer complementary questions: what result is required, what mechanism produces it, and under what conditions that mechanism can be reused. Figure~\ref{fig:task-function-grid} connects the task and temporal-function views developed in the subsequent chapters.

Figure~\ref{fig:survey-organization} maps the survey onto the construction process. Follow the arrows from the LLM side and reusable runtime to temporal contracts, task modules, evaluation, and open problems; the sequence mirrors the construction process of a Time Series Agent. \section{What Is a Time Series Agent?}

A Time Series Agent is a system in which an LLM operates through a reusable runtime and time series-specific modules.

\begin{tcolorbox}[
  colback=surveyWarn,
  colframe=surveyWarn,
  boxrule=0pt,
  arc=7pt,
  left=12pt,
  right=12pt,
  top=10pt,
  bottom=10pt
]
\[
\begin{aligned}
\textbf{LLM-based Agent} &\approx \text{LLM}+\text{Harness},\\
\textbf{Time Series Agent} &\approx \text{LLM}+\text{General Harness}+\text{Time Series Harness Module}.
\end{aligned}
\]
\end{tcolorbox}

The LLM interprets the request and current observations, chooses the next tool or environment action, and uses feedback to revise its plan. The General Harness supplies interaction, memory, tools, execution, control, planning, optimization, and coordination. The Time Series Harness Module preserves temporal state, operations, context, and validation; statistical models, time series foundation models, databases, simulators, retrieval systems, and evaluators operate as callable components within it.

At an abstract level, the runtime can be written as
\begin{equation}
  a_k = \pi_\theta(s_k), \qquad
  s_{k+1} = \mathcal{H}(s_k,a_k,o_{k+1}),
  \label{eq:agent-loop}
\end{equation}
where $s_k$ is the visible state at execution step $k$, $a_k$ is a tool or environment action, $o_{k+1}$ is the returned observation, and $\mathcal{H}$ is the Harness transition. Time Series Harness Modules add temporal type information to each state, action, and observation.

We describe each contribution using one set of coordinates: its LLM or Harness locus, its mechanism, and its task. LLM-side contributions change how the model represents temporal inputs, produces analytical responses, or selects actions from workflow feedback. A Harness module receives one scope: General Harness for domain-independent execution, General Time Series Harness for reusable temporal functionality, or Task-Specific Harness for a procedure tied to a task output or objective. The five mechanism dimensions in Table~\ref{tab:component-vocab} provide a common comparison vocabulary throughout the Harness chapters. Task labels describe the outputs supported by a module. Functional labels expose temporal data management, processing, context and memory, and validation within this same record. A paper may contribute several modules, and a module may have several mechanism or task labels while contributing one unit to its assigned layer.

Task, function, and scope answer different design questions. The task specifies the required result; the function identifies the problem a module solves; and the scope records the assumptions that constrain its reuse. The four temporal functions used in Section~\ref{sec:general-ts-harness} group concrete applications of these mechanism dimensions. A temporal-data interface, for example, combines interface and state mechanisms, while a temporal validator combines control, feedback, and executable checks. A module retains its scope when another task calls it; a new task-specific objective can instead motivate a distinct specialized module.

\paragraph{Scope of inclusion.} Inclusion in the corpus does not imply that every reviewed work is a complete Agent. We distinguish three roles. \emph{Model-side supporting techniques} supply temporal encoding, understanding, reasoning, or prediction, as in Time-LLM \citep{jin2024timellm} and ChatTS \citep{chatts2025}; they are reviewed for the capabilities they provide to the LLM side. \emph{Fixed workflows} execute a predetermined sequence of model and tool calls; they serve as components of larger systems and as reference points for adaptive control. \emph{Feedback-driven Agents} select or revise subsequent actions according to observations returned by tools or environments, as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:agent-loop}. Multiple roles, tool calls, or reasoning chains are not sufficient on their own for the third role; the defining condition is that returned feedback changes later actions.

\paragraph{Layer assignment.} Table~\ref{tab:layer-rules} states the assignment rule. The tests are applied from the most specific layer to the most general, and each module receives the first layer whose condition it meets. A layer describes the scope implied by a module's inputs, state, and assumptions. Demonstrated cross-task reuse is a separate claim that requires evaluation on more than one task or domain. Accordingly, a module is not task-specific merely because it first appears in a forecasting paper, and a generic interface does not by itself establish transfer.

The lower panel illustrates how to use the grid. A demand-planning Agent first needs an execution environment, aligned historical observations, and context available at the forecast origin. It then selects or revises a forecast and passes that result to an inventory procedure with explicit costs and action constraints. ModernTSF\footnote{\label{fn:moderntsf}ModernTSF repository: \url{https://github.com/Diaugeia/ModernTSF}.} can supply numerical forecasting components, Dr-CiK \citep{drcik2026} can test research with external context, and TimeSeriesGym \citep{timeseriesgym2025} can test the production of executable analytical artifacts. Inventory outcomes require a scenario environment with the relevant demand, lead-time, cost, and stock constraints. Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} develops this matching of requirements to resources.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Specify the scenario.} State the data sources, event and availability times, channels, units, missingness policy, and the decision or analysis that follows the output.
  \item \textbf{Write the task contract.} Define the output, horizon, admissible information, constraints, evaluation metric, and review authority.
  \item \textbf{Select the runtime.} Choose General Harness mechanisms for interaction, memory, tools, execution, control, planning, optimization, and coordination.
  \item \textbf{Add temporal semantics.} Introduce temporal state, operations, provenance, chronology guards, and validators wherever a generic runtime leaves the meaning ambiguous.
  \item \textbf{Instantiate and evaluate.} Add task-specific modules, then evaluate process validity, temporal validity, task performance, resource use, failures, revision, and downstream decision utility.
\end{enumerate}

This sequence is the practical meaning of the survey's hierarchy. The same runtime can support financial forecasting, traffic prediction, industrial diagnosis, or demand planning; the temporal and task contracts determine which modules and benchmarks must change.

\subsection{Temporal representation and alignment}

Time-LLM \citep{jin2024timellm} makes a frozen language backbone operate on temporal patches. It constructs a compact set of text prototypes $E'$ from the pretrained word-embedding space and uses the temporal patch embeddings as attention queries. For channel $i$ and attention head $k$, its reprogramming operation is
\begin{equation}
  Q_k^{(i)}=\widehat{X}^{(i)}_P W_k^Q,\quad
  K_k^{(i)}=E'W_k^K,\quad V_k^{(i)}=E'W_k^V,\qquad
  Z_k^{(i)}=\operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\frac{Q_k^{(i)}K_k^{(i)\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V_k^{(i)}.
  \label{eq:timellm-reprogramming}
\end{equation}
Here $d_k$ is the key dimension of attention head $k$. The text prototypes provide combinations of language-space cues for local temporal patterns. After aggregation and projection, the reprogrammed patches enter the frozen backbone alongside Prompt-as-Prefix context describing the task, domain, and input statistics. An output projection produces the forecast and is trained with the input transformations under prediction loss. LLM4TS \citep{chang2024llm4ts} likewise studies adapting a pretrained language model to temporal inputs, illustrating how the learned numerical interface can be developed before the Agent workflow is assembled.

ChatTS \citep{chatts2025} develops a conversational temporal interface. Fixed-size patches pass through a five-layer MLP into the text-embedding space and are inserted among the text tokens at their corresponding series positions. Its value-preserving normalization couples normalized patches with scale and offset information in the prompt. For a nonconstant series, the transformation and its reconstruction are
\begin{equation}
  x_t^{\mathrm{norm}}=\frac{x_t-x_{\min}}{x_{\max}-x_{\min}},\qquad
  x_t=(x_{\max}-x_{\min})x_t^{\mathrm{norm}}+x_{\min}.
  \label{eq:chatts-value-preserved}
\end{equation}
This mathematical restatement of the paper's normalization makes the role of the prompt metadata explicit: a statement about magnitude can refer to the original scale while the encoder processes normalized values. In an Agent, the input layer must preserve this correspondence between a numerical series and its language-facing description.

Agentic Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Time Series Analysis \citep{agenticrag2024} conditions specialized language models through differentiable prompt pools. Retrieved continuous prompt embeddings are concatenated with numerical inputs and passed through a learnable projection. The pattern store thus participates in model adaptation: its learned representation determines how historical temporal patterns condition the predictor. This choice suits model-facing temporal conditioning, whereas external episode retrieval preserves inspectable records for the running Agent.

For numerical forecasting, Time-LLM \citep{jin2024timellm} offers prototype-based reprogramming of a frozen backbone, whereas ChatTS \citep{chatts2025} learns a direct patch-to-language interface for conversational analysis. Equation~\ref{eq:timellm-reprogramming} identifies where numerical patches interact with language prototypes; Equation~\ref{eq:chatts-value-preserved} identifies the scale information that must accompany normalized inputs. The choice therefore depends on the required output: a numerical forecast head or answers to temporal questions. The Harness supplies window construction, channel and unit metadata, consistent preprocessing, and routing between the learned interface and downstream tools.

\subsection{Temporal instruction and reasoning}

A temporal encoder becomes useful for analysis through supervision that links signal structure to questions and explanations. ChatTS \citep{chatts2025} generates synthetic series with selected temporal attributes, constructs matching text, and evolves instructions for understanding and reasoning. Controlling the generated attributes supplies known temporal properties around which analytical instructions can be built.



\textit{Towards Time Series Reasoning with LLMs} \citep{chow2024tsreasoning} follows a related learning route through temporal encoding and reasoning supervision. Its encoder warm-up and subsequent language-model adaptation connect temporal perception to explicit reasoning traces. Time-R1 \citep{timer12025} specializes this idea for forecasting: supervised warm-up teaches structured reasoning and complete forecast outputs, followed by reinforcement fine-tuning with format, length, and numerical feedback. Its MSE-based reward maps normalized forecast error to a bounded signal,
\begin{equation}
  \gamma_{\mathrm{MSE}}=2\left(1-\frac{1}{1+e^{-2\operatorname{MSE}}}\right),
  \label{eq:timer1-reward}
\end{equation}
which equals one at zero error and approaches zero as error grows. This bounded transformation compresses differences among high-error forecasts; the normalization of predictions and targets consequently determines the effective sensitivity of the reward. Equation~\ref{eq:timer1-reward} illustrates an outcome-based training choice: forecast error supplies a continuous signal alongside format, output-length, and temporal-structure rewards. The accompanying GRIP optimizer uses nonuniform sampling and adaptive weighting over reasoning paths. Time-R1 \citep{timer12025} evaluates forecasting with MSE and MAE and ablates its reward components (Tables~2, 4, and 6); these experiments support comparisons of numerical performance and training choices. Explanation faithfulness requires an additional test of whether the stated reasoning reflects the evidence and computation that produced the forecast.

STRIDE \citep{stride2026} connects language reasoning to a separate numerical foundation model. A teacher constructs reference reasoning from training windows and their pseudo-future targets; a smaller language model learns this reasoning. Its last hidden states $H$ are mean-pooled and projected into a numerical-model-compatible reasoning vector $e_R$, which is fused with native time series embeddings $E_{\mathrm{TS}}$:
\begin{equation}
  h_R=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{i=1}^{L}H_i,\qquad
  e_R=h_R W_{\mathrm{proj}},\qquad
  E_{\mathrm{fused}}=e_R\oplus E_{\mathrm{TS}},\qquad
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{total}}=\alpha\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}+\beta\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Quantile}}.
  \label{eq:stride-fusion}
\end{equation}
The fusion operation uses sequence prefixing or initial-state substitution according to the forecaster architecture. The joint objective updates the numerical model and projection together with LoRA parameters in the language model. This design lets a language-derived reasoning representation influence continuous forecasts while retaining a dedicated numerical decoder.

The practical choice is between supervising temporal descriptions, rewarding completed forecasts, and coupling language representations to a numerical decoder. ChatTS \citep{chatts2025} supplies attribute-based supervision for temporal questions; Time-R1 \citep{timer12025} ties learning to forecast outcomes; STRIDE \citep{stride2026} retains a dedicated forecaster while injecting a learned reasoning representation. In Equation~\ref{eq:stride-fusion}, the language and quantile losses make this joint adaptation explicit. Their use requires different Harness support: preservation of series--text correspondence, parsing and chronological scoring of forecasts, or synchronization of language features with the numerical model's inputs.

Evaluation follows the capability being selected. Forecast accuracy concerns predicted values; explanation faithfulness concerns whether the explanation reflects the model's actual computation; evidence grounding concerns whether its claims are supported by the supplied series or documents; and action-selection quality concerns feasible, useful tool or environment actions. These distinctions motivate complementary checks: held-out numerical errors, controlled interventions on claimed reasoning factors, verification of cited observations and their availability times, and execution-based action evaluation. Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} connects these requirements to evaluation resources.

\subsection{Learning to operate temporal workflows}

Workflow training changes the model that selects or assesses actions. TSOrchestra, introduced in \textit{Conversational Time Series Foundation Models} \citep{conversationaltsfm2025}, trains a language-model judge for iterative ensemble optimization. A numerical optimizer proposes nonnegative weights over forecasting models that sum to one. The judge inspects metrics, weights, and optimization history to decide whether to continue or accept a configuration. Supervised fine-tuning uses real trajectories and augmented boundary cases; subsequent GRPO training incorporates held-out forecasting performance and explanation feedback. The trained judge controls numerical optimization by deciding when an ensemble configuration is ready to accept.

CastFSR \citep{castfsr2026} trains a broader policy over the Fast--Slow--Reflect workflow. Supervised distillation teaches executable stage sequences, followed by multi-turn reinforcement learning over complete trajectories. Training checks tool calls, stage order, timestamps, and completed forecasts before applying forecasting feedback. At inference, the policy still interacts with the numerical forecasting and contextual-retrieval tools described in Section~\ref{sec:task-specific-harness}. TimeART \citep{timeart2026} similarly combines a trained reasoner with an analytical toolkit, connecting policy learning to explicit temporal operations.

ChatAD \citep{chatad2026}, Section~4.2, develops an anomaly-reasoning model through LoRA-based supervised training and TKTO preference optimization. Feedback combines format compliance, task correctness, fluency, and human preference into score $S$. A smooth preference weight mixes desirable and undesirable response losses:
\begin{equation}
 \alpha=\sigma\!\left(\kappa(S-\eta)\right),\qquad
 \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{TKTO}}=\mathbb{E}_{(x,y,d)\sim\mathcal{D}}
 [\alpha\mathcal{L}_{D}(\pi_\theta,x,y)+(1-\alpha)\mathcal{L}_{U}(\pi_\theta,x,y)].
 \label{eq:chatad-tkto}
\end{equation}
At $S=\eta$, the two losses receive equal weight; increasing $\kappa$ makes the transition between preferred and rejected responses sharper. ChatAD \citep{chatad2026} uses this soft assignment to address noise near a hard preference threshold (Section~4.2.2, Eqs.~3--5). The composite score combines LLM assessments of format, correctness, and fluency with human preference. Thus Equation~\ref{eq:chatad-tkto} compares graded preference supervision with a binary accept/reject decision: its practical requirements include a consistent scoring rubric and calibration of the score relative to $\eta$. ChatAD's evaluation separates detection accuracy, F1, and false-positive rate from open-ended reasoning scores and format-parsing rates (Section~4.3.2, Tables~3--4). The latter measure judged answer quality and parseability; checking explanation faithfulness additionally requires evidence about how the answer was produced. The synthetic-dialogue agents create supervision, while the resulting ChatAD model supplies the learned anomaly-reasoning capability. AnomaMind \citep{anomamind2026} also combines a trained detector with an external evidence-acquisition workflow; its task-specific feedback and detection equations are developed in the anomaly section.

These methods differ in the learned role and the feedback available to train it. TSOrchestra \citep{conversationaltsfm2025} is useful when a judge must accept or continue numerical ensemble optimization; CastFSR \citep{castfsr2026} learns decisions across a prescribed tool workflow; ChatAD \citep{chatad2026} adapts generated analytical responses using graded preferences. A developer therefore selects both a model and its required feedback interface. The Harness must expose optimizer histories or tool observations, retain intermediate state, enforce valid calls and temporal boundaries, and record outcomes for evaluation. For a workflow policy, action quality is tested through executed trajectories and their task outcomes, while the accompanying explanation is assessed separately.

\subsection{Interface and Interaction}

The interaction interface defines what the Agent observes, which actions it can request, and how the result returns to the next reasoning step. TS-Agent \citep{tsagent2025} uses an iterative thought--action--observation loop: the reasoner chooses its next step after receiving the structured result of the preceding analytical operation. This supports questions whose required computation becomes clear during analysis. AION \citep{aion2026} starts from a more explicit interface, consisting of a task file, accessible workspace, and validator. The task file declares the required artifact and constraints before work begins.

These designs support different user interactions. An exploratory request benefits from successive tool observations and follow-up questions; a submission-oriented task benefits from an explicit output specification and acceptance check. Both require a stable action interface so that the LLM can identify an operation, supply its arguments, and interpret success or failure. The numerical operation and its temporal arguments are developed in the following section; the interaction loop can remain the same as the analytical objective changes.

\subsection{Memory and Context}

Memory determines which earlier observations remain available to later steps. TS-Agent \citep{tsagent2025} retains an evidence log so that subsequent reasoning can use completed analytical results. AION \citep{aion2026} records progress, decisions, and structural state around execution and review. Such records serve distinct purposes: the evidence log supports an answer, while the progress record supports continuation after interruption or handoff.

TradingAgents \citep{tradingagents2024} addresses memory through structured reports. Each role extracts the information it needs and returns a completed report to the shared workflow. This gives later roles a stable summary with an identifiable author and purpose. Compared with an unrestricted conversation history, the report boundary makes the input to each decision easier to inspect. In a new Agent, the design choice is which information should remain as raw tool output, which should become a compact report, and which should persist across episodes. Signal-aware retrieval adds a further temporal requirement and is discussed in Section~\ref{sec:general-ts-harness}.

\subsection{Tools and Execution}

Execution converts a selected action into a computation and returns its result, artifacts, and status. TimeSage's reference runtime in TimeSage-MT \citep{timesagemt2026} separates the LLM Planner and Reporter from deterministic validation, profiling, execution, and evaluation. The planner selects a workflow, while the executor carries out its operations. This division makes it possible to inspect whether an error arose in the chosen workflow or in the implementation of an operation.

MetaCaster's Forecaster Trainer \citep{metacaster2026} demonstrates execution at the level of computational jobs. It assembles jobs indexed by model, hyperparameters, and dataset, queues them, assigns available GPUs, monitors processes, and recovers errors or interrupted runs. The reusable mechanism is scheduling and recovery; the job's forecasting objective is supplied by the task procedure. For an engineering Agent, this distinction separates the ability to complete a computation from the quality of the model produced by that computation.

\subsection{Planning and Orchestration}

Planning specifies dependencies among the operations needed to deliver an artifact. TS-Agent \citep{tsagent2025} chooses actions incrementally from accumulated observations. TS-Reasoner \citep{tsreasoner2026} constructs an ordered executable plan and revises it after failure. The first approach makes the next action sensitive to newly observed evidence; the second provides an explicit plan that can be inspected and rerun as a whole.

AION \citep{aion2026} extends planning to the lifecycle of work through protocols for execution, review, rechecking, memory updates, and stopping. The resulting design separates exploratory choices from required checkpoints. For example, an Agent may explore several analytical routes while all routes must still pass an artifact validator. This is useful when the practitioner needs both flexibility in solving the task and a repeatable acceptance process.

Control determines whether the Agent continues, revises its work, requests review, or terminates. TS-Reasoner \citep{tsreasoner2026} provides an executable example: its task decomposer produces an ordered operator plan, the executor runs it, and an execution error triggers a new end-to-end plan. The error message is actionable feedback about the current procedure. AION \citep{aion2026} adds artifact-level validity checks and a separate scored evaluation, allowing a candidate to be returned for revision before acceptance.

The source and timing of feedback determine what can change during an episode. An execution error is immediately available; a resolved forecast error is available only after the target window has been observed. FLAIRR-TS \citep{flairr2025} uses recent ground truth to refine forecasting prompts and is therefore developed with the forecasting methods. A system that combines these controls should expose both the retry policy for failed operations and the schedule under which task outcomes become available for learning.

Coordination defines responsibilities and the information passed between roles. TradingAgents \citep{tradingagents2024} uses structured report-based communication so that each role consumes a defined state and returns a completed result. AION \citep{aion2026} makes work, review, rechecking, and stop signals explicit in the communication protocol. These arrangements connect the ownership of an artifact to the condition under which the next role can act.

The task determines the content of the handoff. In FinCon \citep{fincon2024}, analysts and a manager aggregate financial evidence before portfolio actions; in TimeSeriesScientist \citep{timeseriesscientist2025}, curation and planning outputs guide forecasting and reporting. Their financial or forecasting objectives are analyzed in Section~\ref{sec:task-specific-harness}. These examples suggest defining a role around the artifact it must deliver and the check that permits the next role to proceed. This makes handoffs inspectable and gives failures a recovery point. Across these runtime functions, the shared design objective is to keep action selection, execution state, and feedback connected.

\subsection{Optimization and Evolution}

A Harness can improve through changes to external experience, prompts, or executable tools. TimeClaw with Exploratory Execution Learning (TimeClaw-EEL) \citep{timeclaw2026explore} compares alternative executed trajectories and distills useful experience into a state available to later episodes. TimeClaw-Harness \citep{timeclaw2026harness}, a separate TimeClaw runtime for contextualized time series, develops a tool-promotion mechanism: a code-specialized LLM receives clustered trajectories and task descriptions, extracts a recurring procedure, and produces code with an invocation description. The candidate is tested on held-out similar tasks and enters the registry after meeting the success threshold.

These mechanisms change different reusable assets. Experience distillation changes the guidance available to the reasoner, while tool promotion changes what the executor can call. Both require a record of the source episodes and the criterion used to accept an update. The procedure's analytical content determines its temporal or task scope; the mechanism for extracting, testing, and installing the update remains reusable. The focused TimeClaw-EEL analysis gives the source equations for the experience update.

\section{General Time Series Harness}
\label{sec:general-ts-harness}

The General Time Series Harness specializes the General Harness functions through temporal order, index, frequency, window, horizon, regime, availability time, and leakage control. We organize these requirements around the temporal-grounding mechanisms of AION \citep{aion2026} and the runtime-native operations of TimeClaw-Harness \citep{timeclaw2026harness}. These temporal mechanisms build on the interaction, execution, and feedback interfaces of the General Harness.

The contract boundary makes the distinction explicit. General Harness specifies how an Agent stores state, invokes tools, coordinates roles, and verifies outputs. General Time Series Harness specifies what a valid temporal state or operation means: which observations were available, how a window is aligned, which frequency and units apply, and how a revision is represented. TimeClaw-Harness \citep{timeclaw2026harness} and AION \citep{aion2026} make this boundary explicit through time series-native execution, temporal grounding, and reliability mechanisms.

For a temporal state at decision time $\tau$, the admissible information can be represented as
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{I}_{\tau} = \{(x_i,t_i,v_i): v_i \leq \tau\},
  \label{eq:temporal-availability}
\end{equation}
where $t_i$ is the event time and $v_i$ is the time at which the value became available. The availability boundary admits information published by $\tau$, including a known future calendar event; the task specifies which observation and target windows may be used.

Figure~\ref{fig:temporal-harness-functions} connects the four shared functions through the objects they manage. Read the upper timeline first: availability time determines whether a source can be used at decision time $\tau$. The middle row follows timestamped values, a selected processing window, an aligned event, and an evidence-admission check. The lower paths connect the LLM to requests and returned observations. These operations implement the temporal grounding and evidence checks emphasized by AION \citep{aion2026} and TimeClaw-Harness \citep{timeclaw2026harness}.

\subsection{Temporal Data Management}
\label{sec:temporal-data}

Before an Agent can compare signals or call a model, its inputs must identify what was measured, when it was measured, and how the stored values should be interpreted. The input schema therefore includes timestamps, sampling intervals, units, missingness, and revision information. These properties form the interface between the raw source and every later operation. AION \citep{aion2026}, Section~3.3.1, places this interface before model selection: the task must resolve the observation unit, visible history, cutoff, requested horizon, granularity, and output frequency. The resulting requirements determine which data are admissible and which operations can satisfy the request.

BatteryLake \citep{batterylake2026} makes measurement semantics explicit through a canonical sample schema. Time, voltage, current, temperature, capacity, and step type have fixed units and domains; the current-sign convention distinguishes charging from discharging. This supports consistent interpretation of heterogeneous battery files before downstream health or lifetime analysis. LEMAD \citep{ji2025lemad} addresses a related ingestion problem for operational services: its preprocessing module aligns timestamps, normalizes formats, and handles missing values across heterogeneous streams. The common design choice is to resolve measurement and alignment conventions at ingestion, where subsequent tools can inherit them.

Stored data also need a representation that preserves numerical precision. TimeClaw-Harness \citep{timeclaw2026harness}, Section~3.2, keeps the full time series object in a task-local workspace. Tools run on that object and return compact structured observations to the LLM. SAGE-AD \citep{sagead2026}, Section~3.2, likewise separates the original series used by numerical tools from a compressed summary used in prompting. These mechanisms let the language interface remain compact while the analytical operations retain access to the measurements. The anomaly-specific use of SAGE-AD's resulting evidence is discussed in Section~\ref{sec:task-anomaly}.

Streaming inputs introduce an additional state requirement. CALM \citep{calm2025} buffers incoming observations per key before its stateful sliding-window function emits the ordered window. Without this step, out-of-order arrival changes which observations enter a computation. This mechanism complements a canonical schema: the schema defines the meaning of each sample, while the buffer preserves membership and order as samples arrive. For a new application, the ingestion design follows the source of ambiguity: measurement schemas address heterogeneous files, numerical workspaces preserve tool inputs, and ordered buffers handle streaming arrivals. These choices can be combined as the data sources require. Testing should vary arrival order, units, missing entries, or timestamp formats and inspect the numerical object delivered to the next operation.

\subsection{Temporal Processing}
\label{sec:temporal-processing}

Processing converts a stored series into the evidence needed for analysis. Common operations include window selection, resampling, decomposition, smoothing, correlation analysis, and feature extraction. Their inputs and outputs must preserve the relation to the source sequence: the same numerical array may describe levels, differences, normalized values, or an aggregate over a longer interval. A useful tool interface therefore exposes both the operation and the temporal meaning of its result.

TS-Agent \citep{tsagent2025} supplies analytical APIs that operate directly on native numerical series and return structured findings. TS-Reasoner \citep{tsreasoner2026} wraps statistical tools, learned models, and numerical utilities in a consistent calling structure. TimeART \citep{timeart2026} uses atomic tools for computation-intensive temporal operations. These designs differ in their inventories and calling policies, but each makes a numerical operation independently accessible to the Agent. A developer can compose these operations around the target workflow, inspect their intermediate outputs, and reuse them across analytical requests.

TimeSeriesScientist's Curator \citep{timeseriesscientist2025}, Section~3.2, illustrates how inspection determines the operation. Given a series $D$, it produces data statistics $S$, missing-value information $M$, outlier findings $O$, and a treatment strategy $\pi$:
\begin{equation}
  Q=A_f(D)=(S,M,O,\pi),\qquad
  \widetilde{D}=\phi_{\pi}(D).
  \label{eq:tsci-curator}
\end{equation}
The processed series then supports visualization, decomposition, and autocorrelation analysis. These diagnostics precede the forecasting-specific model selection in Section~\ref{sec:task-forecasting}. For example, identifying missing intervals changes the preprocessing plan, whereas ranking candidate forecasters requires a target horizon and validation loss. Separating the two decisions makes the preparation stage reusable while keeping model selection tied to its output objective.

MAS4TS \citep{mas4ts2026} combines decomposition, smoothing, statistical prediction, constraint projection, and residual correction. Its numerical operators illustrate ingredients of temporal processing; the router selects from a task-indexed chain registry using a downstream task objective. We therefore record the routed chain as a task-specific procedure, while its shared plot-anchor and latent-trajectory representation supplies a General Time Series Harness module. TimeSage-MT \citep{timesagemt2026} similarly provides a skill library that a planner uses across analytical turns. Atomic operators expose intermediate findings for inspection and revision; composed chains capture recurring sequences of analysis. The appropriate granularity depends on where new observations can change the plan. In either case, the next tool needs the processed window, applied transformation, and output scale. This makes explicit intermediate metadata a practical condition for reuse. Evaluation should check individual numerical results and the composed sequence, as in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}.

\subsection{Temporal Context and Memory}
\label{sec:temporal-context}

Temporal analysis often requires information beyond the numerical sequence. Documents describe events, prior episodes provide analogies, and earlier analyses supply reusable procedures. The retrieval problem has two parts: representing relevance to the current signal and determining whether the evidence is available at the current analysis time. An Agent needs both to use retrieved material consistently.

NEXUS \citep{nexus2026}, Section~3.1, aligns historical values and contemporaneous textual factors into a chronological context timeline. The result preserves when a contextual explanation applies to the observations. This representation organizes the history around the temporal relationship between a numerical change and its accompanying evidence.

TimeClaw-Harness \citep{timeclaw2026harness}, Section~3.4, retrieves prior analytical episodes through separate signal and text keys. A memory record is
\begin{equation}
  m=(\mathcal{T},\phi,\psi,\zeta,\rho),
  \label{eq:timeclaw-memory}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{T}$ denotes the task, $\phi$ and $\psi$ are signal and text keys, $\zeta$ is the successful trajectory, and $\rho$ records its transferable rationale. Similar wording may describe different signal structures, while similar shapes may occur under different contextual conditions. Separate keys preserve these two sources of relevance, and the retained trajectory explains how a preceding episode was solved. The combined record supports retrieval by signal structure and context, followed by reuse of the associated solution trajectory.

The task-specific boundary enters when the retrieval or retention policy depends on the target outcome. FinMem \citep{finmem2023} ranks and promotes financial memories for trading, while FLAIRR-TS \citep{flairr2025} combines historical-segment retrieval with observed forecasting errors. Their policies are analyzed under decision support and forecasting in Section~\ref{sec:task-specific-harness}. The memory design should follow the object to be reused: contextual events require temporal alignment, pattern knowledge requires signal-sensitive retrieval, and solution experience requires the associated trajectory and rationale. Task feedback then determines how those records are selected or retained. Comparing retrieved content, availability time, and downstream effects reveals whether the memory contributes relevant evidence to the current decision.

\subsection{Temporal Validation}
\label{sec:temporal-validation}

An analysis can execute successfully while using information from the wrong period or making a claim unsupported by its observations. Temporal validation checks these relationships before the task score is interpreted. AION \citep{aion2026} separates validity findings, including timestamp alignment and leakage boundaries, from scored outcomes such as forecasting error or report quality. The distinction allows the same temporal checks to precede several different output metrics.

Some implementations enforce availability at retrieval time. WorldReasoner \citep{worldreasoner2026} filters article and event records against the simulated forecast date. TIEM \citep{tiem2026} additionally checks the provenance of retained skills: every ancestor must have resolved before the current cutoff. TimeSage-EV \citep{timesageev2026}, Appendix~A.6, imposes a related capability boundary by activating a newly accepted skill only in the next period. These mechanisms address different paths through which later information can enter an earlier analysis: retrieved records, inherited experience, and newly created tools. Equation~\ref{eq:temporal-availability} provides the common information boundary, while each implementation checks a particular access path.

Other checks concern transformations and claims. FactorMAD \citep{duan2025factormad} instructs factor construction to exclude future timestamps and use rolling statistics instead of statistics computed over the full series. This check is separable from the financial value of the factor. TS-Agent \citep{tsagent2025} uses temporal question intents to declare required evidence predicates: an anomaly-location answer requires evidence of an anomaly and its supported segment, while other intents require trend, seasonality, or lag findings. The intent-specific gate is a Task-Specific Harness module: its required predicates and answer schema are defined by the temporal question. Shared numerical tools supply the findings that this QA procedure checks. The admissibility of the input and the sufficiency of the output evidence are complementary checks.

TSQAgent \citep{tsqbench2026} evaluates data quality through dimension selection, evidence inspection, and pairwise adjudication. Its quality judgment can guide data selection before forecasting or classification. Across these examples, a validation interface should return the relevant finding and its supporting data, so that a developer can distinguish an invalid time boundary, a deficient input series, and a poorly supported answer. The placement of each check follows the failure it addresses: availability filters govern evidence access, data-quality judgments guide input selection, and evidence gates assess the support for a requested claim. These checks provide distinct feedback for revision. Task-specific scores then measure the usefulness of the accepted result. Together, the four temporal functions establish the data and evidence conditions under which the task procedures in the next section operate.

\section{Task-Specific Time Series Harness}
\label{sec:task-specific-harness}

Task-specific Harness modules organize shared capabilities around a required output and objective. This section follows the task axis of Figure~\ref{fig:task-function-grid}: each family starts from its inputs and output contract, then compares the procedures used to construct, revise, and assess the result. The temporal data, processing, context, and validation functions in Section~\ref{sec:general-ts-harness} supply reusable building blocks.

The task comparisons focus on how output requirements change tool selection, feedback, and stopping decisions. The four families follow the task grouping of a prior time series Agent survey \citep{chen2026tsagentsurvey} and differ in their output contracts: a forecast is judged against values realized after the horizon, a generated series by fidelity and downstream utility, a diagnosis by localized intervals and supporting evidence, and a decision by the utility and risk of the selected action. Among the 135 retained works, 74 address forecasting and prediction, 35 anomaly detection and diagnosis, 34 decision support, and 11 data augmentation and synthesis; a work may address several families. Conventional time series tasks outside these families also appear: 24 works address temporal question answering, 14 classification, and 6 imputation. These outputs are recorded in the task coordinate; the analytical operators in Section~\ref{sec:temporal-processing} and the question-answering evaluation in Section~\ref{sec:temporal-qa} connect to them. Synthesis remains a separate family despite its smaller count because its output contract differs from prediction, diagnosis, and action selection.

The task contract determines the final object being optimized. For forecasting, a typical contract is
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{y}_{\tau+1:\tau+H}=f_\theta\!\left(\mathcal{I}_{\tau};H\right),
  \label{eq:forecast-contract}
\end{equation}
where $H$ is the declared horizon and $\mathcal{I}_{\tau}$ contains observed target history and covariates known at the origin, including future calendar information available under Eq.~\ref{eq:temporal-availability}.

The four panels in Figure~\ref{fig:task-specific-harness} connect each task output to its construction and evaluation. The illustrations show a forecast and its horizon, generated samples, a localized anomaly with diagnostic evidence, and alternative actions with their outcomes. Read each panel from the illustrated object to the input requirements, procedure, and evaluation criterion below it. The task output determines which feedback can guide revision and what counts as an acceptable result.

\subsection{Forecasting and Prediction}
\label{sec:task-forecasting}

Forecasting fixes an origin, target, horizon, and evaluation rule. The agent must choose how to build a forecast, which contextual evidence to use, and when to revise it. Numerical prediction produces future values or a predictive distribution; event prediction produces classes or probabilities for future outcomes. These contracts lead to different model interfaces and scoring rules.

\noindent\textbf{Model selection and forecast construction.} TimeSeriesScientist \citep{timeseriesscientist2025} develops a numerical forecasting workflow in which the Curator's diagnostics guide the Planner's model search. For each candidate forecaster, the Planner tunes its configuration using validation MAPE:
\begin{equation}
  \theta_i^{\star}=\arg\min_{\theta_i\in\mathcal{C}_i}
  \operatorname{MAPE}_{\mathrm{val}}\!\left(m_i(\theta_i)\right),\qquad
  \operatorname{MAPE}_{\mathrm{val}}(m_i(\theta_i))=
  \frac{100\%}{|\widetilde{D}_{\mathrm{val}}|}
  \sum_{t\in\widetilde{D}_{\mathrm{val}}}
  \left|\frac{x_t-\widehat{x}^{(i,\theta_i)}_t}{x_t}\right|.
  \label{eq:tsci-planner}
\end{equation}
The selected configurations are ranked, and the Forecaster uses their validation results to choose a single model or an ensemble, fixing its strategy and weights before evaluating the test set. This links the temporal diagnostics in Section~\ref{sec:general-ts-harness} to a task-specific selection criterion. TimeCopilot \citep{timecopilot2025} likewise makes forecasting models callable components; the model library supplies numerical capabilities while the agent organizes their use.

\noindent\textbf{Contextual forecasting and revision.} FLAIRR-TS \citep{flairr2025}, Algorithm~1, refines the forecasting prompt through retrieved historical segments and observed forecast errors:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  S_{\mathrm{retr}}^{(k)}&=\operatorname{RetrieveSegments}(X_{\mathrm{HistDB}},X_{\mathrm{Ctx}},M),\\
  C_{\mathrm{aug}}^{(k)}&=\operatorname{AugmentContext}(X_{\mathrm{Ctx}},S_{\mathrm{retr}}^{(k)}),\\
  \widehat{X}^{(k)}&=F_{\mathrm{LLM}}(P_{\mathrm{curr}}^{(k)},C_{\mathrm{aug}}^{(k)},H),\\
  \operatorname{mae}^{(k)}&=\operatorname{MAE}(\widehat{X}^{(k)},X_V),\\
  (P_{\mathrm{next}}^{(k)},d^{(k)})&=R_{\mathrm{LLM}}\!\left(\{(P^{(i)},\operatorname{mae}^{(i)})\}_{i<k},P_{\mathrm{curr}}^{(k)},\tau_{\mathrm{stop}}\right).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:flairr-loop}
\end{equation}
Here $X_V$ is the observed target sequence for an $H$-step refinement window $V=(t_1,\ldots,t_H)$. The refiner proposes prompt updates from earlier prompts and errors; early stopping returns the current prompt, while reaching the iteration limit returns the lowest-MAE prompt. In deployment, this feedback becomes available when the refinement window has resolved.

CastFSR \citep{castfsr2026} separates fast numerical forecasting, slower contextual deliberation, and reflective evaluation. The initial prediction supplies a numerical prior; retrieved context then supports a candidate revision. Its state and action families make that division explicit:
\begin{equation}
  s_k=\Phi(I,M_k,\widehat{Y}^{\mathrm{prior}}_k,C_k),\qquad
  a_k\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid s_k),\quad a_k\in\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{fast}}\cup\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{slow}}\cup\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{reflect}}\cup\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{final}}.
  \label{eq:castfsr-state-action}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  C_k&=T_{\mathrm{ctx}}(D_{\mathrm{task}},D_{\mathrm{domain}},X_{1:L},H_{\mathrm{ctx}},\widehat{Y}^{\mathrm{prior}}_k,M_k;W_k),\\
  \widehat{Y}^{\mathrm{cand}}_k&=G_{\theta}(\widehat{Y}^{\mathrm{prior}}_k,C_k,M_k),\\
  E_k&=E_{\theta}(\widehat{Y}^{\mathrm{cand}}_k,\widehat{Y}^{\mathrm{prior}}_k,C_k,M_k),\qquad
  \widehat{Y}_{1:H}=F_{\theta}(\widehat{Y}^{\mathrm{cand}}_k,E_k,M_k).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:castfsr-revision}
\end{equation}
Here $I=(D_{\mathrm{task}},D_{\mathrm{domain}},X_{1:L},H_{\mathrm{ctx}})$ contains the target and horizon, domain description, observed series, and available contextual information; $M_k$ stores the interaction history and $W_k$ sets the context lookback. Reflection checks the candidate against temporal alignment, contextual support, and domain constraints such as units and capacity. Weak or redundant context can leave the numerical prior unchanged, whereas relevant evidence supports a targeted revision. Last-Mile Forecasting \citep{lastmile2026} and CastFlow \citep{castflow2026} provide related settings for contextual revision, while HydroAgent \citep{hydroagent2026} connects predictions to hydrological simulation and warnings.

SCENARIODIFF \citep{scenariodiff2026} uses a Historical Context Agent, a Scenario Agent, and an Anchor Guidance Agent to translate documents into historical summaries, a future scenario, and sparse anchor constraints. A diffusion forecaster generates the future trajectory conditioned on this information:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x^{(0)},t,\epsilon}
  \left\|x^{(0)}-x_{\theta}\!\left(x^{(t)},t,c\right)\right\|_2^2,
  \qquad
  x^{(t)}=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}x^{(0)}+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon,
  \label{eq:scenariodiff-denoising}
\end{equation}
Here $x^{(0)}$ is the future target horizon, $t$ indexes diffusion steps, $\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)$, and $c$ contains the numerical lookback, historical-text summary, and a scenario inferred from historical information. Anchor Blended Sampling edits anchor-relevant regions of the generated trajectory while retaining the remaining temporal structure. The generation step therefore implements probabilistic forecasting. It differs from the training-series generation below in both its output and its evaluation target.

\noindent\textbf{Event and probability prediction.} Event prediction specifies a future event label or probability as its output. TimeCAP \citep{timecap2025}, Section~3.3, first asks a frozen contextualizer to describe the input series, $s_x=A_C(x)$. A trained multimodal encoder then combines the series and its description to predict the event and construct a retrieval embedding:
\begin{equation}
  (\widehat{y}_{\mathrm{MM}},z)=E_{\phi}(x,s_x),\qquad
  \mathcal{S}=\{(s_{x_j},y_j):j\in\operatorname{NN}_k(z)\}.
  \label{eq:timecap-retrieval}
\end{equation}
The nearest neighbors are retrieved from the training set. Their descriptions and labels serve as in-context examples for the predictor LLM, which returns a discrete event class $\widehat c_{\mathrm{LLM}}$. This class is converted to a one-hot vector and combined with the encoder logits $\widehat y_{\mathrm{MM}}$, with $\lambda\in[0,1]$:
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{c}_{\mathrm{LLM}}=A_P(s_x,\mathcal{S}),\qquad
  \widehat{y}=\lambda\operatorname{onehot}(\widehat{c}_{\mathrm{LLM}})+(1-\lambda)\widehat{y}_{\mathrm{MM}}.
  \label{eq:timecap-dual-agent}
\end{equation}
The distinctive mechanism is the learned retrieval of relevant event examples and their use in complementary predictors. Here augmentation enriches the prediction context; the output contract is event prediction. AIA Forecaster \citep{aiaforecaster2025} combines search, supervisor aggregation, and calibration for judgmental event forecasts; AUDITFORECAST \citep{auditforecast2026} anchors predictions in a quantitative baseline and updates them in odds space. These methods require probability and outcome evaluation alongside evidence access and trace inspection. Their comparison is most informative when the target event, resolution time, and available evidence are fixed. Across forecasting workflows, a numerical trajectory calls for horizon-specific validation, while an event probability calls for an explicit resolution rule and calibration assessment. Retrieval, model selection, and forecast revision should be chosen according to the evidence available before that target resolves. This connects the Agent's analytical choices to the feedback that can actually guide them.

\subsection{Data Augmentation and Synthesis}
\label{sec:task-synthesis}

Augmentation and synthesis create examples for learning or analysis. The output may be a transformed positive view, an additional training series, or a temporal instruction dialogue. Its evaluation depends on that use: preserving a useful representation, training an accurate downstream model, and teaching a language model require different checks.

BRIDGE \citep{bridge2025}, Section~3, defines text-controlled generation through
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{x}=f_{\theta}(\mathcal{D},l,z),
  \label{eq:bridge-generation}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{D}$ supplies the domain, $l$ describes desired temporal properties such as trends and periodicity, and $z$ is sampled noise. Its multi-agent preparation stage collects textual templates, evaluates the resulting descriptions through a text-conditioned forecasting task, and refines the templates using feedback. The generation stage combines the description with domain prototypes to condition a diffusion model. This separates control of the generated object's properties from construction of the textual conditions used to obtain it. The forecasting evaluator supplies feedback for template refinement; the final generation experiments examine the synthetic series.

MERIT \citep{merit2025} targets unsupervised time series representation learning. A retrieval agent selects relevant series from a candidate set; an augmentation agent uses those examples to choose transformations; and a review agent assesses the resulting positive views. Accepted samples enter a memory bank, while rejection sends the workflow back to retrieval for refinement. The retrieved neighbors and augmented views then support contrastive learning. The review acts on the generated examples during construction, while downstream experiments evaluate the learned representation. Figure~\ref{fig:merit-augmentation} follows these decisions through a schematic augmentation example and shows how the resulting positive set enters the representation learner.

MetaCaster \citep{metacaster2026} generates training series from a small support set and domain context, then trains lightweight forecasters on the generated data. Its Harness Proposer changes the generator's prompts and skills to improve the downstream forecasting result. The optimization objective compares forecasters trained on authentic and generated data:
\begin{equation}
  \theta^{\star}=\arg\min_{\theta}\;\mathbb{E}_{m,l}\,\delta\!\left(\omega(f_l^m),\omega(\bar f_l^m)\right),\qquad
  \bar D^m=\operatorname{MGAgent}(D_{\mathrm{sup}}^m,C^m),
  \label{eq:metacaster-objective}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \delta\!\left(\omega(f_l^m),\omega(\bar f_l^m)\right)=
  \max\!\left\{\frac{\omega(\bar f_l^m)-\omega(f_l^m)}{\omega(f_l^m)},0\right\}
  \label{eq:metacaster-hinge}
\end{equation}
Here $\theta$ denotes the generator's prompt and skills, and $\omega$ measures forecasting error on the evaluation split of a dataset in the harness-optimization corpus. The hinge-style discrepancy penalizes a generated-data forecaster when it performs worse than its authentic-data counterpart. This feedback updates the generator; final generalization is assessed on separate in-domain and out-of-domain datasets. After optimization, the Harness Proposer is removed and the learned prompts and skills guide generation for a new support set. Generation and harness optimization are coupled to the forecasting objective; the job scheduler and recovery mechanism remain separately reusable General Harness components. This provides a concrete example of one paper contributing distinct modules at different layers.

For language-model supervision, ChatAD \citep{chatad2026} constructs temporal instruction dialogues through TSEvol; this model-side use of synthesis is discussed in Section~3. Comparing these methods requires identifying the generated object and the learner or analysis it is intended to support.

Figure~\ref{fig:merit-augmentation} makes the design choice explicit: retrieval supplies examples, augmentation constructs positive views, and review determines which views enter contrastive learning. This workflow suits representation learning, where the generated views must preserve useful signal structure. MetaCaster couples generation to downstream forecast error, making forecaster performance the criterion for revising the generator. Selecting a synthesis workflow therefore starts with the intended learner and the feedback available to improve its training data.

\subsection{Anomaly Detection and Diagnosis}
\label{sec:task-anomaly}

Anomaly detection asks where a series departs from expected behavior; diagnosis adds an explanation of that departure and may lead to a response. These outputs require different evidence. An interval detector needs numerical and contextual support for the flagged region, while a service-diagnosis workflow also needs logs, incident context, and a mapping from symptoms to possible causes.

AnomaMind \citep{anomamind2026} develops the interval-detection workflow through coarse evidence acquisition, adaptive evidence construction, anomaly detection, and iterative refinement. At step $k$, the Agent maintains state $s_k$, selects action $a_k$, and receives numerical or knowledge evidence $o_k$. Its toolkit $B=\{K,N\}$ separates knowledge memory $K$ from numerical diagnostics $N$. The workflow updates the evidence state and applies a learned detector to the accumulated numerical and contextual evidence:
\begin{equation}
  s_{k+1}=E\!\left(s_k,a_k,o_k,\widehat{Y}^{(k)}\right),\qquad
  \widehat{y}_j=D_{\theta}\!\left(u_j,Z_{1:k},C_{1:k}\right),\quad \widehat{y}_j\in\{0,1\},
  \label{eq:anomamind-decision}
\end{equation}
where $u_j=[\ell_j,r_j]$ is a candidate interval and $s_k=(S^{(k)},Z_{1:k},C_{1:k})$ contains the candidate set, numerical diagnostics, and contextual knowledge. During detector training, rule-based feedback combines parse validity, agreement with labeled anomalous intervals, and a false-positive penalty:
\begin{equation}
  r_k=\lambda_p r_k^{\mathrm{parse}}+\lambda_f r_k^{F1}-\lambda_{fp}r_k^{FP},\qquad
  \max_{\theta}\;\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{Y}^{(k)}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid s_k)}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{K}r_k\right].
  \label{eq:anomamind-reward}
\end{equation}
The reward supplies learning signals for a parseable and accurate interval decision. Figure~\ref{fig:anomamind-workflow} follows the four stages and shows how evidence acquisition and iterative revision support that output. The trained detector belongs to the LLM side; the surrounding evidence-acquisition and refinement procedure supplies the anomaly-specific Harness.

SAGE-AD \citep{sagead2026} uses a complementary form of evidence. It preserves the original series for precise tools and a compressed representation for the LLM. Family-specific analyzers and diagnostic visualizations feed a detector that returns anomaly intervals, confidence, and anomaly types. Its in-context reference database is constructed from normal training segments: representative subsequences are selected by clustering, then rule-based perturbations create normal--anomalous examples for nine anomaly types. This makes example retrieval part of the detection procedure, with the family-specific evidence guiding the interpretation of a suspicious interval.

\noindent\textbf{Detection rules and executable pipelines.} ARGOS \citep{argos2025}, Sections~3.1--3.3, turns labeled temporal chunks into executable detection rules. A Detection Agent proposes rules, a Repair Agent executes them on dummy inputs to resolve implementation errors, and a Review Agent compares their accuracy on validation data. During deployment, the rules are combined with an existing detector. The learned artifact is a reusable detector program, whereas AnomaMind selects evidence and refines an interval decision within an analysis. Their evaluation must therefore account for when rule construction occurs and which labels are available during that phase.

AD-AGENT \citep{adagent2025} constructs a pipeline from a natural-language request and a dataset. Its Processor extracts modality and supervision requirements, the Selector chooses a supported library and model, and the subsequent agents retrieve implementation information, generate code, and review execution. TSLib supplies its time series branch. This approach suits requests that require assembling an executable detector from existing libraries. Its Reviewer tests execution on synthetic samples; optional Evaluator and Optimizer agents assess labeled data and tune hyperparameters. For a deployed time series pipeline, executable-code success and detection performance on held-out series provide complementary checks. Across these methods, the central choice is whether to learn evidence acquisition, retrieve diagnostic examples, or construct a detection pipeline; the available supervision, evidence sources, and required output determine which procedure fits the scenario.

\noindent\textbf{From detection to diagnosis.} LEMAD \citep{ji2025lemad} extends the output to service diagnosis in power-grid operations. Metric and log collectors send aligned streams through Kafka to a real-time analysis agent, which correlates the signals and produces alert summaries and preliminary hypotheses. An anomaly-detection agent further evaluates suspicious events using hybrid models and contextual reasoning, returning confidence, root-cause localization, and recommendations. Its reported evaluation includes precision, recall, F1, and latency on service data. The construction lesson is that adding diagnosis changes the evidence inputs and final artifact: an interval label becomes one input to a broader incident-analysis workflow.

\subsection{Decision Support}
\label{sec:task-decision}

Decision-support Agents select an action using predictive or diagnostic evidence. The action space and feedback determine the task-specific procedure: portfolio allocation acts through positions and returns, traffic control acts through signal configurations and queues, and maintenance support produces recommendations for review. The Agent must therefore represent both the temporal state and the consequences of the available actions.

The context policy can itself be specialized for an action. FinMem \citep{finmem2023}, Section~3, separates financial memories into layers with different retention times and ranks events using recency, relevance, and importance. Significant investment memories can be promoted to deeper layers, and retrieved events condition a trading decision together with the current market state. The memory policy therefore depends on the useful lifetime of financial information and trading experience. A general retrieval component supplies storage and access; this task-specific policy decides what to retain and how it influences the action.

FinCon \citep{fincon2024} specifies financial analyst roles that deliver investment insights to a trading manager. Their output contracts include market evidence, stock selection, and risk feedback, making the implemented coordination procedure task-specific. Its prompt-controlled policy maximizes cumulative profit and loss,
\begin{equation}
  \max_{\theta}\;\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}}\alpha^t R_t^{\Pi_{\theta}}\right],
  \qquad R_t^{\Pi_{\theta}}=R(O_t,B_t,A_t),
  \label{eq:fincon-objective}
\end{equation}
where $\theta$ comprises agent prompts, $\alpha$ discounts future rewards, and $O_t$, $B_t$, and $A_t$ denote market observations, managerial reflection, and trading action. FinCon updates prompts between training episodes using comparative performance feedback; within-episode risk alerts also guide testing decisions. Its portfolio manager solves a mean--variance allocation subject to the buy/sell/hold direction selected by the agents:
\begin{equation}
  \max_{w}\;\langle w,\mu\rangle-\langle w,\Sigma w\rangle,\qquad
  w_n\in\begin{cases}[0,1],&\text{buy},\\\left[-1,0\right],&\text{sell},\\\{0\},&\text{hold},\end{cases}
  \quad n=1,\ldots,N.
  \label{eq:fincon-portfolio}
\end{equation}
Here $\mu$ and $\Sigma$ are shrinkage estimates of mean daily returns and their covariance. In FinCon's encoding, $w_n$ is portfolio exposure: buy permits a long position, sell permits a short position, and hold assigns zero exposure. The portfolio is rebalanced daily. The analyst--manager interaction supplies the directional evidence, and the allocation problem converts that evidence into a position under the method's risk objective. TradingAgents \citep{tradingagents2024} emphasizes a different decision procedure: analyst reports feed bullish and bearish research roles, whose debate precedes trading and risk deliberation. Both systems connect evidence aggregation to an action, while their coordination and acceptance procedures differ.

CoLLMLight \citep{collmllight2026} makes the prediction-to-action relation explicit in traffic control. Each intersection observes queue lengths, moving vehicles, waiting time, and occupancy at its own and neighboring lanes. A graph $G$ represents their connectivity, and a history window preserves the observations and signal configurations that preceded the current decision:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{T}_t=\{(O_{t_i},a_{t_i}):t-\Delta t<t_i<t\},\qquad
  X=\operatorname{Prompt}(O_t,G,\mathcal{T}_t,D).
  \label{eq:collmlight-state}
\end{equation}
For complex coordination, the LLM analyzes current traffic, predicts neighboring states under candidate configurations, and selects a signal:
\begin{equation}
  Y_{\mathrm{ana}},\{\widehat{O}^{a}_{t+1}:a\in\mathcal{A}\},\widehat{a}=f_{\mathrm{LLM}}(X).
  \label{eq:collmlight-action}
\end{equation}
Here $D$ supplies the control instructions. Simpler situations omit some reasoning stages according to the number of critical neighboring lanes; simulation-derived data support policy fine-tuning. The resulting action changes future traffic observations, so evaluation measures policy outcomes in the control environment. Portfolio and traffic applications both require reasoning about future outcomes; each instantiates its own state, feasible actions, feedback timing, and utility. A decision-support Harness must connect these quantities to an executable policy and measure the consequences of that policy over time.

A new decision-support Agent should specify these quantities before choosing its reasoning workflow. The numerical or language prediction supplies evidence; the action contract determines how that evidence becomes a position, a control signal, or a recommendation. Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} connects each resulting artifact to an appropriate evaluation protocol.

The comparison yields five observations. First, fixed pipelines are reported where the candidate operations and acceptance criterion are known before execution, as in model-library forecasting, deployment-time series generation, and trading role hierarchies; feedback-driven control appears where the required evidence emerges during analysis, as in interval refinement, context-dependent forecast revision, and event search. Third, evidence for memory is largely within distribution: TimeClaw-Harness and AnomaMind build memory from training splits of the evaluated benchmarks, FinCon updates beliefs across episodes on the same assets, and among these systems only MetaCaster evaluates reused learned artifacts on out-of-domain datasets. Fourth, system gains are rarely separable from model scale, tool access, and compute. \section{Infrastructure and Benchmarks for Time Series Agents}
\label{sec:evaluation}

Evaluation completes the construction of a Time Series Agent: the benchmark must expose the information, actions, and output requirements that the deployed workflow will encounter. AION \citep{aion2026}, Section~3.1, expresses this connection through a task file $\phi$, an accessible workspace $\mathcal{W}$, and a validator $\mathcal{V}$:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{T}=(\phi,\mathcal{W},\mathcal{V}),\qquad
  \phi=(\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{access}},\Sigma,\mathcal{O},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{A}),\qquad
  (g,s)=\mathcal{V}(o).
  \label{eq:aion-task-contract}
\end{equation}
The task file specifies access, prior knowledge, output form, constraints, and metrics. The validator returns validity checks $g$ and scores $s$ for candidate output $o$. This separation lets an evaluator identify both an incorrectly executed analysis and a valid analysis with poor predictive performance. The reviewed benchmarks instantiate these requirements in different ways: some grade submitted code, others test evidence discovery, and others evaluate a sequence of analytical turns or recurring data releases.

\subsection{Evaluation contract}

The unit of evaluation is an episode with a declared information boundary. Its record includes the request, visible data, tool calls, intermediate artifacts, validator decisions, revisions, final output, and resource use. Three questions connect this record to the construction framework. Did the General Harness execute the intended operations and handle failures? Did the temporal modules preserve the data window, numerical meaning, and information availability? Did the task module produce an accurate forecast, useful synthetic sample, supported diagnosis, or effective decision?

These questions require different observations. A tool-call schema check establishes that a requested operation is well formed; running the tool establishes that it executes; comparing its output against a reference establishes numerical correctness. TSAG \citep{tsag2026}, for example, evaluates invocation over hard-coded tool stubs. That protocol isolates tool selection and argument formation. TimeSeriesGym \citep{timeseriesgym2025} instead evaluates artifacts produced in an executable environment, so errors in code, data handling, and submission format affect the outcome. The choice between these protocols follows the capability being tested.

Figure~\ref{fig:evaluation-stack} follows an episode from its task contract and accessible resources through execution to the submitted artifact, and separates the three records needed to interpret its outcome. An inaccurate forecast can arise from a weak predictor, a failed tool call, or incorrectly aligned input; preserving the execution trace distinguishes these causes. Resource measurements add the cost of reaching the result and of recovering from failures.

\subsection{Existing benchmark landscape}

TimeSeriesGym \citep{timeseriesgym2025} evaluates time series ML engineering through 34 challenges from 23 sources, spanning eight temporal problem types. A challenge combines resources such as data, code, papers, and documentation with a task description and a grading function. The Agent must produce a usable artifact within the execution budget. The reported protocol uses a Docker environment and default limits of four hours and 50 steps, and distinguishes valid and reasonable submissions from task-native performance. This makes it suitable for a system expected to inspect a repository, adapt a model, and deliver runnable analysis. Figure~\ref{fig:benchmark-landscape} summarizes the resources-to-artifacts path; the grader closes the loop between the requested engineering task and the submitted result.

TimeSage-MT \citep{timesagemt2026} examines multi-turn analytical continuity. Its 240 tasks span four levels and eight domains; each task includes a visible series slice, dialogue, analysis trajectory, reference code, numerical ground truth, and output requirements. Outcome scoring measures whether the requested result is correct, while capability diagnostics examine the steps used to obtain it. Its reference system combines an LLM Planner and Reporter with deterministic validation, profiling, execution, and evaluation, and exposes 226 analytical skills across 36 categories. It therefore supports comparisons between model backbones and between runtime configurations under the same temporal workflow.

TimeSage-EV \citep{timesageev2026} adds a second timescale: the analysis recurs as new evidence becomes available. The reported snapshot contains 60 scenarios across six domains and 1,485 scenario-period instances spanning February 2023 to May 2026. At each period the Agent receives the permitted evidence and produces structured answers and reports, while the next target release is withheld for evaluation. The benchmark measures keypoint accuracy and coverage together with report faithfulness and quality, and compares independent with sequential contexts. A system intended for recurring economic or operational monitoring can thus be tested for its use of accumulated context as well as its current answer.

Contextual forecasting benchmarks isolate the value of external information. Dr-CiK \citep{drcik2026} provides 240 forecasting tasks and 8,849 documents with supporting evidence, multi-hop dependencies, and distractors, and it shifts dates and anonymizes entities so that the targets are not covered by current model training cut-offs. Its evaluation separates research and evidence recovery from forecast quality, allowing a designer to investigate whether a failure occurred during retrieval or after the relevant context was found. TemporalBench \citep{temporalbench2026} varies historical and future partitions and the availability of contextual or event information across four temporal understanding and forecasting settings. These controlled conditions help test when context changes a numerical or language-facing prediction.

TFRBench \citep{tfrbench2026} evaluates forecasting explanations as well as numerical forecasts on ten datasets across five domains. Its reference construction uses future events and outcomes to select explanatory trajectories; test-time systems receive historical inputs. An LLM judge compares candidate reasoning with those references along domain relevance, forecasting correctness, event relevance, and logic-to-number consistency, alongside numerical metrics such as MAE and MASE. The resulting signal measures agreement with outcome-conditioned reference reasoning. For deployment-oriented evaluation, that signal can be paired with an audit of the information actually available when the forecast was issued.

TraceBench \citep{tracebench2026} studies root-cause attribution through controlled dynamical-system interventions. An Agent identifies which simulator parameter changed, with conditions varying noise, domain context, support examples, and direct versus programmatic submission. A submitted program can also be evaluated on held-out samples. The benchmark records accuracy together with tokens, cost, elapsed time, tool use, Python execution, and plotting behavior. It therefore exposes the trade-off between exploratory analysis and diagnostic generalization. TSQBench \citep{tsqbench2026} addresses a different prerequisite: selecting trustworthy temporal data. Controlled pairs are derived from a common template and degraded along specified quality dimensions, enabling comparisons of quality identification, pairwise judgment, and downstream data-selection utility.

\subsection{Temporal question answering}

Question answering tests whether a model or Agent can interpret temporal evidence in language. TSAQA \citep{tsaqa2026} covers six analytical tasks, including anomaly detection, characterization, comparison, and temporal relationships, using true/false, multiple-choice, and puzzling formats across thirteen domains. MMTS-Bench \citep{mmtsbench2026} contains 2,424 question--answer pairs in Base, InWild, Match, and Align subsets, testing structural perception, temporal reasoning, sequence matching, and cross-modal alignment. Such evaluations are useful when a user asks about a trend, a change, or an event visible in a series. In an operational Agent, the same question can initiate a tool workflow: inspect the series, calculate a statistic, retrieve relevant context, and return an answer tied to those observations.

The appropriate extension depends on the downstream use: a forecast adds a horizon and numerical scoring rule, while diagnosis adds event localization and cause verification. \subsection{Infrastructure for reproducible evaluation}

Infrastructure determines which parts of an episode can be replayed. AION \citep{aion2026} specifies task files, workspaces, artifacts, and validation interfaces. TimeSeriesGym \citep{timeseriesgym2025} packages challenge resources and graders into executable environments. TimeClaw-Harness \citep{timeclaw2026harness} keeps numerical series in a workspace and exposes executable analytical tools, trajectories, and memory. These choices make different boundaries explicit: the task request, the execution environment, and the persistent analytical state.

ModernTSF supplies a forecasting infrastructure case around callable model and dataset resources and reproducible runs. In a new Agent system, such a substrate can supply the numerical tool layer, while the Agent adds evidence retrieval, model selection, revision, and reporting. Recording the dataset version, model configuration, tool inputs and outputs, and resulting artifacts makes these choices comparable across runs. The comparison is especially useful when the same numerical predictor is embedded in several Harness designs.

\subsection{Selecting a benchmark for a new scenario}

Resource selection follows the same task--function grid as system construction. Begin with the required artifact and the mechanism whose contribution is being tested. A numerical model library supplies callable forecasting capabilities; an execution environment establishes whether the Agent can deliver a working analysis; a controlled context benchmark measures how external evidence changes the result. Each answers a different development or evaluation question.

For the demand-planning example in Figure~\ref{fig:task-function-grid}, ModernTSF supplies model and dataset routines for the numerical tool layer. A direct forecasting run provides a baseline for the Agent's selection or revision procedure. Dr-CiK \citep{drcik2026} provides a controlled test of evidence retrieval and contextual prediction, including supporting and distracting documents. TimeSeriesGym \citep{timeseriesgym2025} tests whether the Agent can construct the required executable artifacts under a challenge's grading protocol. An inventory simulator then evaluates how the forecasts affect stockouts, holding costs, and feasible actions under the chosen operational assumptions. These evaluations isolate numerical prediction, evidence use, execution, and downstream utility within the same proposed workflow.

Other task contracts lead to different combinations. TimeSage-MT \citep{timesagemt2026} tests continuity across analytical turns, and TimeSage-EV \citep{timesageev2026} tests revision and capability reuse across evidence releases. TraceBench \citep{tracebench2026} supplies interventions with known causes for diagnostic exploration. TSQBench \citep{tsqbench2026} tests dimension-specific data-quality comparisons before downstream analysis. Selecting a resource by the capability it exposes makes the result interpretable: a quality judgment, a runnable program, a forecast, and a diagnosis are distinct artifacts with distinct evidence requirements.

Augmentation and decision support require evaluation through the use of the resulting data or actions. MERIT \citep{merit2025} evaluates representations learned from generated positive views on downstream temporal tasks, whereas MetaCaster \citep{metacaster2026} evaluates forecasters trained on synthetic series through forecasting error. The intended learner therefore determines the synthesis evaluation, with separate data for revising the generator and assessing its result. FinCon \citep{fincon2024} evaluates trading and portfolio decisions using cumulative return, Sharpe ratio, and maximum drawdown; CoLLMLight \citep{collmllight2026} evaluates traffic-signal decisions in CityFlow through average travel and waiting times. For a new application, these examples guide the choice of action space, outcome measure, and feedback schedule. An inventory workflow would similarly require an environment specifying demand, lead times, feasible actions, and costs, alongside its forecasting tests.

A component comparison should preserve the backbone, accessible tools, data boundary, and resource budget while changing the module under investigation. The record should connect the input, intermediate artifacts, checks, and final output, so that an improvement can be attributed to processing, retrieval, validation, or the task procedure. A repository supplies the means of implementing the comparison; a benchmark supplies a declared protocol for judging it.
\section{Open Problems and Focused System Analyses}

\subsection{Reusable temporal infrastructure}

The preceding comparisons suggest a research priority: make temporal operations reusable through explicit inputs, state, and validation, then test the resulting compositions under changing task requirements. The same numerical operations recur in otherwise different workflows: a forecaster inspects missing values before fitting a model, a diagnostic Agent decomposes a signal before localizing an event, and an analytical assistant aligns observations before comparing periods. TimeClaw-Harness \citep{timeclaw2026harness} and the skill library in TimeSage-MT \citep{timesagemt2026} provide concrete shared tool layers. A next step is to evaluate the transfer of individual tools under changes in task, sampling frequency, units, and data availability. The module-level organization makes this comparison possible because each operation retains its input, output, scope, and source evidence.

A shared tool also needs a stable record of what it operated on. AION \citep{aion2026} connects the task file, workspace, and validator; TimeSage-EV \citep{timesageev2026} makes evidence release time part of the recurring analysis. Together these designs motivate interfaces that carry the observation window, data version, and admissible context through execution. A practical test is to replay an earlier analysis after a later release has arrived and verify that the original evidence boundary is preserved.

\subsection{Open problems}

\noindent\textbf{Connecting temporal perception to executable analysis.} ChatTS \citep{chatts2025} learns to interpret temporal attributes and language instructions, while tool-based systems such as TS-Agent \citep{tsagent2025} explicitly compute analytical evidence. Their combination raises a concrete allocation problem: which judgments should be handled by learned temporal perception, and which should trigger a numerical tool? Matched-backbone experiments can vary the tool policy and compare numerical error, evidence quality, and execution cost. This would identify where model adaptation reduces tool dependence and where explicit computation remains valuable.

\noindent\textbf{Memory under changing information.} TimeSage-MT \citep{timesagemt2026} tests continuity within a dialogue, whereas TimeSage-EV \citep{timesageev2026} tests recurring analysis as releases accumulate. These settings distinguish remembering a useful result from deciding whether it is still applicable. Future memory evaluations can preserve an old explanation while changing its supporting observation, release vintage, or regime, then test whether the Agent revalidates, revises, or retires that explanation. This links memory quality to the temporal validity of later actions.

\noindent\textbf{Transferring learned procedures.} TimeClaw-Harness \citep{timeclaw2026harness} promotes generated tools using held-out execution, and TimeClaw-EEL \citep{timeclaw2026explore} distills successful trajectories into external experience. The remaining question is how far each acquired capability transfers. Evaluation can hold out a task family, a domain, or a temporal regime while keeping the base LLM fixed. Recording tool identity, training episodes, and promotion criteria makes it possible to distinguish reusable procedure learning from repeated adaptation to similar instances. TimeSage-EV \citep{timesageev2026} provides a concrete availability rule through next-period skill activation.

\noindent\textbf{Evidence-sensitive evaluation.} Dr-CiK \citep{drcik2026} controls supporting and distracting documents, TraceBench \citep{tracebench2026} controls diagnostic interventions, and TFRBench \citep{tfrbench2026} scores reasoning against outcome-conditioned references. These protocols address different relationships between evidence and result. Combining controlled evidence changes with an executable trace can test whether a correct answer was supported by information available to the Agent and whether removing a relevant observation changes the conclusion appropriately. Such tests would make reasoning evaluation more directly connected to the operations that produced the answer.

\noindent\textbf{Closing the prediction-to-decision loop.} Forecast quality matters through its use. FinCon \citep{fincon2024} couples analytical evidence to portfolio positions, and CoLLMLight \citep{collmllight2026} connects local traffic observations to network control. These settings introduce action costs, risk, delayed feedback, and effects on subsequent observations. Evaluations should compare the complete decision procedure under the same information and action constraints, recording both predictive performance and the resulting utility. A method that changes recommendations also needs evaluation across repeated decisions, where its own actions can alter the future data distribution.

\subsection{Design implications}

\noindent\textbf{ChatTS: selecting a temporal interface.} ChatTS \citep{chatts2025} makes the numerical input interface a design variable. An analytical assistant can compare direct text serialization with a learned temporal encoder while keeping its runtime, tools, and task inputs fixed. Preserving scale metadata in both conditions allows the comparison to examine magnitude-sensitive questions as well as temporal shape. Answer quality, evidence grounding, and tool use then reveal how the input representation changes the analysis.

\noindent\textbf{TimeClaw-EEL: learning from executed trajectories.} TimeClaw-EEL \citep{timeclaw2026explore} develops an Explore--Compare--Distill--Reinject loop with a fixed underlying LLM. Alternative tool trajectories are executed, scored, and distilled into external experience. For task instance $x$, the method selects the best valid execution using the task loss:
\begin{equation}
  q(\pi_k;x)=-L_{\tau}(\widehat{y}_k,y^{\star}),\qquad
  \pi^{\star}(x)=\arg\max_{\pi_k\in\Pi_{\mathrm{valid}}(x)}q(\pi_k;x).
  \label{eq:timeclaw-compare}
\end{equation}
Here $\pi_k$ denotes an executed candidate trajectory, $L_{\tau}$ the loss for task type $\tau$, and $y^{\star}$ the exploration target. The scored comparison occurs during experience construction; later inference receives the retained experience. The comparison is summarized and cleaned, and the resulting evidence updates the task-scoped experience state $A_s$:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  n_x&=\operatorname{Summarize}\!\left(x,\Pi(x),\{q(\pi_k;x)\}_{k=1}^{K},\pi^{\star}(x)\right),\\
  e_x&=\operatorname{Clean}(n_x),\qquad
  A_s^{(m+1)}=\operatorname{Distill}\!\left(A_s^{(m)},e_x\right).
\end{aligned}
  \label{eq:timeclaw-distill}
\end{equation}
The experience-update mechanism can use different task losses and is recorded as General Harness. Individual analytical operations retain their temporal or task-specific scopes. Comparing the initial and updated experience states on held-out episodes, with the backbone and tool access fixed, measures the contribution of retained experience. Separate tests across new instances, domains, and task types establish the scope over which that content remains useful.

\noindent\textbf{ModernTSF: connecting numerical models to an Agent.} ModernTSF\footnote{ModernTSF repository: \url{https://github.com/Diaugeia/ModernTSF}.} can hold the numerical forecasting substrate constant while the Agent workflow changes. A direct forecasting run and an Agent-controlled run can share the same model routines, data windows, and computational budget; the latter adds model selection or revision. Recording the chosen configurations, validation outcomes, and final errors makes the comparison informative about the selection procedure and its execution cost.

\section{Conclusion}

This survey reviews Time Series Agents through LLM-side capabilities, harness architectures, and evaluation, organized by a module-level taxonomy of General Harness, General Time Series Harness, and Task-Specific Harness. The scope of reuse supports three design judgments for a new scenario: which runtime mechanisms can be retained, which operations require temporal semantics such as availability time and chronology guards, and which procedures must be rebuilt around the task objective and its feedback. Effective Time Series Agents require alignment between LLM capabilities, harness responsibilities, and evaluation protocols: model capabilities must be supported by appropriate temporal interfaces, execution must respect information boundaries, and evaluation must distinguish workflow validity from task and decision utility. Comparisons across forecasting, synthesis, anomaly diagnosis, and decision support show where this alignment remains incomplete and motivate research on transferable temporal procedures, experience under changing regimes, and evaluation of downstream actions. The accompanying structured literature records support continued comparison as new modules and tasks emerge.
