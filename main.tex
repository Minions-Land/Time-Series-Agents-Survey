\abstract{Time series analysis supports forecasting, diagnosis, and decision making in changing environments. Time Series Agents carry out these analyses through workflows in which an LLM reads the state, calls tools, and interprets the results. Building such an Agent requires deciding which mechanisms can be reused from a general Agent runtime, which need temporal semantics such as information availability, and which must be redesigned around a task. Existing surveys classify whole papers or systems by model adaptation, reasoning, or task family, leaving module-level reuse decisions implicit. This survey addresses that gap with one decomposition: a Time Series Agent consists of an LLM, a General Harness, and a Time Series Harness, which splits into a General Time Series Harness shared across tasks and a Task-Specific Harness bound to one task. We assign every module of each reviewed work to one of these terms by its scope of reuse. The LLM acts as the caller of the Harness, while each Harness term has a vocabulary grounded in prior work: mechanism dimensions for the General Harness, temporal functions of the availability record for the General Time Series Harness, and a task grid of output modes on the two sides of the current time for the Task-Specific Harness, with one evaluation criterion per term. Applied to the literature, this decomposition shows that most works automate an existing analysis, that solutions to time series requirements are rarely tested and mostly stay inside task modules, and that projection and hindsight decision have no evaluated output. Together, the decomposition and module-level classification offer a common basis for building Time Series Agents from reusable modules and evaluating each term against the requirement it serves.}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Time series analysis supports decisions in environments that change as new observations arrive. Demand planning uses forecasts to set inventory, equipment monitoring uses anomalies to schedule maintenance, and traffic control uses current measurements to select actions \citep{lim2021deepts,collmllight2026}. In each setting, a numerical prediction or diagnostic result is one part of a longer workflow: the system must acquire data, inspect temporal context, call analytical tools, validate the result, and pass an admissible output to the next decision. Recent Agent systems make these steps executable through workspaces, evidence checks, and tool feedback \citep{tsagent2025,aion2026}. Research on such Agents is growing quickly, and its emphasis is shifting from adapting the model to engineering the surrounding runtime. Contributions to every part of an Agent grow approximately exponentially, and Harness modules now outnumber modifications to the LLM (Figure~\ref{fig:contribution-trend}).

The construction problem is therefore broader than adapting an LLM to a time series. A developer must decide which interaction, memory, planning, and execution mechanisms can be carried over from a general Agent; which operations need temporal semantics such as order, windows, units, and availability time; and which procedures must be redesigned around a task objective. General Harness research supplies reusable runtime mechanisms \citep{ning2026codeharness,lin2026agenticharness,guo2026harnesssurvey}, while temporal workspaces and contextual forecasting protocols constrain the information and numerical state that those mechanisms may use \citep{timeclaw2026explore,drcik2026}. The output contract then changes the required feedback: a forecast is judged at a future horizon, an anomaly workflow must localize and support a suspicious interval, and a decision must connect evidence to an action and its consequences \citep{chen2026tsagentsurvey}.

This survey answers that construction question with one formula: a Time Series Agent is an LLM, a General Harness, and a Time Series Harness, and the Time Series Harness is a General Time Series Harness plus a Task-Specific Harness (Eq.~\eqref{eq:tsa-formula}, Section~\ref{sec:what-is}). The LLM is the caller: it reads the state, makes function calls, and interprets their results. The General Harness (GH) supplies the runtime that any Agent needs, whatever its domain. The Time Series Harness supplies what that runtime does not know about time series: its modules either hold temporal semantics shared across tasks (General Time Series Harness, GTS) or are bound to the output and objective of one task (Task-Specific Harness, TSK). Statistical models and time series foundation models are tools within the Harness rather than part of the LLM term. The three parts of the title follow the formula: LLMs are its first term, Harness architectures are the other two, and evaluation checks each term.

\subsection{Position relative to existing surveys}

Time series surveys supply complementary accounts of forecasting concepts and deep architectures \citep{lim2021deepts,wang2024tslib,cheng2026tsfsurvey}, of anomaly and change point detection \citep{darban2024anomaly,aminikhanghahi2017changepoint}, of foundation and large models \citep{liang2024foundation,ye2024tsfmsurvey,jin2026largemodels}, of LLM adaptation and cross-modality modeling \citep{zhang2024llmts,jiang2024empowering,liu2025crossmodality}, of multimodal temporal modeling and synthetic data \citep{jiang2025multimodal,liu2025synthetic}, and of question answering over time series \citep{li2026tsqasurvey}. Our construction question links these bodies of work: which general mechanisms remain reusable, which require temporal semantics, and which become tied to a particular task?

Six recent surveys review Time Series Agents directly. Whereas those surveys assign a category to a whole paper or system according to how it works or which problem it solves, we ask a constructive question: for a new scenario, which parts can be reused from a general Agent, which need temporal semantics shared across tasks, and which must be rebuilt for the task. Specifically, Zhang et al.\ code each paper on four axes: representation, model adaptation, workflow autonomy, and analytical task \citep{promptstoagents2026}. Chang et al.\ place each paper at one reasoning topology and one primary objective: traditional analysis, explanation and understanding, causal inference and decision making, or generation \citep{changsurvey2026}. Chen et al.\ group systems by problem family (forecasting, synthesis, anomaly diagnosis, and decision support) and compare their architecture, tools, and memory \citep{chen2026tsagentsurvey}. Xu et al.\ review Agents for forecasting and prediction by architecture (standalone, augmented with tools, or hybrid) and examine their training, evaluation, and applications \citep{xu2026forecastagentsurvey}. Hu et al.\ trace a progression from foundation models through translators and reasoners to Agents, organizing capabilities into five layers: perception, reasoning, planning and action, memory and knowledge, and temporal world models, with reliability across all five \citep{hu2026agenticts}. Hu et al.\ also write the Agent as a tuple of these capability components; our formula instead divides the Harness by the scope over which a module is reused, so one capability, such as memory, can fall in any of the three Harness terms. Manjunath and Pruefer review Agents for renewable energy forecasting through six layers of the forecasting pipeline, from data acquisition to Agent reasoning and reporting \citep{manjunath2026renewableagents}.

Our constructive focus yields six differences from these system-level and paper-level classifications (Table~\ref{tab:direct-prior-comparison}). First, the unit is a module, assigned by the scope over which it can be reused, so one paper can contribute to several terms. Second, the LLM is the caller of the Harness, and forecasting models are tools within the Harness. Third, temporal validity is the function of a shared Harness term, applied in data management, processing, context, and validation, rather than only a concern of evaluation. Fourth, the task grid crosses output modes with the current time and shows two well defined cells without an evaluated task output. Fifth, evaluation assigns one criterion to each term. Sixth, the formula yields a construction order, reference modules for each cell, and an assessment of a new work, with every classification linked to a source passage.

At the same time, several choices in our framework build on precedents in these six surveys and in software engineering. The objectives of Chang et al.\ already separate decision making and generation from analysis, as our output modes do. Zhang et al.\ code time series foundation models as tools, and Xu et al.\ and Hu et al.\ describe Agents that call forecasting models as tools; we make this caller view the organizing basis. Hu et al.\ separate the closed loop that Time Series Agents share with language Agents from the temporal pressures placed on that loop, which parallels our division between the General Harness and the General Time Series Harness. Xu et al.\ define the information set available before the decision time and treat leakage and contamination in more depth than we do; our availability record extends that set with the event time and the availability time of each value. Chen et al.\ link documented failure modes to design choices and implementation checks. Beyond time series, decomposing language Agents into modules has precedent in cognitive architectures \citep{sumers2024coala} and in surveys that separate profile, memory, planning, and action modules \citep{wang2024agentsurvey}. Ordering those modules by their scope of reuse follows commonality and variability analysis in software product line engineering \citep{kang1990foda,clements2001spl}. Zhang et al., Chang et al., and Hu et al.\ also maintain public repositories that list each reviewed work with its category \citep{promptstoagents2026,changsurvey2026,hu2026agenticts}.

\subsection{One formula, one assignment rule}

Our principal contribution is to apply Eq.~\eqref{eq:tsa-formula} at the level of modules. We classify concrete modules rather than whole papers, assigning each Harness module to the most specific of the three Harness terms whose condition it meets based on its inputs, state, operations, feedback, and outputs (Table~\ref{tab:layer-rules}). One paper can therefore contribute modules to several terms: a forecasting Agent may reuse a General Harness planner, add a General Time Series Harness availability check, and define a Task-Specific Harness model selection procedure. Existing approaches develop both action selection through tool feedback \citep{tsagent2025} and temporal interpretation through model adaptation \citep{jin2024timellm,chatts2025}; the formula places these contributions in the LLM term and the Harness terms they use.

The formula is the only concept we introduce. Every vocabulary is adopted from prior work or derived from the definition of its term (Table~\ref{tab:term-basis}), and the LLM term needs none because every LLM-side contribution modifies the same caller. The General Harness uses five mechanism dimensions synthesized from Agent and Harness research \citep{sumers2024coala,ning2026codeharness,lin2026agenticharness}. The General Time Series Harness uses four temporal functions derived from the availability record of a temporal state, whose event time and availability time correspond to valid time and transaction time in temporal databases \citep{snodgrass1985taxonomy,jensen1999temporal}. These four functions are the structures, operators, and integrity rules of a data model \citep{codd1980datamodels}, together with the scope of the record. The Task-Specific Harness uses a task grid with two standard axes: the output mode, defined by how an output is checked \citep{hastie2009esl,gneiting2007scoring}, and whether the output concerns the record observed by the current time or what comes after the current time \citep{wiener1949extrapolation}. Each cell of this task grid holds standard task names \citep{wu2023timesnet,kong2025timemqa}. The four evaluation criteria check one term each. Each Harness vocabulary is divided once more by a single criterion of the same origin (Tables~\ref{tab:component-vocab}, \ref{tab:gts-vocab}, and \ref{tab:tsk-vocab}), and Table~\ref{tab:cell-lookup} lists reference modules in every layer for each cell of the task grid.

The formula also gives a way to assess a new work. We place a work's modules into the four terms and check each placement against the scope the authors claim, because a general mechanism can be presented as task-specific and a task procedure as general. The resulting map reveals the work's contribution: automating an existing analysis, adding an implementation where many exist, supplying a module where none exists, or consolidating a task solution into a shared time series module (Section~\ref{sec:task-impact}).

Applied to the reviewed works, the formula yields four findings. First, most works automate an existing analysis, letting the LLM and the General Harness run it with existing time series tools. Second, solutions to time series requirements stay inside tasks: fewer than half of the works that propose methods test the requirement their modules address, such as information availability or regime change, and most tested solutions are task modules. Third, generality is claimed more often than shown: only about half of the shared modules are applied by their own sources to tasks in at least two cells of the task grid. Fourth, the task grid exposes two cells that Agents produce only internally: projection and hindsight decision appear inside decision procedures and reflection memories but have no evaluated task output, and few forecasting works report a probabilistic forecast. Section~\ref{sec:task-impact} gives the counts.

Each section of the paper answers the next design or evaluation question in this sequence.

\subsection{Contributions}

The remainder of this paper follows the terms of Eq.~\eqref{eq:tsa-formula}. Sections~\ref{sec:intro} and~\ref{sec:what-is} define the problem, the Time Series Agent, the module assignment rule, and the vocabulary of each term. Section~\ref{sec:llm-side} reviews the LLM side, and Sections~\ref{sec:general-harness}--\ref{sec:task-specific-harness} build the Harness from general to task-specific: Section~\ref{sec:general-harness} synthesizes the General Harness into five mechanism dimensions (with control, verification, and coordination discussed within planning and orchestration), while Sections~\ref{sec:general-ts-harness} and~\ref{sec:task-specific-harness} examine how these mechanisms acquire shared temporal semantics in the General Time Series Harness and task-bound procedures in the Task-Specific Harness. Section~\ref{sec:task-impact} applies the formula to assess the reviewed works and new works; Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} evaluates the terms through infrastructure and benchmarks; and Sections~\ref{sec:open-problems} and~\ref{sec:conclusion} derive open problems and conclusions. Module assignments, source passages, and official resources are recorded in source-linked Paper Cards in a public repository.\footnote{Survey and companion resources: \url{https://github.com/Minions-Land/Time-Series-Agents-Survey}.} Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{One formula applied at module level.} We assign every module of each reviewed work to one term of Eq.~\eqref{eq:tsa-formula} by its scope of reuse, treating the LLM as the caller of the Harness and describing each Harness term with an established vocabulary, including a task grid that crosses the four output modes (regression, classification, generation, and decision making) with the current time.
  \item \textbf{A comparative synthesis by term.} We analyze representative methods through their mechanisms, assumptions, feedback, and evaluation. Across the task grid, we list reference modules in every layer for each cell (Table~\ref{tab:cell-lookup}) and compare eleven complete systems to show which modules carry over to a new scenario and which must be rebuilt (Table~\ref{tab:representative-systems}).
  \item \textbf{An assessment of contribution with source-linked records.} We place a new work's modules into the terms, check its claimed scope against the assignment rule, and read its contribution from the resulting map, from automation to consolidation into a shared module (Section~\ref{sec:task-impact}), with Paper Cards linking every module assignment to its supporting passage and official implementation (Appendix~\ref{app:full-ledger}).
  \item \textbf{Findings on the state of the field.} We show that most reviewed works automate existing analyses, that solutions to time series requirements are rarely tested and mostly stay inside task modules, that reuse is demonstrated for only about half of the shared modules, and that projection and hindsight decision have no evaluated task output, from which the open problems of Section~\ref{sec:open-problems} follow.
\end{itemize}




\section{What Is a Time Series Agent?}
\label{sec:what-is}

A Time Series Agent is a system in which an LLM operates through a reusable runtime and modules specific to time series. Equation~\eqref{eq:tsa-formula} states this decomposition, the central concept of our survey: it specializes a general LLM-based Agent and splits the Time Series Harness into shared and task-specific terms.

\begin{tcolorbox}[
  colback=surveyWarn,
  colframe=surveyWarn,
  boxrule=0pt,
  arc=7pt,
  left=12pt,
  right=12pt,
  top=10pt,
  bottom=10pt
]
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\textbf{LLM-based Agent} &\approx \text{LLM}+\text{Harness},\\
\textbf{Time Series Agent} &\approx \text{LLM}+\text{General Harness}+\text{Time Series Harness},\\
\textbf{Time Series Harness} &= \text{General Time Series Harness}+\text{Task-Specific Harness}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:tsa-formula}
\end{equation}
\end{tcolorbox}

The LLM interprets the request and current observations, chooses the next tool or environment action, and uses feedback to revise its plan. The General Harness supplies interface and interaction, memory and context, tools and execution, planning and orchestration, and optimization and evolution. The Time Series Harness manages temporal state, operations, context, and validation. Within it, statistical models, time series foundation models, databases, simulators, retrieval systems, and evaluators operate as callable tools. Such components can also sit outside a single Agent: a workbench exposes shared knowledge, data, and experiment tools to any compatible Agent, so improvements persist across Agents and teams \citep{tsflab2026}.

At an abstract level, the runtime follows the interleaved reasoning and acting loop of ReAct \citep{yao2023react}, which \citet{xu2026forecastagentsurvey} also use to formalize forecasting Agents:
\begin{equation}
  a_k = \pi_\theta(s_k), \qquad
  s_{k+1} = \mathcal{H}(s_k,a_k,o_{k+1}),
  \label{eq:agent-loop}
\end{equation}
where $s_k$ is the visible state at execution step $k$, $a_k$ is a tool or environment action, $o_{k+1}$ is the returned observation, and $\mathcal{H}$ is the Harness transition. Time Series Harness modules add temporal type information to each state, action, and observation.

Each term is described with one vocabulary, listed in Table~\ref{tab:term-basis} with its basis. None of these vocabularies introduces a new classification: the mechanism dimensions come from prior Agent surveys, the task names inside each cell of the task grid are standard in time series analysis, the two axes of the grid combine standard output modes with the classical division at the current time, and the temporal functions and evaluation criteria follow directly from the definitions of their terms. The LLM term needs no vocabulary, because every LLM-side contribution changes the same caller. Each module is assigned to a single term of Eq.~\eqref{eq:tsa-formula} and counted once there by its unique identifier, even though it may carry multiple descriptive annotations for its mechanism dimensions, temporal functions, or tasks.

\noindent\textbf{Scope of inclusion.} Beyond describing individual terms, the formula fixes which works are in scope and what role each plays. A work that modifies only the LLM term is an LLM-side technique, as in Time-LLM \citep{jin2024timellm} and ChatTS \citep{chatts2025}. A work that contributes Harness modules is a fixed workflow if its sequence of model and tool calls is predetermined, and a feedback-driven Agent if returned observations $o_{k+1}$ alter subsequent actions $a_k$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:agent-loop}. Benchmarks, environments, and datasets evaluate the terms and are recorded as resources. Among the 162 works in scope, about two thirds (65\%) are feedback-driven Agents, while fixed workflows (13\%), LLM-side techniques (11\%), and resources (10\%) each account for about a tenth.

Generalizing this example, a developer builds a Time Series Agent for a new scenario in five steps:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Specify the scenario.} State the data sources, event and availability times, channels, units, missingness policy, and the decision or analysis that follows the output.
  \item \textbf{Write the task contract.} Define the output, horizon, admissible information, constraints, evaluation metric, and review authority.
  \item \textbf{Select the runtime.} Choose General Harness mechanisms for interface and interaction, memory and context, tools and execution, planning and orchestration, and optimization and evolution.
  \item \textbf{Add temporal semantics.} Introduce temporal state, operations, provenance, chronology guards, and validators wherever a generic runtime leaves the meaning ambiguous.
  \item \textbf{Instantiate and evaluate.} Add task-specific modules, then evaluate process validity, temporal validity, task performance, resource use, failures, revision, and downstream decision utility.
\end{enumerate}

These five steps follow Eq.~\eqref{eq:tsa-formula} from left to right. A single General Harness runtime can support financial forecasting, traffic prediction, industrial diagnosis, or demand planning, while the temporal and task contracts determine which Time Series Harness modules and benchmarks must change.

\section{LLM Side: The Caller of the Harness}
\label{sec:llm-side}

In Eq.~\eqref{eq:agent-loop}, the LLM is the policy $\pi_\theta$: it reads the visible state $s_k$, selects the next action $a_k$, usually a function call with its arguments, and interprets the returned observation $o_{k+1}$. Every LLM-side contribution we review serves this role, so the LLM side is not divided into categories. Two questions organize it instead: what training adds to the model that makes the calls, and which time series capabilities remain with the tools it calls. Training has been used to let the model read time series directly (Section~\ref{sec:llm-read}) and to make it a better caller of temporal workflows (Section~\ref{sec:llm-caller}). Time series foundation models, by contrast, enter the Agent as forecasting tools (Section~\ref{sec:llm-tsfm}). A model that perceives time series natively, as multimodal LLMs perceive images, is the prospect that connects these lines (Section~\ref{sec:llm-native}). Figure~\ref{fig:llm-contributions} places these contributions in the interaction loop: training changes the model, while execution, state, and feedback remain in the Harness.

\subsection{Reading time series inside the model}
\label{sec:llm-read}

Even when trained on temporal data, an adapted model still acts as the caller once placed in an Agent. Temporal training improves what the caller perceives and how it reasons about a result, whereas operations that must be computed exactly and audited, including statistics over declared windows, forecasts with calibrated uncertainty, and checks of availability time, remain tool calls in the Harness (Sections~\ref{sec:general-ts-harness} and~\ref{sec:task-specific-harness}). Existing LLM-side adaptations span two stages: input interfaces that map temporal patches into the language backbone, and post-training supervision that links signal structure to questions and reasoning traces.

At the input interface, Time-LLM \citep{jin2024timellm} makes a frozen language backbone operate on temporal patches. It constructs a compact set of text prototypes from the pretrained word embedding space and reprograms each patch by cross-attention, with the patch embeddings as queries and the prototypes as keys and values. The text prototypes provide combinations of language space cues for local temporal patterns. After aggregation and projection, the reprogrammed patches and a Prompt-as-Prefix context (describing the task, domain, and input statistics) enter the frozen backbone. A linear output head projects the backbone states to the forecast, and only the input reprogramming and output projection are trained under prediction loss. LLM4TS \citep{chang2024llm4ts} likewise adapts a pretrained language model to temporal inputs, showing that the learned numerical interface can be trained before the Agent workflow is assembled.

ChatTS \citep{chatts2025} aligns time series patches directly with text tokens for conversational analysis. A five-layer MLP maps fixed-size patches into the text embedding space and inserts them among the text tokens at their corresponding series positions. Its value-preserving normalization rescales each series to the unit interval and writes the minimum and range into the prompt, so the original values stay recoverable: a statement about magnitude can refer to the original scale while the encoder processes normalized values. In an Agent, the input layer must preserve this correspondence between a numerical series and its language-facing description. Treating this numerical input interface as a controlled design variable lets an evaluator compare direct text serialization against a learned temporal encoder while holding the runtime, tools, and task inputs fixed. When both conditions preserve scale metadata, the comparison can test questions that depend on magnitude as well as temporal shape; differences in answer quality, evidence grounding, and tool calls then isolate how the input representation shapes the analysis.

Agentic Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Time Series Analysis \citep{agenticrag2024} conditions specialized language models through differentiable prompt pools. Retrieved continuous prompt embeddings are concatenated with numerical inputs and passed through a learnable projection. Because the prompt pool is trained with the predictor, it adapts the model's internal representation to historical temporal patterns. Such internal conditioning contrasts with external episode retrieval in the Harness, which preserves inspectable records at runtime.

For numerical forecasting, Time-LLM \citep{jin2024timellm} reprograms a frozen backbone, whereas ChatTS \citep{chatts2025} learns a direct interface from patches to language tokens for conversational analysis. Time-LLM fixes where numerical patches meet language prototypes, and ChatTS fixes the scale information that must accompany normalized inputs. The choice therefore depends on the required output: a numerical forecast head or answers to temporal questions. In both cases, the Harness supplies window construction, channel and unit metadata, consistent preprocessing, and routing between the learned interface and downstream tools.

Once an input encoder maps patches into the backbone, post-training supervision teaches the model to link signal structure to questions and explanations. ChatTS \citep{chatts2025} generates synthetic series with selected temporal attributes, pairs them with matching text, and evolves instructions for understanding and reasoning. Controlling the generated attributes supplies known temporal properties around which analytical instructions can be built.

\textit{Towards Time Series Reasoning with LLMs} \citep{chow2024tsreasoning} follows a related route by combining temporal encoding with reasoning supervision. Its encoder warm-up and subsequent language model adaptation connect temporal perception to explicit reasoning traces. Time-R1 \citep{timer12025} specializes this approach for forecasting: supervised warm-up teaches structured reasoning and complete forecast outputs, followed by reinforcement fine-tuning with format, length, and numerical feedback. Its accuracy reward maps normalized forecast error to a bounded signal that equals one at zero error and approaches zero as error grows; because this transformation compresses differences among large errors, the normalization of predictions and targets determines the reward's effective sensitivity. The reward combines forecast accuracy with format, output length, and temporal structure. The accompanying GRIP optimizer uses nonuniform sampling and adaptive weighting over reasoning paths. Time-R1 \citep{timer12025} evaluates forecasting with MSE and MAE and ablates its reward components (Tables~2, 4, and 6) to compare numerical performance and training choices.

ChatAD \citep{chatad2026} (Section~4.2) trains an anomaly reasoning model using supervised fine-tuning with LoRA followed by TKTO preference optimization. Feedback combines LLM assessments of format compliance, task correctness, and fluency with human preference into a composite score. Rather than splitting responses into preferred and rejected sets at a hard threshold, ChatAD weights the desirable and undesirable response losses by a sigmoid of the score's distance from the threshold, which reduces label noise near the threshold (Section~4.2.2, Eqs.~3--5) but requires a consistent scoring rubric calibrated around it. In evaluation, ChatAD \citep{chatad2026} (Section~4.3.2, Tables~3--4) separates detection accuracy, F1, and false positive rate from LLM-judged open-ended reasoning scores and format parsing rates. Multi-agent synthetic dialogues generate the training data, while the resulting ChatAD model acts as the single anomaly reasoner at inference (see Section~\ref{sec:task-specific-harness} for how AnomaMind \citep{anomamind2026} pairs a trained detector with an external evidence-gathering workflow).

Across these post-training methods, developers choose among supervising attribute descriptions (ChatTS \citep{chatts2025}), rewarding forecast outcomes (Time-R1 \citep{timer12025}), and weighting responses by graded preferences (ChatAD \citep{chatad2026}). Each training signal requires matching Harness support: preserving alignment between series values and text tokens, parsing and chronologically scoring forecasts, or calibrating a preference rubric. While ChatTS \citep{chatts2025} answers questions about the shape and scale of a series and Time-R1 \citep{timer12025} emits a forecast together with its reasoning, neither replaces the exact Harness tools on which the Agent's verified output depends.

Evaluating an adapted caller therefore requires testing each claimed capability separately (Figure~\ref{fig:llm-contributions}, bottom). Forecast accuracy measures error on held-out values; explanation faithfulness tests whether stated reasoning reflects the model's actual computation; evidence grounding verifies that claims match the supplied series and documents; and action selection quality measures the outcome of feasible, useful tool or environment actions. These distinctions motivate complementary checks: held-out numerical errors, controlled interventions on claimed reasoning factors, verification of cited observations and their availability times, and evaluation of actions by executing them. Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} connects these requirements to evaluation resources.

\subsection{Training the caller}
\label{sec:llm-caller}

Beyond reading series, post-training can directly optimize the model that selects or assesses workflow actions. TSOrchestra, introduced in \textit{Conversational Time Series Foundation Models} \citep{conversationaltsfm2025}, trains an LLM judge to control iterative ensemble optimization. While a numerical optimizer proposes nonnegative model weights that sum to one, the judge inspects metrics, weights, and optimization history to decide whether to continue searching or accept a configuration. Supervised fine-tuning trains the judge on real trajectories and augmented boundary cases, and subsequent GRPO training incorporates held-out forecasting performance and explanation feedback.

CastFSR \citep{castfsr2026} trains a broader policy over the Fast--Slow--Reflect workflow. Supervised distillation teaches executable stage sequences, followed by multi-turn reinforcement learning over complete trajectories. Training checks tool calls, stage order, timestamps, and completed forecasts before applying forecasting feedback. At inference, the policy still interacts with the numerical forecasting and contextual retrieval tools described in Section~\ref{sec:task-specific-harness}. TimeART \citep{timeart2026} similarly combines a trained reasoner with an analytical toolkit, connecting policy learning to explicit temporal operations.

These methods train the function calling role directly. TSOrchestra \citep{conversationaltsfm2025} is useful when a judge must accept or continue numerical ensemble optimization; CastFSR \citep{castfsr2026} learns decisions across a prescribed tool workflow; TimeART \citep{timeart2026} learns tool use from experience and reflection trajectories. A developer therefore selects both a model and its required feedback interface. The Harness must expose optimizer histories or tool observations, retain intermediate state, enforce valid calls and temporal boundaries, and record outcomes for evaluation. Action quality is tested through executed trajectories and their task outcomes, while any accompanying explanation is assessed separately.

\subsection{Time series foundation models as tools}
\label{sec:llm-tsfm}

Time series foundation models such as TimesFM \citep{das2024timesfm}, Chronos \citep{ansari2024chronos}, and Moirai \citep{woo2024moirai} are pretrained on large collections of series and map a context window to a forecast. In a Time Series Agent they do not belong to the LLM term: they take a series and return values, whereas the LLM decides which model to call, on which window, and whether to accept the result. Last-Mile \citep{lastmile2026} lets an Agent revise a TimesFM baseline forecast through constrained, evidence-backed actions; MoiraiAgent \citep{moiraiagent2026} lets an LLM select a forecasting expert; TimeCopilot \citep{timecopilot2025} exposes statistical, machine learning, and foundation models behind one forecasting interface and combines them in ensembles; and the evolved forecasting code of \citet{promptevolutionforecast2026} receives a default TabPFN forecast before a final LLM pass.

Because a foundation model is a tool, it is also replaceable. MetaCaster \citep{metacaster2026} notes that foundation models remain uneconomical in resource-constrained settings; its Harness instead generates few-shot training series and trains lightweight forecasters as scheduled jobs. TSFLab \citep{tsflab2026} moves forecasting models out of any single Agent: its agent-agnostic workbench integrates 323 forecasting models and 93 datasets that any compatible Agent can call. The choice among foundation, statistical, and lightweight forecasters is therefore a Harness decision about cost and accuracy rather than a property of the LLM.

STRIDE \citep{stride2026} connects language reasoning to a separate numerical foundation model. A teacher constructs reference reasoning from training windows and their pseudo-future targets, and a smaller language model learns to reproduce this reasoning. The language model's last hidden states are mean-pooled and projected into a reasoning vector, which is fused with the native time series embeddings of the numerical model by sequence prefixing or initial-state substitution according to the forecaster architecture. A joint cross-entropy and quantile objective updates the numerical model and the projection together with LoRA parameters in the language model. This design lets a language-derived reasoning representation influence continuous forecasts while retaining a dedicated numerical decoder. STRIDE thus sits directly on the boundary between the LLM side and the Harness: its language model is trained on the LLM side to emit a latent reasoning vector, whereas its numerical decoder remains a pretrained time series foundation model inside the Harness.

\subsection{Toward native temporal perception}
\label{sec:llm-native}

Vision followed a comparable path: image understanding first reached LLM Agents through external captioning and recognition tools before becoming a native modality of multimodal LLMs. Time series show the first steps of the same path. ChatTS \citep{chatts2025} inserts patch embeddings among text tokens, as vision encoders do for image patches; TimeMaster \citep{timemaster2025} and Zephyrus-R1 \citep{zephyrusr12026} train vision-language models that read plotted series; and TimeOmni-1 \citep{timeomni2026} trains a single model for temporal reasoning across four tasks. If time series become a native modality of omni-modal LLMs, perception will move from the series views of Section~\ref{sec:temporal-processing} into the model. Operations that must be exact and auditable, including statistics over declared windows, calibrated forecast distributions, and checks of availability time, should remain Harness tools. Which temporal capabilities belong in the model and which in the Harness remains an open problem (Section~\ref{sec:open-problems}).

\section{General Harness}
\label{sec:general-harness}

The General Harness connects the LLM's action choices to executable operations, persistent state, and feedback. We synthesize five recurring mechanism dimensions from Agent architecture and Harness research \citep{sumers2024coala,wang2024agentsurvey,ning2026codeharness,lin2026agenticharness}, as summarized in Table~\ref{tab:term-basis}. A recent survey of Agent engineering likewise treats an Agent as a foundation model coupled with an execution Harness and divides the Harness into six runtime responsibilities: observation, context, control, action, state, and verification \citep{guo2026harnesssurvey}. Our five dimensions cover these responsibilities: observation and action fall under interface and interaction, context and state under memory and context, control under planning and orchestration, and verification under verification tools.

Table~\ref{tab:component-vocab} gives each dimension a design question and divides it into second-level classes by one criterion: the part of the Agent loop an interface defines, the scope and lifetime of stored information, the object executed, the object organized by orchestration, and the reusable asset changed by an update. These classes follow the Harness mechanisms of \citet{ning2026codeharness}, the memory types of \citet{sumers2024coala}, and the editable Harness components of \citet{lin2026agenticharness}. Multiple dimensions often operate together within one analytical episode to retrieve context, plan a sequence, execute tools, and revise an artifact using validation feedback. The subsections below show how existing Time Series Agents implement these five mechanism dimensions and which modules transfer across tasks. Sections~\ref{sec:general-ts-harness} and \ref{sec:task-specific-harness} then show how temporal semantics and task objectives specialize these general runtime mechanisms.

\subsection{Interface and Interaction}
\label{sec:gh-interface}

The interface defines what the Agent observes, which actions it can request, and how a result returns to the next reasoning step. Its three classes correspond to the three parts of the loop in Eq.~\eqref{eq:agent-loop}: the reasoning step, the action, and the environment that returns the observation.

\noindent\textbf{Reasoning loop.} TS-Agent \citep{tsagent2025} uses an iterative thought--action--observation loop: the reasoner chooses its next step after receiving the structured result of the preceding analytical operation. TimeART \citep{timeart2026} applies the same loop to atomic temporal tools. Step-by-step execution supports questions whose required computation becomes clear only during analysis.

\noindent\textbf{Action interface.} An action interface lets the LLM identify an operation, supply its arguments, and interpret success or failure. Open-TI \citep{openti2024} matches a user intent to a tool and retries when the match fails, and NeuroAct \citep{neuroact2026} discloses tools and skills progressively so that the model sees only the operations relevant to the current stage. Section~\ref{sec:general-ts-harness} covers the numerical operations and their temporal arguments; the action interface itself remains unchanged when the analytical objective changes.

\noindent\textbf{Environment and users.} AION \citep{aion2026} starts from an explicit environment: a task file, an accessible workspace, and a validator. The task file declares the required artifact and constraints before work begins. Environment observations need not be text; for example, the plotting tool of Zephyrus-R1 \citep{zephyrusr12026} returns an image of the series. Human users also form part of the environment: exploratory requests rely on successive observations and follow-up questions, whereas automated submissions rely on explicit output specifications and acceptance checks. BatteryLake \citep{batterylake2026} routes low-confidence extractions to a person for verification, and TimeSeriesScientist \citep{timeseriesscientist2025} returns a report of its workflow decisions, metrics, and provenance.

\subsection{Memory and Context}
\label{sec:gh-memory}

Memory determines which earlier observations remain available to later steps. Its classes differ in scope and lifetime: the next step, the current episode, a knowledge store, or several roles \citep{sumers2024coala,ning2026codeharness}.

\noindent\textbf{Working context.} Working context holds what the next step needs. TS-Reasoner \citep{tsreasoner2026} keeps a buffer of explored solution paths so that its planner does not evaluate them again. The design choice is which information remains as raw tool output and which becomes a compact summary.

\noindent\textbf{Episodic records.} Episodic records store intermediate artifacts and state across an analytical run. TS-Agent \citep{tsagent2025} retains an evidence log so that subsequent reasoning can cite completed analytical results. AION \citep{aion2026} records progress, decisions, and structural state around execution and review, and CLIMATEAGENT \citep{climateagent2025} persists plans, scripts, results, and logs with checkpoints. The evidence log supports the final answer, whereas the progress record supports continuation after interruption or handoff.

\noindent\textbf{Knowledge retrieval.} Knowledge retrieval fetches external documents or prior cases from persistent storage. FinAgent \citep{finagent2024} stores summaries with a dedicated query field for vector similarity search, and From Plots to Words \citep{plotstowords2026} retrieves system configuration and operational knowledge for downstream reasoning. When retrieval is keyed by signal structure or bounded by availability time, it belongs to the General Time Series Harness (Section~\ref{sec:temporal-context}).

\noindent\textbf{Shared state.} Shared state passes intermediate results across multiple roles within an Agent. TradingAgents \citep{tradingagents2024} shares state through structured reports: each role extracts the information it needs and returns a completed report with an identifiable author and purpose, making the input to each decision easier to inspect than an unrestricted conversation history. MAS4TS \citep{mas4ts2026} instead exchanges role states through a confidence-gated shared memory.

\subsection{Tools and Execution}
\label{sec:gh-tools}

Execution converts a selected action into a computation and returns its result, artifacts, and status. The classes differ in what is executed: a registered function, code or a computational job, or a check \citep{ning2026codeharness}.

\noindent\textbf{Function calls.} A function call invokes a registered capability. Agentic RAG for TSA \citep{agenticrag2024} routes each request through a coordinator role to specialized sub-agents and their tools, so supporting a new task family requires adding a sub-agent rather than rebuilding the runtime. FinArena \citep{finarena2025} calls external retrieval only when the model judges that its pretrained knowledge cannot answer the query.

\noindent\textbf{Code and job execution.} Generated code and long computations need a controlled environment. SIREN \citep{siren2026} runs Python through a guarded server that validates paths and tools before execution, and TS-Reasoner \citep{tsreasoner2026} lets the LLM write a custom operator when its library lacks one. MetaCaster's Forecaster Trainer \citep{metacaster2026} executes at the level of computational jobs: it assembles jobs indexed by model, hyperparameters, and dataset, queues them, assigns available GPUs, monitors processes, and recovers errors or interrupted runs. The reusable mechanism is scheduling and recovery; the job's forecasting objective is supplied by the task procedure.

\noindent\textbf{Verification tools.} A verification tool returns a deterministic check rather than an analytical result. TimeSage's reference runtime in TimeSage-MT \citep{timesagemt2026} separates the LLM Planner and Reporter from deterministic validation, profiling, execution, and evaluation, and HydroAgent \citep{hydroagent2026} binds each skill to an output contract that a deterministic executor enforces. Separating planning from deterministic verification reveals whether a failure arose in the chosen workflow or in an operator's implementation, distinguishing execution validity from analytical quality.

\subsection{Planning and Orchestration}
\label{sec:gh-planning}

Orchestration organizes four objects: the steps of a plan, the execution of those steps, the checkpoints a result must pass, and the roles that share the work \citep{ning2026codeharness}.

\noindent\textbf{Decomposition and planning.} Planning specifies dependencies among the operations needed to deliver an artifact. TS-Agent \citep{tsagent2025} chooses actions incrementally from accumulated observations. TS-Reasoner \citep{tsreasoner2026} constructs an ordered executable plan and revises it after failure. The first approach makes the next action sensitive to newly observed evidence; the second provides an explicit plan that can be inspected and rerun as a whole. From Plots to Words \citep{plotstowords2026} turns a query into a hierarchical plan whose steps are delegated, and SAGE-Sleep \citep{sagesleep2026} routes each request by intent before planning.

\noindent\textbf{Execution control.} Control determines whether the Agent continues, revises its work, requests review, or terminates. In TS-Reasoner \citep{tsreasoner2026}, the task decomposer produces an ordered operator plan, the executor runs it, and an execution error triggers a new end-to-end plan; the error message is actionable feedback about the current procedure. CLIMATEAGENT \citep{climateagent2025} likewise retries a failed step with its error. The source and timing of feedback determine what can change during an episode. An execution error is immediately available, whereas a resolved forecast error is available only after the target window has been observed. Because FLAIRR-TS \citep{flairr2025} refines forecasting prompts using realized ground truth, we assign that feedback loop to the Task-Specific Harness (Section~\ref{sec:task-specific-harness}). A complete Agent should therefore separate its immediate retry policy for failed operations from the delayed schedule on which ground-truth outcomes arrive.

\noindent\textbf{Checkpoints and governance.} Checkpoints and governance enforce quality gates on candidate outputs and on updates to the Harness itself. AION \citep{aion2026} governs the workflow lifecycle through explicit protocols for execution, review, rechecking, memory updates, and stopping. It combines artifact-level validity checks with a separate scored evaluation so a candidate can be returned for revision before acceptance. This design separates exploratory choices from required checkpoints: an Agent may explore several analytical routes, but every route must pass an artifact validator. Steward \citep{steward2026} applies the same principle to Harness edits, admitting a prompt change only after schema, allow-list, append-only, and similarity checks while keeping a one-commit revert.

\noindent\textbf{Multi-agent coordination.} Coordination defines responsibilities and the information passed between roles. TradingAgents \citep{tradingagents2024} uses structured reports so that each role consumes a defined state and returns a completed result, and AION \citep{aion2026} makes work, review, rechecking, and stop signals explicit in the communication protocol. AgentFM \citep{agentfm2025} reassigns system roles at runtime, while TS-Debate \citep{multimodaldebate2026} assigns each modality to a dedicated role that exchanges reports over debate rounds. While the communication protocol belongs to the General Harness, the task fixes the content of each handoff: FinCon \citep{fincon2024} passes financial evidence from analysts to a portfolio manager, whereas TimeSeriesScientist \citep{timeseriesscientist2025} passes curation and planning outputs to forecasting and reporting roles (Section~\ref{sec:task-specific-harness}). Defining each role by its required artifact and acceptance check keeps handoffs inspectable and recoverable across tasks.

\subsection{Optimization and Evolution}
\label{sec:gh-optimization}

A Harness improves by changing one of its reusable assets across episodes: the prompt, the stored experience, the tools and skills, or the Harness configuration \citep{lin2026agenticharness}. Each update needs a record of the source episodes and the criterion used to accept it. Writing $A^{(m)}$ for the assets after $m$ updates, $\zeta_j$ for a recorded trajectory of Eq.~\eqref{eq:agent-loop}, and $q_j$ for its score under the acceptance criterion, every class below is an instance of
\begin{equation}
  A^{(m+1)}=\mathcal{U}\!\left(A^{(m)},\{(\zeta_j,q_j)\}_{j=1}^{J}\right),
  \label{eq:asset-update}
\end{equation}
with the LLM parameters $\theta$ fixed, as in Harness optimization that edits prompts, memory, tools, and configuration rather than model weights \citep{lin2026agenticharness}. The classes differ in which asset is updated and how $\mathcal{U}$ makes the update; an evaluation compares $A^{(m)}$ and $A^{(m+1)}$ on held-out episodes.

\noindent\textbf{Prompt optimization.} Prompt optimization updates static instructions across episodes using validation feedback. ATLAS \citep{atlas2025} edits only the static instruction block of a trading prompt, keeps its runtime placeholders fixed, and scores each candidate with the task metric. TradeGrad \citep{textualgradienttrading2026} samples scored programs and asks an LLM to estimate a textual gradient from them. Both take the task metric only as an input, so they are General Harness mechanisms.

\noindent\textbf{Experience distillation.} Experience distillation extracts reusable guidance from completed trajectories to inform future runs. NeuroAct \citep{neuroact2026} stores conditional recommendations keyed by task and model evidence and revises later decisions from their outcomes. TimeClaw with Exploratory Execution Learning (TimeClaw-EEL) \citep{timeclaw2026explore} formalizes this process as an Explore--Compare--Distill--Reinject loop around a fixed LLM: the Harness executes alternative tool trajectories, scores them, and distills the winning trace into external experience available to later episodes. For each task instance, the Harness scores executed candidate trajectories by their negative task loss against the exploration target, retains the best valid one, and summarizes, cleans, and distills the comparison into a task-scoped experience state, an instance of Eq.~\eqref{eq:asset-update}. The scoring happens only during experience construction; at inference time the Harness supplies only the retained experience.
Because this experience-update mechanism accepts arbitrary task losses, it belongs to the General Harness, whereas the individual analytical operations keep their temporal or task-specific scopes. An evaluator can measure the value of distilled experience by comparing the initial and updated experience states on held-out episodes while holding the backbone and tool access fixed. Separate transfer tests across unseen instances, domains, and task cells then establish the scope over which the distilled experience remains useful.

\noindent\textbf{Tool and skill evolution.} Tool and skill evolution turns recurring code patterns from past trajectories into registered operations. In TimeClaw-Harness \citep{timeclaw2026harness}, a separate TimeClaw runtime for contextualized time series, a code-specialized LLM inspects clustered trajectories and task descriptions, extracts a shared procedure, and writes executable code with an invocation description. The Harness tests the candidate tool on held-out similar tasks and registers it once it meets a success threshold. TimeSage-EV \citep{timesageev2026} similarly induces reusable Python skills and admits them after a quality check.

\noindent\textbf{Harness configuration search.} Harness configuration search mutates and selects entire workflows or Harness settings using validation scores. EvoTS-Agent \citep{evotsagent2026} evolves whole trajectories through revision, alternative strategies, and recombination, and accepts a new incumbent only when an external validation score improves. AQuA \citep{aqua2026} compiles and scores one configuration change at a time. Meta-Harness \citep{lee2026metaharness} optimizes a Harness end to end outside time series.

These four classes update different assets: prompt optimization and experience distillation change the guidance given to the LLM, whereas tool evolution and configuration search change the operations and workflow executed by the Harness. In every case, the analytical content of the updated asset determines its temporal or task scope, but the mechanism that extracts, tests, and installs the update belongs to the General Harness.

\section{General Time Series Harness}
\label{sec:general-ts-harness}

The General Time Series Harness specializes the General Harness mechanism dimensions through temporal order, index, frequency, window, horizon, regime, availability time, and leakage control. These temporal mechanisms build on the interaction, execution, and feedback interfaces of the General Harness.

The contract boundary makes the distinction explicit. The General Harness specifies how an Agent stores state, invokes tools, coordinates roles, and verifies outputs. The General Time Series Harness specifies what makes a temporal state or operation valid: which observations were available, how a window is aligned, which frequency and units apply, and how a revision is represented. TimeClaw-Harness \citep{timeclaw2026harness} and AION \citep{aion2026} make this boundary explicit through native time series execution, temporal grounding, and reliability mechanisms.

At the current time, denoted by $\tau$, when the Agent issues its output, the admissible information is
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{I}_{\tau} = \{(x_i,t_i,v_i): v_i \leq \tau\},
  \label{eq:temporal-availability}
\end{equation}
where $t_i$ is the event time and $v_i$ is the time at which the value became available. In a backtest, the current time is the simulated origin rather than the wall-clock time at which the experiment runs. The availability record keeps the two times that temporal databases distinguish: the valid time at which a fact holds and the transaction time at which it is recorded \citep{snodgrass1985taxonomy,jensen1999temporal}. Real-time macroeconomic data sets keep every released vintage of a series for the same reason \citep{croushore2001realtime}, and LLM forecasts that use information published after the current time show look-ahead bias \citep{glasserman2023lookahead,gao2025lookahead}. The information boundary admits any record published by the current time, including known future calendar events, whereas the task specifies which observation and target windows may be used.

Read the upper timeline first: availability time determines whether a source can be used at the current time. The lower paths connect the LLM to requests and returned observations.

The four temporal functions follow from the availability record in Eq.~\eqref{eq:temporal-availability}. A shared module can store and version the records $(x_i,t_i,v_i)$ (data management), operate on the values $x_i$ over windows of $t_i$ (processing), admit information other than the series into the availability record $\mathcal{I}_\tau$ (context and memory), or check that inputs and outputs respect $v_i\leq\tau$, windows, and units (validation). Together with the scope of what the record admits, these functions match the components of a data model in \citet{codd1980datamodels}: data structures, operators, and integrity rules. The same division appears in the basic steps of a forecasting task, which gather data and accumulated expertise, analyze and model them, and evaluate the result \citep{hyndman2021fpp}.

Each function applies the General Harness mechanism dimensions of Table~\ref{tab:component-vocab} to temporal objects: data management draws on interfaces and state; processing on tools and workflow; context on memory and retrieval; and validation on control and feedback. A module can serve several functions, but none of the four can be removed: all 97 General Time Series Harness modules carry at least one function, and each function contains modules that carry no other (26 for processing, 8 for validation, 8 for context and memory, and 5 for data management). Coordination, scheduling, and optimization act on any function and belong to the General Harness, whereas the output a module produces belongs to the Task-Specific Harness. Section~\ref{sec:task-specific-harness} examines how target outputs specialize these shared capabilities.

Table~\ref{tab:gts-vocab} divides each temporal function using the same availability record. Data management and validation split by the component they handle: the value $x_i$ with its measurement, the event time $t_i$, and the availability time $v_i$. Data management adds the workspace that holds the records, and validation adds the claims made from them. Processing splits by what an operation returns: a transformed series, statistics about the series, a view of the series for the LLM, or a reusable library of operations. Context and memory splits by what enters the availability record $\mathcal{I}_\tau$: external events and text, historical analogues of the current series, and procedural experience from earlier analyses \citep{chen2026tsagentsurvey}. External events and procedural experience correspond to semantic and procedural memory in \citet{sumers2024coala}. Historical analogues instead follow weather prediction \citep{lorenz1969analogues}, retrieving past states of the observed system that resemble the present state rather than the Agent's own episodes. The availability and past-only checks of validation guard against the leakage defined by \citet{kaufman2012leakage}, and chronological evaluation follows the rolling-origin design of \citet{tashman2000outofsample}. \subsection{Temporal Data Management}
\label{sec:temporal-data}

Before an Agent can compare signals or call a model, its inputs must identify what was measured, when it was measured, when it became available, and how the stored values should be interpreted. The input schema therefore includes timestamps, sampling intervals, units, missingness, and revision information. These properties form the interface between the raw source and every later operation. AION \citep{aion2026} (Section~3.3.1) places this interface before model selection: the task must resolve the observation unit, visible history, cutoff, requested horizon, granularity, and output frequency. The resulting requirements determine which data are admissible and which operations can satisfy the request.

\noindent\textbf{Measurement schema.} BatteryLake \citep{batterylake2026} makes measurement semantics explicit through a canonical sample schema. Time, voltage, current, temperature, capacity, and step type have fixed units and domains, and a sign convention on current distinguishes charging from discharging. Fixing these conventions ensures consistent interpretation of heterogeneous battery files before downstream health or lifetime analysis. SIREN \citep{siren2026} normalizes timestamps, units, and spatial matches across its weather sources, and World2TS \citep{world2ts2026} consolidates reports into timestamped event records with a declared scope so that events are stored with the same care as numerical values.

\noindent\textbf{Time index and alignment.} LEMAD \citep{ji2025lemad} addresses a related ingestion problem for operational services: its preprocessing module aligns timestamps, normalizes formats, and handles missing values across heterogeneous streams. AgriWorld \citep{agriworld2026} resamples agricultural data to a canonical frequency. Streaming inputs add an ordering requirement. CALM \citep{calm2025} buffers incoming observations per key before its stateful sliding-window function emits the ordered window; without this step, out-of-order arrival changes which observations enter a computation. The schema defines the meaning of each sample, while the index and the buffer preserve its position and order as samples arrive.

\noindent\textbf{Point-in-time access.} A record can be used only from its availability time $v_i$. SwingGuard \citep{swingguard2026} aligns time-indexed evidence under an availability cutoff before its trading procedure runs, and Quant-Arena \citep{quantarena2026} serves market observations through an interface scoped by the decision date. Storing $v_i$ with the data lets the availability check of Section~\ref{sec:temporal-validation} be enforced at access rather than reconstructed afterwards.

\noindent\textbf{Temporal workspace.} Stored data also need a representation that preserves numerical precision. TimeClaw-Harness \citep{timeclaw2026harness} (Section~3.2) keeps the full time series object in a task-local workspace; tools run on that object and return compact structured observations to the LLM. SAGE-AD \citep{sagead2026} (Section~3.2) likewise separates the original series used by numerical tools from a compressed summary used in prompting, and SAGE-Sleep \citep{sagesleep2026} exposes sensor data through a queryable store with normalized timestamps. Both designs keep the language context compact while preserving full numerical precision for tool execution. Section~\ref{sec:task-anomaly} examines how SAGE-AD uses this evidence for anomaly detection.

For a new application, a developer selects and combines these ingestion classes to match the ambiguities of the raw data sources. Once the ingestion pipeline is assembled, testing should vary arrival order, units, missing entries, and timestamp formats, and inspect the numerical object delivered to the next operation.

\subsection{Temporal Processing}
\label{sec:temporal-processing}

Temporal processing converts a stored series into the evidence needed for analysis. Inputs and outputs must preserve their relation to the source sequence because the same numerical array may describe levels, differences, normalized values, or an aggregate over a longer interval. A tool interface must therefore expose both the operation and the temporal meaning of its result.

\noindent\textbf{Preprocessing and transformation.} In TimeSeriesScientist \citep{timeseriesscientist2025} (Section~3.2), the Curator module inspects the input series before selecting a transformation. It produces data statistics, missing-value information, outlier findings, and a treatment strategy, and applies that strategy to the series.
The processed series then supports visualization, decomposition, and autocorrelation analysis. These diagnostics precede the task-specific model selection for forecasting in Section~\ref{sec:task-forecasting}. For example, identifying missing intervals changes the preprocessing plan, whereas ranking candidate forecasters requires a target horizon and validation loss. Separating the two decisions makes the preparation stage reusable while keeping model selection tied to its output objective. GenAutoML \citep{genautoml2026} wraps a model with reversible instance normalization and resamples a series causally before imputing missing values; recording the transformation allows the Harness to map results back to the original scale.

\noindent\textbf{Statistical and spectral analysis.} TS-Agent \citep{tsagent2025} supplies analytical APIs that operate directly on native numerical series and return structured findings. TESSA \citep{tessa2024} computes decomposition, lag, rolling-window, and correlation features, CastFlow \citep{castflow2026} profiles distribution statistics and spectral entropy of the observed series, and ElliottAgents \citep{elliottagents2025} enumerates candidate wave structures on historical charts. The output is evidence about the series rather than a new series.

\noindent\textbf{Series views for the LLM.} Processing can also change how a series is shown to the LLM. MAS4TS \citep{mas4ts2026} represents a series through plot anchors and a latent trajectory, TS-Debate \citep{multimodaldebate2026} renders time and frequency charts with numerical lookup, and the Metric Agent of AgentFM \citep{agentfm2025} turns multivariate measurements into intervals, trends, and anomaly descriptions. Every view must retain a mapping to the underlying values so that claims derived from the view can be checked against the series.

\noindent\textbf{Operator and skill libraries.} TS-Reasoner \citep{tsreasoner2026} wraps statistical tools, learned models, and numerical utilities in a consistent calling structure, and TimeART \citep{timeart2026} uses atomic tools for intensive temporal computations. TimeSage-MT \citep{timesagemt2026} provides a skill library that a planner uses across analytical turns. MAS4TS combines decomposition, smoothing, statistical prediction, constraint projection, and residual correction into chains. Because its router selects from a task-indexed chain registry using a downstream objective, we assign the routed chain to the Task-Specific Harness and the plot-anchor representation to the General Time Series Harness. Atomic operators expose intermediate findings for inspection and revision, whereas composed chains capture recurring sequences of analysis. The right granularity depends on where new observations can change the plan. In either case, the next tool needs the processed window, applied transformation, and output scale, making explicit intermediate metadata a prerequisite for reuse. Evaluation should verify both individual numerical results and the composed sequence, as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}.

\subsection{Temporal Context and Memory}
\label{sec:temporal-context}

Temporal analysis often requires information beyond the numerical sequence: documents describe external events, earlier windows provide historical analogues, and earlier analyses supply reusable procedures. In each class, retrieval must solve two problems: measuring relevance to the current signal and verifying that the evidence is available at the current time. An Agent needs both checks to use retrieved material without leakage.

\noindent\textbf{External events and text.} NEXUS \citep{nexus2026} (Section~3.1) aligns historical values and contemporaneous text into a chronological context timeline, linking each numerical change to the period in which its textual explanation applies. TFRBench \citep{tfrbench2026} retrieves external events only from a bounded window and returns them with their dates. \citet{williams2025cik} distinguish five kinds of such contextual information for forecasting: intemporal, historical, covariate, future, and causal.

\noindent\textbf{Historical analogues.} Similar past windows or cases can inform the current analysis. FLAIRR-TS \citep{flairr2025} ranks past windows by their similarity to the current one, SIREN \citep{siren2026} keeps a database of earlier operational cases with their event conditions, and MOSAIC \citep{mosaic2026} retrieves prior cases conditioned on a profile of the current data. Relevance is defined by the signal, so retrieval must compare series structure as well as text.

\noindent\textbf{Procedural experience.} TimeClaw-Harness \citep{timeclaw2026harness} (Section~3.4) retrieves prior analytical episodes through separate signal and text keys. Each memory record stores the task, a signal key, a text key, the successful trajectory, and its transferable rationale. Similar wording may describe different signal structures, while similar shapes may occur under different contextual conditions. Separate keys preserve these two sources of relevance, and the retained trajectory explains how a preceding episode was solved. TSHarness \citep{tsharness2026} stores analytical procedures with their applicability conditions, recommended tools, and evidence requirements, and FinCon \citep{fincon2024} decays procedural and episodic memories according to the timeliness of the underlying information.

Memory crosses into the Task-Specific Harness when its retrieval or retention policy depends on the target outcome. FinMem \citep{finmem2023} ranks and promotes financial memories for trading, while FLAIRR-TS \citep{flairr2025} combines analogue retrieval with observed forecasting errors; Section~\ref{sec:task-specific-harness} analyzes both policies under decision making and forecasting. Whereas the shared memory structure depends on whether the Agent stores dated events, signal analogues, or trajectories, task feedback determines how those records are ranked and retained. To test whether memory contributes valid evidence to a decision, an evaluator should compare the retrieved content, its availability time, and its downstream effect.

\subsection{Temporal Validation}
\label{sec:temporal-validation}

An analysis can execute successfully while using information from the wrong period, computing with future values, reading malformed data, or making a claim unsupported by its observations. Temporal validation checks these four relationships before the task score is interpreted. AION \citep{aion2026} separates validity findings, including timestamp alignment and leakage boundaries, from scored outcomes such as forecasting error or report quality. The distinction allows the same temporal checks to precede several different output metrics.

\noindent\textbf{Availability checks.} Several systems enforce availability at retrieval time. WorldReasoner \citep{worldreasoner2026} filters article and event records against the simulated forecast date. TIEM \citep{tiem2026} also checks the provenance of retained skills so that every ancestor resolved before the current cutoff. TimeSage-EV \citep{timesageev2026} (Appendix~A.6) imposes a related capability boundary by activating a newly accepted skill only in the next evaluation period. These mechanisms guard three paths through which future information can leak into an analysis: retrieved records, inherited experience, and newly created tools. Equation~\eqref{eq:temporal-availability} defines the common information boundary, while each module checks a specific access path. TraceMAS \citep{tracemas2026} likewise reviews retrieved evidence for both availability and cross-source consistency.

\noindent\textbf{Past-only computation.} A computation can use the future even when every input was available at the current time. FactorMAD \citep{duan2025factormad} restricts factor construction to trailing rolling statistics and excludes future timestamps, separating temporal validity from the financial value of the factor. AQuA \citep{aqua2026} builds the same constraint into its operator grammar, which admits only trailing-window and current-timestamp operators.

\noindent\textbf{Data and schema checks.} Validation also checks that values mean what the schema says. BatteryLake \citep{batterylake2026} admits converted files only through a schema validation gate, and NeuroAct \citep{neuroact2026} checks input shapes, channels, and units before windowed training runs. TSQAgent \citep{tsqbench2026} judges data quality with a shared library of temporal measurements; its selection and adjudication procedure is task-specific, and its judgment can guide data selection before forecasting or classification.

\noindent\textbf{Temporal claim verification.} The last check concerns what is claimed from the data. TS-Debate \citep{multimodaldebate2026} verifies each claim against the temporal scope it refers to, and EpiCoach \citep{epicoach2026} records hypotheses and predictions together with the data cutoff at which they were made. TS-Agent \citep{tsagent2025} uses temporal question intents to declare required evidence predicates: an anomaly location answer requires evidence of an anomaly and its supported segment, whereas other intents require trend, seasonality, or lag findings. Because the temporal question defines both the required predicates and the answer schema, TS-Agent's intent-specific gate belongs to the Task-Specific Harness, while shared numerical tools supply the findings it checks.

Across these examples, a validation interface should return the relevant finding and its supporting data, so that a developer can distinguish an invalid information boundary, a computation that uses the future, a deficient input series, and a poorly supported answer. These checks provide distinct feedback for revision. Task-specific scores then measure the usefulness of the accepted result. Together, the four temporal functions establish the data and evidence conditions under which the task procedures in the next section operate.

\section{Task-Specific Harness}
\label{sec:task-specific-harness}

Task-Specific Harness modules organize shared capabilities around a required output and objective. We classify tasks along two axes that need no new concepts. The first is the output mode, given by how the output is checked. Regression produces numerical values that are compared with observed values, and classification produces classes or class probabilities that are compared with observed classes \citep{hastie2009esl,bishop2006prml}. Generation produces new content, text or series, that has no single reference and is judged by its quality, fidelity, or use; decision making produces an action that is judged by its consequences. A probability does not change the mode: a predictive distribution of a continuous quantity is a probabilistic regression output, the probability of a discrete event is a classification output, and each is scored by its own proper scoring rules \citep{gneiting2007scoring,gneiting2011point}. The second axis is time. The output refers either to the record observed by the current time, $\mathcal{I}_\tau$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:temporal-availability}, or to quantities that become observable only after the current time. This is the classical division between interpolation or smoothing and extrapolation or prediction \citep{wiener1949extrapolation,kalman1960filtering}. Formally, a task asks for an output about a set of target times $\mathcal{T}$ from the admissible information,
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{y}=f\!\left(\mathcal{I}_{\tau};\mathcal{T}\right),\qquad
  \mathcal{T}\subseteq(-\infty,\tau]\ \text{(observed record)}\quad\text{or}\quad
  \mathcal{T}\subseteq(\tau,\tau+H]\ \text{(after the current time)},
  \label{eq:task-contract}
\end{equation}
where $H$ is the horizon, measured from the forecast origin \citep{hyndman2021fpp}. The output mode fixes what $\widehat{y}$ is and how it is scored: values, classes, new content, or an action.

The two axes define the eight cells of the task grid (Table~\ref{tab:tsk-vocab}), and each cell contains the standard task names of time series analysis \citep{wu2023timesnet,wang2024tslib,kong2025timemqa}. Three rules place a module into the task grid. First, a module enters every cell that its outputs occupy. Second, question answering is a format rather than a task: a question about the observed series with a single correct answer is classification, a free-text answer or explanation is generation, and a question about future values or events is forecasting. Third, a method without an output of its own, such as representation or transfer learning, enters the cell of the downstream task on which it is evaluated.

Among the 162 works in scope, regression appears in nearly half of the works after the current time (46\%) and in 9\% on the observed record; classification reverses that balance (40\% on the observed record and 9\% after the current time); generation appears only on the observed record (25\%), and decision making only after the current time (28\%). Classification is thus the second most common output mode, and most of it concerns the observed record: anomaly detection, classification of series, root cause identification, and closed questions. Among the 84 works that forecast a value or an event, 90\% report point forecasts and about one in five (21\%) report probabilistic forecasts; some report both. Two cells, projection (generating futures under assumed conditions) and hindsight decision (choosing after the fact the action that should have been taken at an earlier time), are well defined but have no evaluated task output. Both appear in the reviewed systems only as internal mechanisms (Sections~\ref{sec:task-generation} and~\ref{sec:task-decision}). The temporal data, processing, context, and validation functions of Section~\ref{sec:general-ts-harness} supply reusable building blocks for every cell (Figure~\ref{fig:task-specific-harness}).

\subsection{Regression}
\label{sec:task-regression}
\label{sec:task-forecasting}

A regression output is a number, or a distribution over numbers, that is compared with values either in the record observed by the current time or after the current time.

\noindent\textbf{Imputation and estimation.} Imputation appears mostly as one output of multi-task workflows: MAS4TS \citep{mas4ts2026} routes forecasting, classification, imputation, and anomaly detection requests to tool chains over a shared memory, and TESSA \citep{tessa2024} evaluates its series annotations through downstream forecasting and imputation. Estimation recovers a quantity that is not measured directly: the physiological Agent of \citet{physiologicalagent2025} orchestrates the retrieval of wearable PPG signals and a heart-rate estimation pipeline, and HydroAgent \citep{hydroagentcalibration2026} calibrates hydrologic model parameters through tool-mediated simulator episodes.

\noindent\textbf{Value forecasting.} A value forecast describes numerical values after the current time. Forecasting fixes an origin, target, horizon, and evaluation rule, and the Agent must choose how to build a forecast, which contextual evidence to use, and when to revise it. A point forecast reports one value per target time; a probabilistic forecast reports an interval, quantiles, a predictive distribution, or sample paths. The task contract determines the final object being optimized. A value forecast instantiates Eq.~\eqref{eq:task-contract} with numerical outputs at the $H$ target times after the current time; $\mathcal{I}_{\tau}$ contains the observed target history and the covariates known at the origin, including future calendar information available under Eq.~\eqref{eq:temporal-availability}.

The reviewed procedures for value forecasting either select and combine numerical models or revise a forecast with context. In TimeSeriesScientist \citep{timeseriesscientist2025}, the Curator's temporal diagnostics guide the Planner's search over numerical forecasters. For each candidate forecaster, the Planner tunes its configuration to minimize validation MAPE.
The Planner ranks the selected configurations, and the Forecaster uses their validation results to choose a single model or an ensemble, fixing its strategy and weights before evaluating the test set. This selection rule links the temporal diagnostics in Section~\ref{sec:general-ts-harness} to a task-specific objective. TimeCopilot \citep{timecopilot2025} likewise makes forecasting models callable components: the model library supplies numerical capabilities while the Agent organizes their use.

The second procedure revises a value forecast with context. FLAIRR-TS \citep{flairr2025} (Algorithm~1) refines the forecasting prompt through retrieved historical segments and observed forecast errors. In each iteration it retrieves segments similar to the current context, forecasts an $H$-step refinement window with the augmented prompt, and measures MAE against the realized values; a refiner then proposes the next prompt from earlier prompts and errors; early stopping returns the current prompt, while reaching the iteration limit returns the lowest-MAE prompt. In deployment, this feedback becomes available when the refinement window has resolved.

CastFSR \citep{castfsr2026} separates fast numerical forecasting, slower contextual deliberation, and reflective evaluation. The initial prediction supplies a numerical prior; retrieved context then supports a candidate revision. At each step the policy chooses among fast, slow, reflective, and final actions on a state that holds the task inputs, the interaction history, the current numerical prior, and the retrieved context. A slow step builds context and proposes a candidate revision, a reflective step assesses it, and the final step returns the forecast. Reflection checks the candidate against temporal alignment, contextual support, and domain constraints such as units and capacity. Weak or redundant context can leave the numerical prior unchanged, whereas relevant evidence supports a targeted revision. Last-Mile Forecasting \citep{lastmile2026} and CastFlow \citep{castflow2026} provide related settings for contextual revision, while HydroAgent \citep{hydroagent2026} connects predictions to hydrological simulation and warnings.

SCENARIODIFF \citep{scenariodiff2026} uses a Historical Context Agent, a Scenario Agent, and an Anchor Guidance Agent to translate documents into historical summaries, a future scenario, and sparse anchor constraints. A diffusion forecaster, trained with the standard denoising objective, generates the future trajectory conditioned on the numerical lookback, a summary of historical text, and the inferred scenario. Anchor Blended Sampling edits anchor-relevant regions of the generated trajectory while retaining the remaining temporal structure. Because its sampled trajectories are scored against the realized future rather than evaluated without a reference, SCENARIODIFF is a probabilistic forecaster rather than a generation procedure.

\subsection{Classification}
\label{sec:task-classification}
\label{sec:task-analysis}
\label{sec:task-anomaly}

A classification output is a class or a class probability. On the observed record the reference lies in the data and its labels, so feedback can be computed during the analysis; for a future event it is available only after the outcome resolves.

\noindent\textbf{Classification and detection.} Classification Agents attach a label to a series or a window. FETA \citep{feta2025} decomposes a multivariate series by channel, retrieves exemplars by dynamic time warping, reasons over each channel, and aggregates the channel decisions by confidence. MarsTSC \citep{marstsc2026} instead builds a class knowledge bank from a few labeled examples and revises it through self-checks at test time.

\noindent\textbf{Anomaly detection and root cause identification.} Anomaly detection asks where a series departs from expected behavior; diagnosis explains that departure and may guide a response. These outputs require different evidence. An interval detector needs numerical and contextual support for the flagged region, whereas a workflow for diagnosing service incidents also needs logs, incident context, and a mapping from symptoms to possible causes.

AnomaMind \citep{anomamind2026} structures interval detection into coarse evidence acquisition, adaptive evidence construction, anomaly detection, and iterative refinement. Each step adds numerical diagnostics or knowledge evidence to the state, with knowledge memory kept separate from numerical diagnostic tools, and a learned detector labels each candidate interval from the accumulated evidence. During detector training, rule-based feedback combines parse validity, agreement with labeled anomalous intervals, and a false positive penalty.
The reward supplies learning signals for a parseable and accurate interval decision. The trained detector belongs to the LLM side, while the surrounding evidence acquisition and refinement procedure forms the Task-Specific Harness.

SAGE-AD \citep{sagead2026} uses a complementary form of evidence. It preserves the original series for precise tools and passes a compressed representation to the LLM. Family-specific analyzers and diagnostic visualizations feed a detector that returns anomaly intervals, confidence, and anomaly types. To build its in-context reference database from normal training segments, the system clusters representative subsequences and applies rule-based perturbations to create normal and anomalous examples for nine anomaly types. Example retrieval thus becomes part of the detection procedure, with evidence specific to the anomaly family guiding the interpretation of a suspicious interval.

\textit{Detection rules and executable pipelines.} ARGOS \citep{argos2025} (Sections~3.1--3.3) turns labeled temporal chunks into executable detection rules. A Detection Agent proposes rules, a Repair Agent executes them on dummy inputs to fix implementation errors, and a Review Agent scores their accuracy on validation data. During deployment, the Harness runs the accepted rules alongside an existing base detector. The learned artifact is a reusable detector program, whereas AnomaMind selects evidence and refines an interval decision within a single analysis. Evaluating rule generation must therefore account for when rule construction occurs and which labels are available during that phase.

AD-AGENT \citep{adagent2025} constructs a pipeline from a natural-language request and a dataset. Its Processor extracts modality and supervision requirements, the Selector chooses a supported library and model, and subsequent roles retrieve implementation details, generate code, and review execution, with TSLib supplying the time series branch. This pipeline-synthesis approach suits requests that assemble an executable detector from existing libraries. Its Reviewer tests execution on synthetic samples, while optional Evaluator and Optimizer roles assess labeled data and tune hyperparameters. For a deployed time series pipeline, code execution success and detection accuracy on held-out series provide complementary checks.

\textit{From detection to diagnosis.} LEMAD \citep{ji2025lemad} extends the output to service diagnosis in power-grid operations. Metric and log collectors send aligned streams through Kafka to a real-time analysis role, which correlates the signals and produces alert summaries and preliminary hypotheses. An anomaly detection role then evaluates suspicious events using hybrid models and contextual reasoning, returning confidence, root cause localization, and recommendations. Its reported evaluation includes precision, recall, F1, and latency on service data. For system design, adding diagnosis changes both the required evidence and the final artifact: an interval label becomes one input to a broader workflow for incident analysis. Root cause identification selects the responsible component or fault type from candidates; TraceBench \citep{tracebench2026} evaluates such attribution in controlled simulated tasks. Across these anomaly detection and diagnosis methods, the designer must choose whether to learn evidence acquisition, retrieve diagnostic examples, construct a detection pipeline, or correlate service streams for root cause attribution according to the available supervision, evidence sources, and required output.

\noindent\textbf{Closed question answering.} A question about the observed series with a single correct answer is a classification output. TS-Reasoner \citep{tsreasoner2026} decomposes a query into a workflow of temporal operators, executes it, and revises the plan from execution feedback, while TimeART \citep{timeart2026} trains a tool-using reasoner over atomic temporal tools. The intent-specific evidence gate of TS-Agent \citep{tsagent2025} (Section~\ref{sec:temporal-validation}) is the corresponding Task-Specific Harness check: the question type fixes which findings an answer must cite. Section~\ref{sec:temporal-qa} discusses how question answering is evaluated.

\noindent\textbf{Event forecasting.} Event forecasting specifies a future event label or probability as its output. TimeCAP \citep{timecap2025} (Section~3.3) first asks a frozen contextualizer to describe the input series. A trained multimodal encoder combines the series and its description to predict the event and to embed the case for retrieval. The Harness retrieves the nearest training cases, whose descriptions and labels serve as in-context examples for the predictor LLM, and the system mixes the class predicted by the LLM with the encoder's prediction through a fixed weight.
The distinctive mechanism is the learned retrieval of relevant event examples and their use in complementary predictors. Here augmentation enriches the prediction context, while the output contract remains event prediction. AIA Forecaster \citep{aiaforecaster2025} combines search, supervisor aggregation, and calibration for judgmental event forecasts; AUDITFORECAST \citep{auditforecast2026} anchors predictions in a quantitative baseline and updates them in odds space. To compare event forecasters fairly, an evaluation must score probabilities and outcomes alongside evidence access and reasoning traces while holding the target event, resolution time, and available evidence fixed. Across forecasting workflows, a numerical trajectory requires validation over a declared horizon, whereas an event probability requires an explicit resolution rule and calibration check. Retrieval, model selection, and forecast revision must therefore rely only on evidence and feedback that resolve before the target horizon.

\subsection{Generation}
\label{sec:task-generation}

A generated output has no single reference: a description, an explanation, or a new series is judged by its quality, its fidelity, or its use.

\noindent\textbf{Description and explanation.} Open questions, reports, and diagnoses produce text about the observed series. Time-RA \citep{timera2025} defines a structured task for anomaly diagnosis and refines its reasoning data with feedback; LineupRL \citep{lineuprl2026} rewards a captioner when a frozen verifier identifies the described series among distractors with matched statistics; and Zephyrus-R1 \citep{zephyrusr12026} scores forecast-discussion reports with a rubric gated by numerical grounding and claim entailment. Because no single reference exists, these procedures replace reference matching with a verifiable proxy: series identification, claim entailment, or a grounded rubric.

\noindent\textbf{Synthesis and augmentation.}\label{sec:task-synthesis} Synthesis and augmentation create new series for learning or analysis. Augmentation derives new series from existing ones, such as a transformed positive view; conditioned synthesis produces series from text, domain context, or a small support set, such as additional training series. By contrast, temporal instruction dialogues generated to train a language model, as in ChatAD \citep{chatad2026} through TSEvol, are LLM-side training data (Section~\ref{sec:llm-side}). Comparing synthesis methods therefore requires identifying both the generated object and the learner or analysis it supports, because preserving a useful representation and training an accurate downstream model require different checks.

MERIT \citep{merit2025} targets unsupervised time series representation learning. A retrieval role selects relevant series from a candidate set, an augmentation role uses those examples to choose transformations, and a review role assesses the resulting positive views. Accepted samples enter a memory bank, while rejection returns the workflow to retrieval for refinement. The retrieved neighbors and augmented views then supply positive pairs for contrastive learning. The review role filters generated examples during construction, while downstream experiments evaluate the learned representation.

BRIDGE \citep{bridge2025} (Section~3) generates a series from sampled noise, conditioned on a domain and on a text description of desired temporal properties such as trend and periodicity. Its preparation stage collects textual templates across multiple roles, evaluates the resulting descriptions through a text-conditioned forecasting task, and refines the templates using feedback. The generation stage combines the refined description with domain prototypes to condition a diffusion model. This two-stage design separates controlling the generated series from constructing the text prompt. The forecasting evaluator supplies feedback for template refinement, while final generation experiments evaluate the synthetic series.

MetaCaster \citep{metacaster2026} generates training series from a small support set and domain context, then trains lightweight forecasters on the generated data. Its Harness Proposer changes the generator's prompts and skills to improve the downstream forecasting result. This is an instance of Eq.~\eqref{eq:asset-update} in which the assets are the generator's prompts and skills. Its score is a hinge discrepancy between forecasters trained on authentic and generated data, computed from forecasting error on held-out evaluation splits, which penalizes a forecaster trained on generated data when it performs worse than its counterpart trained on authentic data. This error gap updates the generator, while final generalization is assessed on separate in-domain and out-of-domain datasets. After optimization, the system removes the Harness Proposer and uses the learned prompts and skills to guide generation for a new support set. Generation and Harness optimization are coupled to the forecasting objective, whereas the job scheduler and recovery mechanism remain separately reusable General Harness modules. MetaCaster thus illustrates how one paper contributes distinct modules to different layers.

Across series synthesis workflows, the target learner determines the review criterion: MERIT filters augmented views to preserve signal structure for contrastive representation learning, whereas MetaCaster couples generation to downstream forecast error so that forecaster accuracy drives generator revision. Selecting a synthesis workflow therefore starts with the intended learner and the feedback available to improve its training data.

\noindent\textbf{Projection.} The cell after the current time is empty as a task output. A projection generates the future under an assumed condition, such as an intervention or a stress scenario. Climate science distinguishes such scenario-dependent projections from predictions, because the assumed condition may never occur \citep{ipcc2021glossary}; a projection therefore cannot be scored against the realized future, and generated trajectories that are scored in that way, as in SCENARIODIFF, are probabilistic forecasts. In the reviewed systems, projections appear only inside decision procedures: EpiWorld \citep{epiworld2026} rolls out a policy-conditioned epidemic world model, AgriWorld \citep{agriworld2026} checks the causal direction of what-if interventions, and NeuroAgentic \citep{neuroagentic2026} perturbs a TimesFM forecast according to each proposed control command. These rollouts select an action, and only the resulting action is evaluated; no reviewed work evaluates the projection itself.

\subsection{Decision Making}
\label{sec:task-decision}

Decision making Agents select an action using predictive or diagnostic evidence. The action space and feedback determine the task-specific procedure: portfolio allocation acts through positions and returns, traffic control acts through signal configurations and queues, and maintenance support produces recommendations for review. The Agent must therefore represent both the temporal state and the consequences of the available actions. After the current time, the two tasks differ in who executes the action: among the 46 decision making works, about two in five (41\%) recommend actions for review and nearly two thirds (63\%) execute them.

\noindent\textbf{Hindsight decision.} The cell on the observed record is also empty. A hindsight decision is the action that should have been taken at an earlier time, given the outcome that has since been observed; it can be checked by replaying the earlier state in a simulator or by counterfactual estimation \citep{pearl2009causality}. Online learning measures regret against the best action in hindsight \citep{cesabianchi2006prediction}, and hindsight relabeling turns observed outcomes into training goals \citep{andrychowicz2017her}. The reviewed Agents form hindsight judgments only to improve later decisions: EpiWorld distills counterfactual rollout feedback into regime-specific lessons, ForecastCompass \citep{forecastcompass2026} revises its memories from resolved questions, FinMem \citep{finmem2023} reflects on past trades, and TimeClaw-EEL \citep{timeclaw2026explore} compares alternative executed trajectories. None outputs the hindsight decision as a result to be evaluated.

\noindent\textbf{Advisory decision.} In an advisory decision, the Agent recommends an action that a person reviews and executes. AgentFM \citep{agentfm2025} chains detection, diagnosis, and mitigation for failures in distributed databases; AgenticTwin \citep{agentictwin2026} grounds its mitigation role in retrieved knowledge; FinArena \citep{finarena2025} writes a financial report conditioned on the user's risk preference; and Smart Energy Agent \citep{smartenergyagent2026} reports appliance diagnoses with their reliability. Because a person takes the action, the procedure must expose the evidence and the risk behind each recommendation.

\noindent\textbf{Autonomous control.} In autonomous control, the Agent sets positions or control signals itself and observes their consequences. Even the context policy can be specialized for an action. FinMem \citep{finmem2023} (Section~3) separates financial memories into layers with different retention times and ranks events using recency, relevance, and importance. Significant investment memories are promoted to deeper layers, and retrieved events condition a trading decision together with the current market state. The memory policy therefore depends on the useful lifetime of financial information and trading experience. While a general retrieval module supplies storage and access, this task-specific policy decides what to retain and how it influences the action.

FinCon \citep{fincon2024} specifies financial analyst roles that deliver investment insights to a trading manager. Their output contracts include market evidence, stock selection, and risk feedback, making the implemented coordination procedure task-specific. Its prompt-controlled policy maximizes discounted cumulative profit and loss over market observations, managerial reflections, and trading actions. FinCon updates prompts between training episodes using comparative performance feedback; risk alerts within an episode also guide testing decisions. Its portfolio manager solves a mean--variance allocation over shrinkage estimates of mean daily returns and their covariance, constrained by the direction that the analyst roles select: buy permits a long position, sell a short position, and hold zero exposure. The portfolio is rebalanced daily. The analyst--manager interaction supplies the directional evidence, and the allocation problem converts that evidence into a position under the method's risk objective. TradingAgents \citep{tradingagents2024} emphasizes a different decision procedure: analyst reports feed bullish and bearish research roles, whose debate precedes trading and risk deliberation. Both systems connect evidence aggregation to an action, while their coordination and acceptance procedures differ.

CoLLMLight \citep{collmllight2026} makes the prediction-to-action relation explicit in traffic control. Each intersection observes queue lengths, moving vehicles, waiting time, and occupancy at its own and neighboring lanes. A graph represents their connectivity, and a history window preserves the observations and signal configurations that preceded the current decision; both enter the prompt with the control instructions. For complex coordination, the LLM analyzes current traffic, predicts neighboring states under each candidate configuration, and selects a signal. Simpler situations omit some reasoning stages according to the number of critical neighboring lanes, and simulation data support policy fine-tuning. Because the chosen action changes future traffic observations, evaluation measures policy outcomes in the control environment. Portfolio and traffic applications both require reasoning about future outcomes, and each defines its own state, feasible actions, feedback timing, and utility. A decision making Harness must connect these quantities to an executable policy and measure the consequences of that policy over time.

Decision procedures also differ in how they map evidence to an action. Most reviewed Agents map the current evidence to an action directly, whereas others first project the consequences of candidate actions and then choose among them (as in EpiWorld, AgriWorld, and NeuroAgentic in Section~\ref{sec:task-generation}), corresponding to model-based planning in reinforcement learning \citep{sutton2018rl}. Before choosing a direct or projection-based reasoning workflow, a new decision making Agent should specify its state, feasible actions, feedback timing, and utility. The numerical or language prediction supplies evidence, while the action contract determines how that evidence becomes a position, a control signal, or a recommendation. Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} connects each resulting artifact to an appropriate evaluation protocol.

\subsection{Reference modules for each cell}

The comparison yields five observations. First, fixed pipelines appear where the candidate operations and acceptance criterion are known before execution, as in model-library forecasting, series generation at deployment, and trading role hierarchies; feedback-driven control appears where the required evidence emerges during analysis, as in interval refinement, context-dependent forecast revision, and event search. Second, execution errors, parse checks, held-out labels, and simulator rollouts are available during system construction (ARGOS, AnomaMind, TimeClaw-Harness, CoLLMLight), whereas FLAIRR-TS, AIA Forecaster, and FinCon depend on errors, resolutions, or returns that exist only after the target resolves, which limits how often they can revise in deployment. Third, evidence for memory remains largely within distribution: TimeClaw-Harness and AnomaMind build memory from training splits of the evaluated benchmarks, FinCon updates beliefs across episodes on the same assets, and among these systems only MetaCaster evaluates reused learned artifacts on out-of-domain datasets. Fourth, system gains are rarely separable from model scale, tool access, and compute. Several comparisons pit a tool-equipped Agent against a prompted LLM without tools, and backbone sweeps change the model without fixing tools or token budget. In the matched GPT-5-nano comparison of TimeClaw-Harness, TimeSeriesScientist and ReAct reach similar average accuracy (40.5\% and 40.8\%), so the margin of TimeClaw-Harness co-varies with its native tools and memory rather than isolating dynamic control. Fifth, results are comparable only within shared protocols such as ETT forecasting or the KPI and Yahoo anomaly benchmarks; even there, slicing, windowing, metric variants such as point-adjusted event F1, and the position of the LLM knowledge cutoff relative to the test period differ across papers.

\section{Assessing a New Work with the Formula}
\label{sec:task-impact}


With all four terms of Eq.~\eqref{eq:tsa-formula} defined in Sections~\ref{sec:llm-side}--\ref{sec:task-specific-harness}, we now apply the formula to the reviewed works as a whole and to the assessment of a new work. We first synthesize the empirical distribution of modules across layers, mechanism dimensions, temporal functions, and output modes, and then show how the same map evaluates an individual contribution.

Figure~\ref{fig:contribution-trend} traces how module contributions developed across all four terms. Each term grows approximately exponentially, but at a different rate: the fitted doubling time is 9.2 months for the LLM side, 6.6 months for the Task-Specific Harness, 7.1 months for the General Harness, and 5.3 months for the General Time Series Harness. Task-specific procedures remain the largest layer and have led the LLM side since February 2024; the General Harness and General Time Series Harness overtook the LLM side only in October 2026. The composition of new work shifted accordingly: the LLM side supplied about a third of new modules until the end of 2024 (35\% and 33\%) but between 7\% and 18\% in each later period. Two conclusions follow. First, research effort is moving from adapting the model toward engineering the runtime and temporal modules around it. Second, task-specific modules still outnumber shared temporal modules by more than three to one. All panels and statistics in this paragraph cover the 162 works, dated by their first public version.

Figure~\ref{fig:contribution-trend}c classifies every Harness module in the vocabularies of Table~\ref{tab:term-basis}, from its layer through its mechanism dimensions (Table~\ref{tab:component-vocab}) to the output modes it serves. Three observations follow. First, the layers differ in what they implement. Most General Time Series Harness modules provide tools and execution (69\%): time series adaptation mainly exposes windowed statistical and spectral operations, preprocessing, and point-in-time retrieval as tools. General Harness and Task-Specific Harness modules are instead dominated by planning and orchestration (62\% and 65\%). The General Time Series Harness thus supplies 28\% of the modules for tools and execution but only 6\% of those for planning and orchestration and 3\% of those for optimization and evolution (only two modules). Temporal control of a workflow, such as horizon-aware planning or cutoff-aware execution control, is still written either as a general mechanism or inside a task procedure. Second, sharing depends on the output mode. Shared modules make up 48\% of the modules applied to regression and 46\% of those applied to classification, compared with 34\% for decision making and 64\% for generation. In regression and classification, tools and execution come from shared modules about as often as from task modules (58\% shared in regression and 50\% in classification). Planning, by contrast, remains mostly task-specific, with task modules nearly doubling shared ones (63\% task-specific in regression and 64\% in classification). In decision making, task modules outnumber shared ones in every dimension because the action space, constraints, and feedback are bound to the application. Third, the panel locates empty cells: no General Time Series Harness module optimizes or evolves the Harness for decision making, only one does so for regression and one for generation, and only two plan for decision making. In these cells, a new work fills a structural gap rather than adding another implementation.

Across the temporal functions of Section~\ref{sec:general-ts-harness}, data management is provided more than four times as often by shared modules as inside task procedures (81\% shared), whereas processing, context and memory, and validation are implemented about twice as often inside task procedures (65\%, 70\%, and 65\% task-specific). Nearly half (46\%) of the 97 General Time Series Harness modules have demonstrated reuse across at least two cells of the task grid (Table~\ref{tab:tsk-vocab}). Together with its faster growth, this pattern indicates that time series modules are consolidating around tools and data management, while orchestration, validation, and the improvement of the Harness itself remain bound to individual tasks. The same pattern holds more strongly for tested solutions to time series requirements, as the evidence below shows. Workbenches independent of any single Agent carry this consolidation further: TSFLab \citep{tsflab2026} moves forecasting knowledge, temporal data protocols, and experiment execution out of individual Agents into shared infrastructure that any compatible coding Agent can use and that maintains itself. The record schema and complete profiles appear in Appendix~\ref{app:full-ledger}.

\noindent\textbf{Placement.} We assign each module of the work to one term using the rule in Table~\ref{tab:layer-rules} and label it with that term's vocabulary (Table~\ref{tab:term-basis}): a mechanism dimension for the General Harness, a temporal function for the General Time Series Harness, and a cell of the task grid for the Task-Specific Harness. The result is a set of cells, such as a General Time Series Harness validation module used for forecasting, rather than a single label for the paper.

\noindent\textbf{Scope check.} A work states the scope of its mechanisms, and the assignment rule tests that statement. Two errors are possible. First, a general mechanism can be presented as task-specific: a prompt optimizer, an experience-distillation loop, or a feedback loop is described and evaluated on one task, even though its logic takes only the task metric as an input, as in ATLAS \citep{atlas2025}, TimeClaw-EEL \citep{timeclaw2026explore}, and TSHarness \citep{tsharness2026}. Such a module belongs to the General Harness and can be carried to another task. Conversely, a task-specific procedure can be presented as general: AQuA's manager and specialist pipeline \citep{aqua2026} reads as a general multi-agent runtime, but its roles and plan are built for factor research on forward returns, so it is a Task-Specific Harness module. Reuse evidence shows how often generality is claimed rather than tested. A General Harness or General Time Series Harness module has demonstrated reuse only when its source applies it to tasks in at least two cells of the task grid; half (50\%) of the 189 shared modules meet this standard, while the generality of the remaining half (50\%) is inferred from their interfaces.

\noindent\textbf{Size of the contribution.} Once placed and checked, a work adds to the map in one of four ways, ordered from most to least common. First, it can automate an existing analysis by combining the LLM and the General Harness with existing time series tools so that the analysis runs without an analyst; more than four in five (84\%) of the 162 works do this for at least one task. Second, it can add another implementation to an occupied cell, as most task-specific processing modules do (nearly two thirds, 65\%, of all temporal processing modules). Third, it can fill an empty or thin cell: two task cells (projection and hindsight decision) have no evaluated task output, no General Time Series Harness module optimizes the Harness for decision making, and fewer than half (43\%) of the seven shared validation modules in decision making have demonstrated reuse. Fourth, it can consolidate by moving a solution that earlier works rebuilt inside task modules into a General Time Series Harness module and demonstrating its reuse in a second cell of the task grid. A contribution of any kind counts only when the evaluation tests the time series requirement that the module addresses, such as an availability check evaluated with and without leakage control, a memory evaluated under a regime change, or an action evaluated by its consequences. Almost half (49\%) of the 143 works whose main contribution is not a benchmark or dataset report such a test, and among the 181 links between a tested requirement and the module that meets it, three in five (60\%) point to Task-Specific Harness modules and only 14\% to General Time Series Harness modules. Consolidation is therefore the rarest and most valuable contribution, and Section~\ref{sec:open-problems} takes the untested and unconsolidated requirements as its agenda.


\section{Infrastructure and Benchmarks for Time Series Agents}
\label{sec:evaluation}

Evaluation completes the construction of a Time Series Agent: a benchmark must expose the information, actions, and output requirements that the deployed workflow will encounter. AION \citep{aion2026} (Section~3.1) formalizes this connection through a task file, an accessible workspace, and a validator. The task file specifies access, prior knowledge, output form, constraints, and metrics, and the validator returns validity checks and scores for a candidate output. This separation lets an evaluator identify both an incorrectly executed analysis and a valid analysis with poor predictive performance. The reviewed benchmarks instantiate these requirements in different ways: some grade submitted code, others test evidence discovery, and others evaluate a sequence of analytical turns or recurring data releases.

\subsection{Evaluating each term}


The unit of evaluation is an episode with a declared information boundary. Its record includes the request, visible data, tool calls, intermediate artifacts, validator decisions, revisions, final output, and resource use. Each term of Eq.~\eqref{eq:tsa-formula} raises one question about this record. Did the General Harness execute the intended operations and handle failures? Did the temporal modules preserve the data window, numerical meaning, and information availability? Did the task module produce an accurate forecast, useful synthetic sample, or supported diagnosis, and does the output improve the decision that uses it? The LLM term is evaluated through the same record, since its contribution appears only in the actions it selects.

The four evaluation criteria answer these questions: process validity, temporal validity, task performance, and decision utility. Write $\zeta$ for the record, $\mathcal{E}(\zeta)$ for the evidence it uses, $\widehat{y}$ for the output of Eq.~\eqref{eq:task-contract}, and $y$ for the realized target. Process validity asks whether every call in $\zeta$ is well formed, executes, and returns a checked result. The other three criteria are
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{E}(\zeta)\subseteq\mathcal{I}_{\tau},\qquad
  S(\widehat{y},y),\qquad
  U\!\left(\alpha(\widehat{y}),y\right)-U\!\left(\alpha_0,y\right),
  \label{eq:eval-criteria}
\end{equation}
namely temporal validity, task performance under the scoring rule $S$ of the output mode \citep{gneiting2007scoring}, and decision utility, the gain in realized utility $U$ when the action $\alpha$ follows the output rather than a reference action $\alpha_0$. Temporal validity is therefore a property of the trace rather than of the output, and task performance and decision utility can disagree, because a more accurate output need not change the action; the distinction follows Murphy's separation of forecast quality from forecast value \citep{murphy1993goodforecast}.

The Paper Cards show how unevenly they are reported. Across the 162 works in scope, nearly all (95\%) report task performance and nearly two thirds (64\%) report some evidence of temporal validity, whereas only two in five (41\%) report process validity and one in five (20\%) report decision utility. Even in decision making, only about two thirds of the works (65\%) report decision utility. Testing a time series requirement means measuring the matching criterion under a controlled change: temporal validity for information availability and regime change, process validity for verifiable conclusions, and decision utility for the consequences of an action. Reported evidence of temporal validity is usually only a chronological split, which shows that the evaluation setup respects time but not that the Agent would detect or survive a violation.

Measuring these four criteria requires observing different layers of the episode record. A schema check verifies that a tool call is well formed, tool execution verifies that it runs, and a reference check verifies that its numerical output is correct. TSAG \citep{tsag2026}, for example, evaluates invocation over hard-coded tool stubs, a protocol that isolates tool selection and argument formation. TimeSeriesGym \citep{timeseriesgym2025} instead evaluates artifacts produced in an executable environment, so errors in code, data handling, and submission format affect the outcome. The choice between these protocols follows the capability being tested.

Figure~\ref{fig:evaluation-stack} follows an episode from its task contract and accessible resources through execution to the submitted artifact, and separates the three records needed to interpret its outcome. An inaccurate forecast can arise from a weak predictor, a failed tool call, or incorrectly aligned input; preserving the execution trace distinguishes these causes. Resource measurements add the cost of reaching the result and of recovering from failures.

\subsection{Existing benchmark landscape}

End-to-end engineering benchmarks test whether an Agent can turn repository resources into a working model. TimeSeriesGym \citep{timeseriesgym2025} evaluates time series ML engineering through 34 challenges from 23 sources, spanning eight temporal problem types. Each challenge combines resources such as data, code, papers, and documentation with a task description and a grading function, and the Agent must produce a usable artifact within the execution budget. The protocol runs inside a Docker environment with default limits of four hours and 50 steps, separating submission validity from task performance. This executable setup suits Agents expected to inspect a repository, adapt a model, and deliver runnable analysis. The grader closes the loop between the requested engineering task and the submitted result.

Interactive analysis benchmarks test whether an Agent maintains state across user follow-ups. TimeSage-MT \citep{timesagemt2026} examines multi-turn analytical continuity across 240 tasks spanning four levels and eight domains; each task includes a visible series slice, dialogue, analysis trajectory, reference code, numerical ground truth, and output requirements. Outcome scoring measures whether the requested result is correct, while capability diagnostics examine the steps used to obtain it. Its reference system pairs an LLM Planner and Reporter with deterministic validation, profiling, execution, and evaluation across 226 analytical skills in 36 categories. Evaluators can therefore compare LLM backbones or runtime configurations while holding the temporal workflow fixed.

Streaming evaluation adds a second timescale, where the analysis recurs as new evidence arrives. TimeSage-EV \citep{timesageev2026} provides 60 scenarios across six domains and 1,485 scenario-period instances spanning February 2023 to May 2026. At each period, the Agent receives the permitted evidence and produces structured answers and reports, while the next target release is withheld for evaluation. The benchmark measures keypoint accuracy and coverage alongside report faithfulness and quality, comparing independent and sequential contexts. Evaluators can thus test whether an Agent built for recurring economic or operational monitoring uses accumulated context alongside current evidence.

Contextual forecasting benchmarks isolate the value of external information. Dr-CiK \citep{drcik2026} provides 240 forecasting tasks paired with 8,849 documents containing supporting evidence, multi-hop dependencies, and distractors. To prevent memorization from pretraining, it shifts dates and anonymizes entities so that targets fall outside model training cutoffs. Its evaluation separates evidence recovery from forecast quality, allowing a designer to determine whether a failure occurred during retrieval or after the relevant context was found. TemporalBench \citep{temporalbench2026} varies historical and future partitions and the availability of contextual or event information across four temporal understanding and forecasting settings. These controlled conditions test when external context changes a numerical or language prediction.

Explanation benchmarks test whether an Agent's stated rationale aligns with numerical reality. TFRBench \citep{tfrbench2026} evaluates forecasting explanations alongside numerical forecasts on ten datasets across five domains. Its reference construction uses future events and outcomes to select explanatory trajectories, while test-time systems receive only historical inputs. Alongside numerical metrics such as MAE and MASE, an LLM judge scores candidate reasoning against those references for domain relevance, forecast accuracy, event relevance, and consistency between logic and numbers. These judge scores measure agreement with reference explanations conditioned on the true outcome. To evaluate an Agent for deployment, an evaluator can pair those scores with an audit of the information available at the current time.

Diagnostic and data curation benchmarks isolate upstream prerequisites of reliable modeling. TraceBench \citep{tracebench2026} studies root cause attribution through controlled interventions in dynamical systems. An Agent identifies which simulator parameter changed under varying noise, domain context, support examples, and direct versus programmatic submission, and a submitted program can also be evaluated on held-out samples. Recording accuracy alongside tokens, cost, elapsed time, tool use, Python execution, and plotting behavior exposes the trade-off between exploratory analysis and diagnostic generalization. TSQBench \citep{tsqbench2026} addresses an earlier prerequisite: selecting trustworthy temporal data. It derives controlled pairs from a common template and degrades them along specified quality dimensions to test quality identification, pairwise judgment, and downstream data selection.

The rows support concrete selection decisions: engineering artifacts call for an execution environment; multi-turn analysis calls for continuity checks; contextual forecasts call for evidence controls; and diagnosis calls for interventions with known causes.

\subsection{Temporal question answering}
\label{sec:temporal-qa}


Whereas the benchmarks in Table~\ref{tab:benchmark-families} evaluate full analytical workflows, temporal question answering isolates whether a model or Agent can interpret temporal evidence in language, a capability that maps to classification or generation when evaluated alone or acts as an intermediate check inside a larger workflow. TSAQA \citep{tsaqa2026} covers six analytical tasks, including anomaly detection, characterization, comparison, and temporal relationships, using true/false, multiple-choice, and puzzling formats across thirteen domains. MMTS-Bench \citep{mmtsbench2026} contains 2,424 question--answer pairs in Base, InWild, Match, and Align subsets, testing structural perception, temporal reasoning, sequence matching, and cross-modal alignment. Such evaluations are useful when a user asks about a trend, a change, or an event visible in a series. In an operational Agent, the same question can initiate a tool workflow: inspect the series, calculate a statistic, retrieve relevant context, and return an answer tied to those observations.

\subsection{Infrastructure for reproducible evaluation}
\label{sec:eval-infrastructure}


Evaluation infrastructure determines which parts of an episode can be replayed. AION \citep{aion2026} specifies task files, workspaces, artifacts, and validation interfaces. TimeSeriesGym \citep{timeseriesgym2025} packages challenge resources and graders into executable environments. TimeClaw-Harness \citep{timeclaw2026harness} keeps numerical series in a workspace and exposes executable analytical tools, trajectories, and memory. Together, these platforms make three replay boundaries explicit: the task request, the execution environment, and the persistent analytical state.

Most Time Series Agents embed domain support inside their own Harness, where it serves a single Agent and must be rebuilt for the next. TSFLab \citep{tsflab2026}, the first workbench dedicated to time series forecasting research among the reviewed works, moves this infrastructure outside the Agent into four shared components: a forecasting guide that documents workflows, evaluation settings, and common pitfalls; multi-level knowledge cards covering 323 methods, 93 datasets, and 71 reusable model units at summary, card, and source levels; an experiment runner that executes declared protocols under one data, training, and evaluation specification; and a recursive improver that merges verified methods, datasets, and bug fixes against their papers and code after checking usability, complexity, and consistency. In paper reproduction and benchmarking, the workbench raised task completion by 20--163\% and reduced fresh tokens per completed task by 11--66\% relative to Agents without it. Because this substrate is decoupled from any single Agent, new coding Agents can use it without modification as their numerical tool layer while adding evidence retrieval, model selection, revision, and reporting, and the output of one task (such as a verified reproduction) becomes a reusable resource for the next. Logging the dataset version, model configuration, tool inputs and outputs, and resulting artifacts makes runs directly comparable across Harness designs: a direct forecasting baseline and an Agent-controlled run can share identical model routines, data windows, and compute budgets, ensuring that performance differences reflect the Agent's selection procedure and execution cost.

TimeClaw-Harness \citep{timeclaw2026harness} connects a numerical workspace to temporal tools and multimodal memory, making explicit where the series is stored, how analytical operations execute, and how past trajectories are retrieved. Along the lower path, the Harness promotes recurring executed procedures into candidate tools and validates them on held-out similar tasks before registration. Reproducing a run therefore requires saving the workspace, memory, and tool-set state alongside the model and task inputs. TimeClaw-EEL \citep{timeclaw2026explore} compares executed trajectories over this state to distill reusable experience, showing why the trajectory log and memory state must also be versioned when an Agent optimizes its own procedures.


\subsection{Selecting a benchmark for a new scenario}

Benchmark selection follows the same task grid and Harness vocabularies as system construction. A developer starts with the required output mode and the specific module whose contribution is being tested. A numerical model library supplies callable forecasting capabilities; an execution environment tests whether the Agent can deliver a working analysis; a controlled context benchmark measures how external evidence changes the result. Each resource answers a distinct development or evaluation question.

For the demand planning example in Figure~\ref{fig:task-function-grid}, TSFLab \citep{tsflab2026} supplies model and dataset routines for the numerical tool layer. A direct forecasting run provides a baseline for the Agent's selection or revision procedure. Dr-CiK \citep{drcik2026} provides a controlled test of evidence retrieval and contextual prediction, including supporting and distracting documents. TimeSeriesGym \citep{timeseriesgym2025} tests whether the Agent can construct the required executable artifacts under a challenge's grading protocol. An inventory simulator then evaluates how the forecasts affect stockouts, holding costs, and feasible actions under the chosen operational assumptions. These evaluations isolate numerical prediction, evidence use, execution, and downstream utility within the same proposed workflow.

A monitoring or incident-response scenario combines a different slice of Table~\ref{tab:benchmark-families}: TimeSage-MT \citep{timesagemt2026} and TimeSage-EV \citep{timesageev2026} test multi-turn continuity and recurring revision across evidence releases, TSQBench \citep{tsqbench2026} tests upstream data quality filtering, and TraceBench \citep{tracebench2026} tests root cause attribution under known interventions. Matching each benchmark to the capability it isolates keeps evaluation interpretable: a data quality judgment, a runnable program, a forecast, and a diagnosis are distinct outputs that require distinct evidence.

By contrast, generation and decision making lack general Agent benchmarks and must be evaluated through how downstream learners or environments use their outputs. MERIT \citep{merit2025} evaluates representations learned from generated positive views on downstream temporal tasks, whereas MetaCaster \citep{metacaster2026} evaluates forecasters trained on synthetic series through forecasting error. The downstream learner therefore defines the evaluation of generated series, using separate data splits to revise the generator and to test the trained model. For decision making, FinCon \citep{fincon2024} evaluates trading and portfolio actions using cumulative return, Sharpe ratio, and maximum drawdown, while CoLLMLight \citep{collmllight2026} evaluates traffic-signal control in CityFlow through average travel and waiting times. For a new application, these setups guide the choice of action space, outcome measure, and feedback schedule.

A module comparison should preserve the backbone, accessible tools, information boundary, and resource budget while changing the module under investigation. The record should connect the input, intermediate artifacts, checks, and final output, so that an improvement can be attributed to processing, retrieval, validation, or the task procedure. A repository supplies the means of implementing the comparison; a benchmark supplies a declared protocol for judging it.
\section{Open Problems}\label{sec:open-problems}


The assessment in Section~\ref{sec:task-impact} defines what counts as an open problem. Agents already automate most analytical workflows, and higher scores on existing protocols show only that a workflow runs well; they do not show that a time series requirement is met. The open problems below address time series requirements that current works claim to meet but rarely test, or test only inside one task module. These include verifiable conclusions (tested in 29\% of the 49 work--task pairs that claim them), information availability (enforced only by the test split in 60\% and tested in 45\% of the 20 pairs that claim it), robustness to changing regimes, decision consequences beyond trading and traffic, and operational deployment (tested in only four works). Each problem sits at one term of Eq.~\eqref{eq:tsa-formula}: the division of labor between the LLM and the Harness, shared context and memory, the transfer of task solutions into shared modules, the temporal validity and process validity of evaluation, the decision utility of task outputs, and the empty cells of the task grid. Each problem is stated together with the controlled comparison that would count as a solution. A gain of a few points in accuracy or speed does not count.

\noindent\textbf{Dividing temporal capabilities between the model and the Harness.} ChatTS \citep{chatts2025} learns to interpret temporal attributes and language instructions, whereas tool-based systems such as TS-Agent \citep{tsagent2025} explicitly compute analytical evidence. Their combination raises a concrete allocation problem: which judgments belong to learned temporal perception, and which should trigger a numerical tool? Experiments that hold the backbone fixed while varying the tool policy can compare numerical error, evidence quality, and execution cost to show where model adaptation reduces tool dependence and where explicit computation remains necessary. This division of labor also governs verifiability: a computed statistic can be checked directly, whereas a perceived pattern must be verified against numerical evidence. Verifiability is critical in anomaly diagnosis, yet only about one in five (21\%) of the 42 works with an anomaly task test it. The question becomes sharper as time series become a native modality of omni-modal LLMs (Section~\ref{sec:llm-native}): perception can move into the model, while exact statistics, calibrated distributions, and availability checks must remain auditable Harness tools. A controlled comparison should hold the Harness fixed, replace tool-based series views with native perception, and measure which outputs change and which remain verifiable.

\noindent\textbf{Memory under changing information.} TimeSage-MT \citep{timesagemt2026} tests continuity within a dialogue, whereas TimeSage-EV \citep{timesageev2026} tests recurring analysis as releases accumulate. Together, these settings distinguish remembering a past result from deciding whether it still applies. Nonstationarity is tested in 24 work--task pairs, mostly as held-out domains or regimes for the whole system, and no test isolates whether retained context is revalidated when the regime changes. To connect memory quality to the temporal validity of later actions, an evaluation should hold a stored explanation fixed while altering its supporting observation, release vintage, or regime, and then check whether the Agent revalidates, revises, or retires that explanation. Memory also requires a stable provenance record: AION \citep{aion2026} links the task file, workspace, and validator, while TimeSage-EV \citep{timesageev2026} records evidence release time as part of recurring analysis. An evaluator can replay an earlier analysis after a later data release arrives to test whether the General Time Series Harness preserves the original information boundary.

\noindent\textbf{Transferring learned procedures.} TimeClaw-Harness \citep{timeclaw2026harness} promotes generated tools using held-out execution, and TimeClaw-EEL \citep{timeclaw2026explore} distills successful trajectories into external experience. The open question is how far each acquired capability transfers. An evaluation can hold out a task cell, a domain, or a temporal regime while keeping the base LLM fixed. When the evaluation records tool identity, training episodes, and promotion criteria, it can distinguish reusable procedure learning from repeated adaptation to similar instances. TimeSage-EV \citep{timesageev2026} enforces a concrete availability rule: a skill becomes active only in the next period. Transfer applies to engineering safeguards as well as learned procedures. Among the 181 module links that meet a tested requirement, three in five (60\%) are task modules, so a leakage guard or shift-robust memory that works in one system is usually rebuilt in the next. To turn a single-task solution into shared infrastructure, developers must extract it into a General Time Series Harness module and verify that it works in a second cell of the task grid across different sampling frequencies, units, and availability rules. TimeClaw-Harness \citep{timeclaw2026harness} and the skill library of TimeSage-MT \citep{timesagemt2026} provide shared tool layers on which to test such transfer.

\noindent\textbf{Evidence-sensitive evaluation.} Dr-CiK \citep{drcik2026} controls supporting and distracting documents, TraceBench \citep{tracebench2026} controls diagnostic interventions, and TFRBench \citep{tfrbench2026} scores reasoning against outcome-conditioned references. Each protocol probes a different link between evidence and output. By pairing controlled evidence perturbations with an executable trace, an evaluator can verify both that a correct answer relies on information available to the Agent and that removing a relevant observation alters the conclusion. Such tests tie process validity directly to the operations that produced the answer. The same principle applies to information availability. Most works enforce a cutoff by construction or assume one, and only nine compare a system with and without leakage control or measure foreknowledge directly \citep{openforecaster2026,tiem2026,aiaforecaster2025}. When an evaluator injects post-cutoff evidence and checks whether the Agent detects or ignores it, temporal validity becomes a measured property of the Agent rather than an assumption of the data split.

\noindent\textbf{Closing the prediction-to-decision loop.} Forecast quality matters through the decisions it informs. FinCon \citep{fincon2024} couples analytical evidence to portfolio positions, and CoLLMLight \citep{collmllight2026} connects local traffic observations to network control. Both settings introduce action costs, risk, delayed feedback, and feedback loops onto future observations. Evaluations should therefore compare complete decision procedures under identical information and action constraints, recording both task performance and downstream decision utility. When a method alters recommendations over time, it must also be tested across repeated closed-loop decisions where its own actions shift the future data distribution. Decision consequences are tested in about a third (33\%) of the 46 decision making works, almost all in trading or traffic control, and only about two thirds (65\%) report decision utility. Operational deployment, the strongest test of practical value, appears in only four works \citep{trafficr12025,steward2026,epicoach2026,finanalyst2026}. Domains such as energy, maintenance, healthcare, and supply chains require the same closed-loop evidence.

\noindent\textbf{Projection as an evaluated output.} Generating futures under assumed conditions is a well defined task that has no evaluated output in the reviewed works (Section~\ref{sec:task-generation}). Systems such as EpiWorld \citep{epiworld2026}, AgriWorld \citep{agriworld2026}, and NeuroAgentic \citep{neuroagentic2026} use projections only as intermediate inputs to choose actions. Because a counterfactual projection cannot be scored against the single realized future, evaluating it requires alternative references: consistency with a simulator or known physical mechanism, coverage of decision-relevant scenarios, and agreement with interventions that were subsequently executed. A simulation environment that rolls out the same initial state under multiple candidate actions would make projection a directly testable output.

\noindent\textbf{Hindsight decision as an evaluated output.} Choosing the action that should have been taken at an earlier time is the other empty cell of the task grid. Agents form such hindsight decisions inside reflection and lesson memories, as in EpiWorld, ForecastCompass \citep{forecastcompass2026}, FinMem \citep{finmem2023}, and TimeClaw-EEL \citep{timeclaw2026explore}, but never expose them as evaluated outputs. An evaluator can check a hindsight decision directly by replaying the earlier state in a simulator under the proposed action and measuring its regret against the best action in hindsight \citep{cesabianchi2006prediction}. Scoring the hindsight decision on its own also tests whether the stored lessons that guide later decisions are sound.

\noindent\textbf{Probabilistic outputs.} Only about one in five (21\%) of the 84 works that forecast a value or an event report a probabilistic forecast; the remaining works report only point forecasts. Because decisions under risk require a calibrated distribution rather than a single point estimate, forecasting Agents should be evaluated with proper scoring rules \citep{gneiting2007scoring}. Agents that revise numerical forecasts using qualitative context, such as Last-Mile \citep{lastmile2026} and CastFSR \citep{castfsr2026}, should be compared on the calibration of their revised distributions as well as on point error.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This survey organizes Time Series Agents through one decomposition: a Time Series Agent consists of an LLM, a General Harness, and a Time Series Harness, which splits into a General Time Series Harness and a Task-Specific Harness. The LLM is the caller of the Harness, and time series foundation models are tools within it. We assign every reviewed Harness module to one term by its scope of reuse and describe each term with a dedicated vocabulary: five mechanism dimensions for the General Harness, four temporal functions of the availability record for the General Time Series Harness, and a task grid of four output modes on the two sides of the current time for the Task-Specific Harness. For a new scenario, this assignment answers three construction questions: which runtime mechanisms can be reused from a general Agent, which operations require temporal semantics shared across tasks (such as availability time and chronology guards), and which procedures must be rebuilt around the task objective. The task grid then maps these choices to reference modules for each cell, while evaluation follows the same terms to separate process validity, temporal validity, task performance, and decision utility.

Applied to the reviewed works, this decomposition shows that most of them automate an existing analysis, and that projection and hindsight decision have no evaluated task output. Furthermore, tested solutions to time series requirements appear in fewer than half of the works that propose methods and mostly stay inside task modules rather than shared time series modules, while verifiable diagnosis, measured information availability, robustness to regime change, and deployment evidence remain largely untested. Progress should therefore be judged by the requirements a work meets under controlled tests and by whether its solutions move from task modules into shared time series modules. Building a reliable system requires aligning the caller, the runtime, and the evaluation: the Harness must provide temporal interfaces that match the capabilities of the LLM, execution must respect the information boundary at the current time, and evaluation must separate whether a workflow ran correctly from whether its output is accurate and useful once acted on. With this alignment, researchers and practitioners can assemble Time Series Agents from tested, reusable modules and compare them on the requirements that matter for the decisions they support.
