Abstract
Time-series reasoning remains a significant challenge in multimodal large language
models (MLLMs) due to the dynamic temporal patterns, ambiguous semantics, and
lack of temporal priors. In this work, we introduce TimeMaster, a reinforcement
learning (RL)-based method that enables time-series MLLMs to perform structured,
interpretable reasoning directly over visualized time-series inputs and task prompts.
TimeMaster adopts a three-part structured output format, reasoning, classification,
and domain-specific extension, and is optimized via a composite reward function
that aligns format adherence, prediction accuracy, and open-ended insight quality.
The model is trained using a two-stage pipeline: we first apply supervised finetuning (SFT) to establish a good initialization, followed by Group Relative Policy
Optimization (GRPO) at the token level to enable stable and targeted rewarddriven improvement in time-series reasoning. We evaluate TimeMaster on the
TimerBed benchmark across six real-world classification tasks based on Qwen2.5VL-3B-Instruct. TimeMaster achieves state-of-the-art performance, outperforming
both classical time-series models and few-shot GPT-4o by over 14.6% and 7.3%
performance gain, respectively. Notably, TimeMaster goes beyond time-series
classification: it also exhibits expert-like reasoning behavior, generates contextaware explanations, and delivers domain-aligned insights. Our results highlight
that reward-driven RL can be a scalable and promising path toward integrating
temporal understanding into time-series MLLMs.

Introduction

Time series analysis is a fundamental technique in data mining, enabling the modeling of temporal
patterns and informing decision-making across critical domains such as healthcare [1, 2], industrial
monitoring [3, 4], and environmental surveillance [5]. While the advancements of deep learning have
propelled progress in classical time series tasks like forecasting [6, 7, 8, 9, 10] and classification [7, 9],
the recent surge of large language models (LLMs) [11, 12, 13, 14] invites a broader question: can the
rich reasoning abilities of LLMs be extended to the temporal domain, yielding a general-purpose
time series analysis that not only predicts but also reasons, explains and advises?
Reasoning is an emergent capability of modern LLMs, enabled by their strong natural language
understanding and instruction-following abilities, as represented by Chain-of-Thought Prompting
[15]. These strengths provide a natural foundation for moving beyond traditional tasks toward
interpretable time-series reasoning (TsR) [16]. Despite this, current approaches on time-series
multimodal LLMs (MLLMs) face fundamental limitations in achieving deep, robust reasoning.
Prompt-based methods [17, 18, 19], while flexible and capable of zero- and few-shot generalization,

like GPT-4o [20]. This could lead to shallow or inconsistent reasoning over time-series data. On
the other hand, post-training strategies, including supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on synthetic [21, 22]
or human-annotated [23, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 22] time series datasets, can introduce better domain
alignment. Yet, these approaches often suffer from limited data diversity, overfitting to template-like
patterns, and constrained generalizability. Critically, these datasets with aligned time-series and text
pairs often fail to fully capture the breadth of reasoning skills and domain knowledge necessary for
robust temporal comprehension. As such, training time-series MLLMs solely on static datasets tends
to produce superficial outputs that lack the depth, reliability, and interpretability required for effective
time series reasoning.
In this work, we propose TimeMaster, a reinforcement learning (RL) [29]-based method for multimodal time series reasoning. TimeMaster addresses critical limitations of existing approaches,
including dependence on large-scale manually collected datasets, weak reasoning capabilities, and
poor performance across diverse time series domains. Instead, TimeMaster learns through iterative RL
training, enabling the model to progressively acquire, refine, and generalize its time-series reasoning
abilities in a self-improving manner.
Central to our approach is a structured output format that unifies the model’s response into three
components: reasoning, classification, and extension. Given a time series input, the model first
generates a coherent chain of thought to explain its analysis, then outputs a classification result,
and optionally provides an extension such as suggestions, diagnostics, or actionable insights based
on specific task requirements. To optimize the model, TimeMaster employs a two-stage learning
pipeline. First, we initialize the model with basic task alignment through SFT on a high-quality
dataset containing just 1k samples, a fraction of the scale used in prior work (e.g., approximately
200k in [27]). Then, TimeMaster adopts RL training driven by a composite reward function: a
format reward to enforce structural consistency, a hard reward to ensure classification accuracy, and
a soft reward to evaluate the quality of open-ended extension. Both the format and hard rewards
provide strict binary feedback. The soft reward, due to the open-ended nature of the extension, is
computed using an LLM-as-a-Judge [30]. This reward design enables RL to shape both precision and
expressiveness in multimodal time series reasoning. Figure 1 illustrates an example of TimeMaster
applied to a complex reasoning task on electromyography (EMG) signal classification, highlighting
its ability to perform structured reasoning and informed classification.
We evaluate TimeMaster on T IMER B ED [17], a comprehensive benchmark designed to assess timeseries reasoning across six real-world classification tasks. Empirical results show that TimeMaster
achieves state-of-the-art performance, surpassing both traditional time-series baselines and strong
multimodal LLMs: it outperforms few-shot GPT-4o by 7.3% and classical time-series models by
over 14.6% on average. More importantly, TimeMaster exhibits emergent expert-like reasoning
behaviors, including iterative self-correction, temporal pattern recognition, and the articulation of
causal explanations. These capabilities substantially enhance the interpretability and real-world

scenarios, TimeMaster moves beyond outputting simple labels; it generates rich, human-aligned
explanations that reflect domain reasoning, improving both transparency and trustworthiness. Overall,
this work demonstrates a promising step toward equipping multimodal LLMs with time-series
reasoning capabilities, facilitating more context-aware, interpretable, and actionable insights in
real-world applications.

Related Work

Time-series foundation models. Recent studies have explored diverse paradigms for leveraging
large language models (LLMs) in time series analysis, with a particular focus on enhancing predictive
performance. A prominent direction [31, 25, 32, 33, 34] involves reprogramming pre-trained LLMs,
such as GPT-2 [35] and LLaMA-7B [14], as feature extractors by reformulating time series data into
textual sequences. This formulation enables direct inference or forecasting by leveraging LLMs’
strong sequential modeling abilities. To support more expressive and context-rich time series analysis,
recent work has also emphasized the multimodal nature of real-world applications, integrating intraseries semantics with external contextual signals [36]. Typical strategies include augmenting time
series with domain-relevant textual descriptions [31, 37, 32, 33], aligning physiological signals with
medical documentation [38], linking stock price trends to financial news [23, 39], and incorporating
geographic information [40], traffic records [41], or extra event data [42] into traffic flow modeling.
Reasoning in time-series LLMs. With the growing integration of language models into temporal
modeling, the field is moving toward a unified reasoning paradigm centered on explanation and
causal inference. Despite its promise, time series reasoning (TsR) remains in an early stage. Recent
efforts [18, 43, 19] explore prompting strategies tailored to structured reasoning in time series tasks.
For example, VL-Time [17] introduces visualized time series directly into GPT-4o for zero-shot or
few-shot reasoning, offering an alternative to purely numerical inputs. Nevertheless, such methods
are constrained by the absence of temporal priors and domain-specific inductive biases in standard
LLM pretraining. To address these limitations, some approaches adopt post-training strategies using
synthetic time-series–text paired corpora. For example, Time-MQA [27] aggregates 200k timeseries–text pairs annotated by GPT-4o across various domains for supervised fine-tuning. However,
constructing such datasets remains resource-intensive, often relying on manual schema design or
commercial LLM APIs. This poses challenges to encompassing broad domain knowledge and
capturing the full diversity of real-world temporal phenomena. Our work proposes a cost-efficient yet
effective strategy to equip multimodal LLMs with time-series reasoning capabilities, using only a
small amount of task-specific supervision.
RL in large language models. Reinforcement learning (RL) [29] has emerged as a powerful
framework for advancing the capabilities of LLMs. One of the most influential approaches in
this domain is Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) [44, 45, 46, 47], which
introduces human preference as a guiding signal during the fine-tuning stage. By optimizing model
outputs against reward models trained on human judgments, RLHF enables LLMs to generate
responses that are more aligned with human expectations in terms of helpfulness, coherence, and
ethical appropriateness. In addition, RL is increasingly used to enhance the reasoning abilities of
LLMs [48, 49, 50]. Recent work, such as DeepSeek-R1 [51], incorporates RL objectives to encourage
logical reasoning and structured problem-solving. Instead of solely optimizing for linguistic fluency,
these models are trained to maximize rule-based rewards, such as correctness in mathematical
derivations, enabling LLMs to perform complex reasoning tasks with greater accuracy and internal
consistency. Given the growing demand for reliable decision-making in the time-series domain, we
propose leveraging the potential of RL by developing strategies that address key gaps in time-series
reasoning with multimodal LLMs.

TimeMaster

Time-series data, collected from diverse sources such as scientific experiments, industrial systems,
and healthcare monitoring, underpins a wide range of classification tasks critical to real-world
decision-making. Beyond conventional classification, practical decision-making often demands
3

models capable of reasoning over temporal patterns, articulating causal relationships, and proposing actionable insights. While recent time-series models [9, 52, 52, 53] have achieved substantial
progress, they predominantly focus on optimizing numeric prediction objectives, often at the expense of interpretability and explainability. Meanwhile, LLMs such as [14, 13] excel in natural
language generation but are not inherently equipped for structured temporal reasoning, limiting their
effectiveness in understanding and reasoning about the complex dynamics of real-world time series.
In this section, we present TimeMaster, an RL-enhanced time-series MLLM that acquires expert-level
reasoning over time-series signals. It accepts multimodal inputs and outputs a structured explanation,
classification, and optional extension, optimized for both accuracy and interpretability.
3.1

Multimodal Input

Time series reasoning refers to the open-ended and structured capability of an MLLM to interpret
and analyze temporal patterns by jointly considering raw time series data X and auxiliary contextual
information q for classification tasks. Formally, let X = {xt }Tt=1 denote a multivariate time series
of length T , where each xt ∈ RD represents a D-dimensional observation at time step t. Let
q = {q1 , q2 , . . . } be a sequence of textual tokens, such as natural language instructions or domain
knowledge, where each qi ∈ Vtext is sampled from a textual vocabulary specific to the MLLM. Unlike
traditional approaches [31, 25, 32, 33, 34, 9, 52] that focus solely on predictions, our formulation
of time series reasoning empowers the model to uncover latent temporal structures, infer causal
relationships, and generate interpretable insights through natural language. It bridges low-level
numerical signals with high-level reasoning objectives within a unified, multimodal framework.
We formally define a time-series MLLM as πθ parameterized by θ, which specifies a conditional
probability distribution over the output sequence y = {y1 , y2 , . . . , yK }, given the inputs X and q:

Optional Extension

The think block expresses open-ended time-series reasoning in a “chain-of-thought” [15] style. This
includes pattern recognition, trend analysis, causal inference, or domain-specific signal interpretation.
For example, in an EMG signal, the model might describe amplitude variation, waveform shape, and
frequency shifts before concluding. The class block outputs a discrete label, indicating the predicted
class (e.g., Normal, Myopathy, Neuropathy). This enables rigorous evaluation against ground truth
during training. The extension block optionally provides follow-up insights or suggestions, such
as diagnostics, forecasts, remediation plans, or next-step actions. This facilitates practical usability,
especially in domains like medicine and industrial monitoring.

simultaneously: structural correctness, classification accuracy, and the open-ended relevance of advice.
To impose these multifaceted requirements, we construct a composite reward that decomposes along
three axes: format reward, hard reward, and soft reward. Each targets a distinct dimension of output
quality, as visualized in Figure 2.

Format reward rfmt . The format reward enforces strict adherence to the predefined output
structure. A regex-based parser automatically checks if the generated response contains correctly
nested and ordered <think>, <class>, and <extension> tags, with non-empty reasoning and
classification spans. If the format is fully correct, a binary reward of rfmt (y) = 1 is assigned;
otherwise, rfmt (y) = 0. Figure 2 (left) demonstrates a case of format reward, where clinically valid
reasoning is penalized if the tag structure is broken (format error), ensuring that the model maintains
syntactic discipline throughout training.

results. Given ground-truth labels c⋆ ∈ C, an exact match between the predicted <class> tag and the

Soft reward rsoft . As the content of <extension>, such as suggestions, diagnostics, or actionable

to evaluate each extension along four dimensions: (1) Specificity: Assess whether the extension
is tailored to the given context, rather than offering generic or broadly applicable suggestions.
(2) Appropriateness: Determine whether the extension is reasonable and well-aligned with the
preceding reasoning and prediction. (3) Relevance: Evaluate whether the extension logically follows
from the prior context, avoiding fallback or disconnected recommendations. (4) Depth: Judge

Optimization for Time-Series Reasoning

adopt a two-stage training pipeline. We begin with SFT on a low-resource dataset consisting of 1k

by aligning the model with the desired structured output format and domain semantics. We then
proceed to RL, where we optimize the model using token-level Group Relative Policy Optimization
(GRPO) [49, 51], which leverages group-wise statistics to normalize advantage estimates without
requiring a separate value network.

4.2

Key Insights from Empirical Evaluations

In this section, we distill key insights from our some empirical findings to highlight the core challenges
of TsR with MLLMs and to demonstrate the effectiveness of TimeMaster in addressing them.
Takeaway I: Training is Essential for Structured Time-Series Reasoning. We begin by comparing
the reasoning chains of three models, i.e., VL-Time (Zero-shot), Qwen2.5-VL (SFT), and TimeMaster
(SFT+RL), as shown in Fig. 3(a). The results show that trained Qwen2.5-VL (SFT) and TimeMaster
(SFT+RL) exhibit clear, structured reasoning patterns that map latent temporal cues to domain
concepts. For example, in the HAR case labeled standing (Fig. 3a), Qwen2.5-VL (SFT) detects
low variance across accelerometer axes, links it to a static-posture pattern, and selects standing
accordingly. The full TimeMaster model goes further: it performs contrastive elimination of dynamic
activities, explicitly mentions the symmetry of micro-fluctuations, and justifies its decision with a
8

causal chain that mirrors expert diagnostic logic. This demonstrates the RL stage’s ability to refine
decision-making beyond the SFT prior. But VL-Time, despite using GPT-4o, offers only a shallow
description and prematurely predicts laying. These findings underscore that training is essential for
incorporating task-specific time-series priors and enabling structured, context-aware TsR.
Takeaway II: RL Unlocks Expert-Level Time-Series Reasoning. We evaluate three models on
two physiological signals: an ECG labeled as other cardiac rhythms (Fig. 3(b)) and an EMG labeled
as neuropathy (Fig. 3(c)). Qwen2.5-VL (SFT) shows basic feature recognition, such as identifying
absent P waves or amplitude variations. However, its reasoning remains shallow and templatedriven, leading to misclassifications. For example, it predicts the ECG as atrial fibrillation based on
“irregular RR intervals” while ignoring noted noise and uncertainty, and mislabels the EMG as healthy
using the amplitude range alone. TimeMaster (RL) demonstrates improved diagnostic reasoning
by incorporating contextual cues and prototype deviation analysis. In the ECG case, it integrates
multiple uncertain cues (e.g., possible noise, irregular RR intervals) to rule out standard arrhythmia
classes. For the EMG, it captures both morphological and temporal markers, such as sharp onset
and brief duration, to support a neuropathic interpretation. Yet, pure RL may be limited by slow
exploration and convergence due to the lack of temporal priors. TimeMaster (RL+SFT) mitigates
this by using lightweight SFT as a warm-up to inject basic domain knowledge. This provides a
good initialization for the follow-up RL training, steers RL toward clinically salient dimensions
early, and improves convergence and stability. As shown, RL with warm-up further facilitates the
emergence of structured diagnostic reasoning via reward-guided exploration, alleviating dependence
on predefined templates and enabling expert-level interpretations through multi-scale pattern synthesis
(e.g., amplitude, waveform morphology, and temporal stability) into a cohesive reasoning framework.

5

Conclusion

In this work, we present TimeMaster, a novel RL-enhanced time-series MLLMs designed for
structured reasoning over visualized time-series data. Our design integrates a two-stage training
pipeline: beginning with SFT for foundational alignment, followed by token-level RL optimization,
which jointly optimizes for format adherence, predictive accuracy, and the generation of open-ended
suggestions. Our empirical evaluations verify that TimeMaster achieves state-of-the-art performance,
outperforming both classical time-series models and few-shot GPT-4o. More importantly, it exhibits
emergent expert-level reasoning abilities, such as causal pattern inference, while also generating
context-aware, clinically meaningful extensions. Our results suggest that RL can be a powerful
9

catalyst to unlock the deep, interpretable temporal reasoning capabilities in time-series MLLMs,
laying a scalable and promising foundation for building robust time-series MLLMs.

Our work still has some limitations. We have only provided an initial implementation of a quantifiable
reasoning evaluation framework, where our composite reward combines structural validity, classification accuracy, and suggestion quality. However, the model may still exhibit occasional mismatches
between the reasoning chain and the final decision. Future research could explore trajectory-aware or
symbolic rewards to explicitly align intermediate reasoning steps with final outputs and to promote
diverse yet internally consistent reasoning patterns. In addition, we only incorporate the task description that accompanies each time series instance. While this supports contextual alignment to some
extent, enriching the input with broader domain narratives or linking it to external knowledge sources
(e.g., news reports) may enhance decision granularity and improve generalization across domains.
Nevertheless, our work demonstrates a promising step toward equipping MLLMs with structured and
interpretable reasoning capabilities in time-series domains.

B

Broader Impacts

One of the major impacts of our study is that it establishes a feasible and scalable research pathway
for time-series multimodal large language models (MLLMs). By integrating a lightweight supervised
warm-up phase using a small set of annotated examples with a token-level reinforcement learning
pipeline (GRPO) guided by composite rewards, we demonstrate that reinforcement learning can effectively enable strong temporal reasoning capabilities without incurring prohibitive annotation costs.
Remarkably, even a single 3B-parameter model is sufficient to achieve state-of-the-art performance
across diverse domains (e.g., healthcare and electricity monitoring), highlighting the advantages of
our data- and compute-efficient methodology for developing temporal foundation models. While
these results are encouraging, we acknowledge that robust reasoning and suggestion capabilities must
be supported by human expert oversight, and that the model remains inadequate for deployment in
high-stakes decision-making scenarios. We highlight our contribution as a valuable foundation for
future research in time-series MLLMs and a meaningful step toward the broader development of
foundation models for time-series data.

E

Dataset Statistics

In this section, we provide additional details on the real-world time-series classification datasets used
in our experiments, drawn from T IMER B ED [17]. These datasets span a wide range of domains,
signal characteristics, and reasoning complexities. Their key statistics are summarized in Table 4,
while detailed descriptions are provided below.
• Right Whale Call Detection (RCW)3 involves identifying North Atlantic right whale vocalizations from underwater acoustic recordings. A distinctive short, rising “whoop” sound serves as
a necessary and sufficient indicator of whale presence, enabling direct mapping between signal
features and class labels.
• Transient Electromagnetic Events (TEE)4 contains satellite-collected power density signals
from the FORTE satellite, used to classify various types of lightning-related electromagnetic
discharges. Each class corresponds to a well-defined physical signature in the waveform, making
the task pattern-centric and signal-driven.
• Electrocardiogram (ECG)5 includes single-lead ECG recordings used to diagnose cardiac
arrhythmias. Accurate classification requires holistic interpretation of multiple waveform components—such as P-wave absence, irregular R-R intervals, and atrial fibrillation indicators.
• Electromyogram (EMG)6 comprises EMG signals used to distinguish healthy subjects from
patients with neuropathic or myopathic disorders. The task involves reasoning over diverse
waveform features including long-duration, high-amplitude motor unit potentials and polyphasic
activity.
• Human Activity Recognition (HAR)7 uses tri-axial accelerometer data from smartphones to
classify six daily physical activities such as walking, standing, and lying. Labels are automatically
generated and reflect latent user-dependent patterns, adding complexity to the modeling process.
• Computer Type Usage (CTU)8 aims to differentiate between desktop and laptop usage based on
24-hour electricity consumption traces. Although labels are programmatically derived, classification relies on subtle temporal usage signatures influenced by individual behavior.
As shown in Table 4, each dataset in T IMER B ED is associated with one of three reasoning types,
reflecting varying levels of complexity and semantic abstraction:

• Simple deterministic reasoning: Tasks where a single salient feature is sufficient to determine
the label. The decision boundary is often explicit and rule-based, allowing for direct mapping
from input to output. For example, the presence of a distinct acoustic pattern in whale calls or a
spike in satellite signal indicates class membership.
• Complex deterministic reasoning: Tasks that require the integration of multiple temporal
patterns or signal components to make a decision. These problems demand holistic reasoning
over structured signal relationships, such as diagnosing arrhythmias by jointly considering P-wave
morphology, heart rate regularity, and waveform intervals.
• Probabilistic reasoning: Tasks characterized by user-specific or hidden variables, where labels
are automatically derived and may not be directly observable in the input. As a result, the model
must learn to infer outcomes under ambiguity and latent context, such as predicting user activity
or device type based on behavior-driven time series.

F

Baselines

We follow the recent time-series reasoning benchmark [17] and the survey [36] for the selection of
the following baselines. Except for Time-MQA, other results in Table 1 are adopted from [17].
• Transformer-based Models ( Transformer [55], Autoformer [56],Informer [57], FEDformer [58], PatchTST [53], iTransformer [52] ): apture long-range dependencies in timedomain sequences using self-attention mechanisms. Serve as strong baselines for time-series
modeling.
• CNN-based Models (TimesNet [9]): Leverage convolutional operations to extract temporal
features across different time scales.
• MLP-based Models (DLinear [59]): Employ lightweight feedforward layers for efficient
modeling of local patterns in time-series data.
• GPT-4o (Numeric, Zero-shot) [17]: Receives tokenized numerical time-series data as input
without any demonstrations. Serves as a unimodal, language-only baseline for evaluating
zero-shot generalization.
• GPT-4o (Numeric, Few-shot) [17]: Extends the numeric input with a few in-context examples9 per class. Evaluates the model’s few-shot reasoning capability using raw numerical
sequences in standard prompt format.
• VL-Time (Zero-shot) [17]: Provides multimodal LLMs with visualized time-series plots
and natural language prompts. Assesses general reasoning ability without demonstrations.
• VL-Time (Few-shot) [17]: Adds a few in-context examples10 to the visual-language input,
enabling pattern generalization with minimal supervision.
• Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (Time-MQA) [27]: A fine-tuned Qwen2.5-7B model11 on the TSQA
dataset (∼ 200k pairs) for time-series question answering. It enables multi-task reasoning
and open-ended question answering via natural language prompts.

Implementation Details of TimeMaster

G.1

Plotting Time Series as Images

Visualizing time series as images offers an intuitive and cost-efficient approach to understanding
temporal patterns, and has been widely adopted in recent studies [54, 36]. Following the methodology
of VL-Following Time [17], we transform time-series data into RGB line plots in the time domain to
serve as inputs for the vision-language model, ensuring a fair comparison. Each channel is rendered
in a distinct color and aligned along a shared timestamp axis. The x-axis represents the Timestamp,
while the y-axis denotes the corresponding signal Value. Legends are incorporated to distinguish
between channels (e.g., body_acc_x, body_acc_y, body_acc_z in HAR datasets).

Training Setup

We initialize our backbone with the publicly available Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct checkpoint [60]12 .
Our overall training pipeline comprises two stages: warm-up through supervised fine-tuning (SFT)
and reinforcement learning with GRPO.
Warm-up via Supervised Fine-tuning. We first sample ∼ 1,000 time series–text paired instances
per dataset using GPT-4o (temperature = 1.0) via the OpenAI API, where the model is prompted to
reason over each time series and generate a corresponding answer (details are provided in Appendix I).
These examples are used to warm-start the SFT model, which is adapted from a publicly available
LLM training repository13 . The corresponding training hyperparameters are summarized in Table 5.
Reinforcement Learning with GRPO. After warm-up, we train the model using the GRPO
algorithm, adapted from a public RL training library14 . The complete GRPO configuration is provided
in Table 6. Rewards are computed using Eq. 5, with coefficients (λfmt , λhard , λsoft ) = (0.1, 0.9, 0). In
case studies assessing extrapolation, we set λsoft = 1.0.

H.3

Prompt for Soft Reward

To assess the quality of open-ended model-generated suggestions (<extension> outputs), we adopt a
soft reward mechanism based on the LLM-as-a-Judge paradigm. Specifically, we employ GPT-4o via
the OpenAI API as a frozen expert model to evaluate each extension along four semantic dimensions.
The judge returns a continuous score between 0.0 and 1.0, representing the averaged quality across
the criteria below.
General Soft Reward Prompt (LLM-as-a-Judge)
You are an expert assistant evaluating a model-generated reasoning,
classification, and recommendation output. The goal is to assess
the quality of the recommendation (i.e., extension) based on the
following structured output:
Reasoning: [reasoning]
Prediction: [class_X]
Extension: [extension]
Score the extension based on the following four criteria, each on
a scale from 0.0 to 1.0. Then return only the average score as a
single float (e.g., 0.625).
Evaluation Criteria:
1. Specificity – Is the extension clearly tailored to the predicted
class or context, avoiding generic language?
2. Appropriateness – Is the recommendation suitable given the
prediction and reasoning?
3. Relevance – Does the extension logically follow from the
preceding reasoning and predicted class?
4. Depth – Does the extension demonstrate domain knowledge, including
detailed actions or next-step considerations?
Instructions:
- Penalize vague or generic outputs (e.g., "be careful") unless
well-justified.
- Reward informative, actionable, and context-aware suggestions.
- Return only a single float between 0.0 and 1.0.

retained as high-quality supervision. We collect approximately 1,000 class-balanced samples per

All collected data will be publicly released upon publication to support future research. Below, we
